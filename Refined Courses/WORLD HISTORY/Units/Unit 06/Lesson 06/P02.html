Historical content

Raphael Lemkin, a Polish-Jewish lawyer, created the word genocide and campaigned for international law. The 1948 Genocide Convention defines the crime through protected groups, prohibited acts, and specific intent. Crimes against humanity and ethnic cleansing may overlap with genocide but require careful distinction.

Under Pol Pot, the Khmer Rouge emptied Cambodian cities, imposed forced labor, and caused executions, starvation, and disease. Not every death fits the legal definition of genocide: persecution of protected ethnic and religious groups supports genocide findings, while mass attacks on political and social groups also constituted crimes against humanity and other atrocities.

During Yugoslavia’s breakup, Bosnia experienced ethnic nationalism, forced displacement, camps, and mass killing. In July 1995, more than 8,000 Bosniak men and boys were killed at Srebrenica; international tribunals recognized this as genocide. In Rwanda, historically and colonially shaped Hutu/Tutsi political identities were exploited during civil war and crisis. Extremists organized genocide against the Tutsi over about 100 days, while moderate Hutu and others were also murdered.

Propaganda, dehumanization, authoritarian leadership, war, armed organizations, institutional breakdown, and impunity interact; hatred is not natural or inevitable. International action depends on mandates, resources, political will, sovereignty, legitimacy, and practical risk. Peacekeeping is not automatic war-making authority. Tribunals such as the ICTY and ICTR may punish individuals after atrocities, while the ICC has limited jurisdiction. Responsibility to Protect expresses a responsibility to protect populations but does not guarantee intervention.

Escalation and risk model

Political crisis + extremist ideology + war or institutional breakdown + targeted propaganda + organized armed actors + impunity → increased mass-atrocity risk

These factors increase risk; they do not make genocide inevitable. Leaders, institutions, communities, and international actors still make choices.
