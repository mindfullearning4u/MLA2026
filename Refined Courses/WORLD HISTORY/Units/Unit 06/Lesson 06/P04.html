P04 Historical Thinking

Genocide, Ethnic Cleansing, and Global Responses

Comparison: seven-step process

  1. Context: What political crisis, war, or institutional breakdown existed?
  2. Targeting: Which people were targeted, and how does the evidence establish intent?
  3. Institutions: Who organized, enabled, resisted, rescued, or documented?
  4. Propaganda: How did leaders shape attitudes without assuming ancient or natural hatred?
  5. International response: What did outside actors know, have authority to do, and choose to do?
  6. Outcomes: Compare prevention, intervention, displacement, and accountability.
  7. Avoid false equivalence: Similar mechanisms do not make cases identical, and roles do not carry equal responsibility.

Modeled comparison

Rwanda and Bosnia both involved organized violence against civilians and failures of international protection, but their political contexts differed. In Rwanda, extremist leaders mobilized genocide against the Tutsi during a rapidly escalating national crisis and civil war. In Bosnia, ethnic cleansing occurred during Yugoslavia’s breakup, and the Srebrenica killings were legally recognized as genocide. Comparison reveals recurring mechanisms—extremist nationalism, propaganda, organized armed actors, and weak international response—while preserving important differences.

Example only; no notebook writing is required on this page.

Correct common errors: Not every mass killing is genocide; groups do not “naturally” hate; the UN cannot act without mandates, resources, and member-state decisions; and some people resist, rescue, document, or aid even under extreme danger.
