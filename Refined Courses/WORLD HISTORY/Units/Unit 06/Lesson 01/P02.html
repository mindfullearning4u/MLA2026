Vocabulary

Cold War
Long-term political, ideological, military, and economic rivalry primarily between the United States and Soviet Union after World War II.
superpower
A state with exceptional global military, political, and economic influence.
capitalism
An economic system based largely on private ownership, markets, and investment.
communism
A political-economic ideology associated in the Soviet context with Communist Party rule and state control of major economic resources.
Eastern Bloc
Soviet-aligned communist states of Eastern and Central Europe during the Cold War.
Western Bloc
The United States and its principal allies during the Cold War.
containment
U.S. policy intended to limit further expansion of Soviet or communist influence.
Truman Doctrine
The 1947 U.S. policy providing aid to Greece and Turkey and associated with containment.
Marshall Plan
The U.S.-supported European recovery program providing substantial economic assistance after World War II.
Iron Curtain
A political metaphor for the division between Soviet-controlled Eastern Europe and Western Europe.
Berlin Blockade
The Soviet restriction of Western land access to West Berlin in 1948–1949.
Berlin Airlift
The Western operation that supplied West Berlin by air during the blockade.
NATO
The Western collective-defense alliance established in 1949.
Warsaw Pact
The Soviet-led military alliance established in 1955.
collective defense
An agreement that members will cooperate in response to an attack on one member.
sphere of influence
A region where a state exercises significant political, military, or economic influence.

How rivalry developed

The United States generally promoted multiparty representative government and a private-property market economy. The Soviet Union operated through Communist Party rule and a state-directed economy. Ideology mattered, but it was not the only cause. Soviet leaders remembered devastating invasions and sought friendly governments along their western border. Western leaders feared Soviet political expansion and suppression of opposition in Eastern Europe.

At Yalta and Potsdam, Allied leaders addressed Germany, Poland, occupation, and postwar boundaries, but no single conference “started” the Cold War. Soviet military presence, political pressure, communist takeovers, containment, economic recovery plans, and repeated action-response cycles deepened division.
