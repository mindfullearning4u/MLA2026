P04 Historical Thinking

Cold War Europe, Ideology, and Early Conflict

Cause and Effect: the six-step process

  1. Identify postwar conditions: defeated Germany, weakened Europe, Soviet forces in Eastern Europe, and two emerging superpowers.
  2. Identify competing interests: What did each government seek, and where did those goals conflict?
  3. Identify actions: Soviet-backed governments, the Truman Doctrine, the Marshall Plan, or the Berlin Blockade.
  4. Identify responses: For example, blockade → airlift.
  5. Trace escalation: Explain how an action described as defensive by one side could appear aggressive to the other.
  6. Evaluate causation: Weigh ideology, security, power, geography, and political decisions rather than naming one automatic cause.

Modeled reasoning

Soviet leaders viewed friendly governments in Eastern Europe as important for security after devastating invasions, but Western governments viewed Soviet domination of those governments as expansion. U.S. containment and the Marshall Plan were intended to strengthen states vulnerable to communist influence, while Soviet leaders interpreted growing U.S. involvement as a threat. The early Cold War therefore developed partly through a security dilemma: actions presented as defensive by one side could appear aggressive to the other.

Correct four common misconceptions

  • The former allies did not suddenly become different in 1945; distrust and ideological conflict predated their wartime alliance.
  • The Cold War was not peaceful merely because the superpowers avoided direct large-scale war with each other.
  • Eastern European states did not all become politically identical or change under identical circumstances.
  • The Cold War did not have one simple cause.
