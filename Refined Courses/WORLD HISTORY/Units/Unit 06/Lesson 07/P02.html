Historical content

The Cold War’s end did not end nationalism. Yugoslavia’s political/economic instability and competing nationalist projects contributed to fragmentation, wars, territorial claims, international intervention, and the Dayton Accords. Separatist and autonomy movements—from Kurdish regions to Chechnya, Quebec, Basque areas, and Northern Ireland—used varied political strategies; nationalist movement is not a synonym for terrorism.

Globalization expanded through trade, finance, transportation, migration, media, and digital communication. Multinational corporations and supply chains can create investment, jobs, technology transfer, and consumer access while also producing displacement, labor/environmental concerns, unequal profits, and vulnerability to shocks. Trade blocs reduce barriers at different depths.

Terrorism uses or threatens violence, especially against civilians/noncombatants, to create fear for political goals. It differs from conventional war, guerrilla war, insurgency, state violence, protest, and civil disobedience. Al-Qaeda carried out the September 11, 2001 attacks on the World Trade Center and Pentagon. Islam must be distinguished from extremist organizations claiming Islamic justification, just as all religions must be distinguished from political extremists. Responses—intelligence, law enforcement, finance controls, military action, and border policy—raise debates about security, civil liberty, sovereignty, effectiveness, and unintended effects. Afghanistan and Iraq require multi-cause, neutral historical analysis; active Iraqi WMD stockpiles matching major prewar claims were not found.
