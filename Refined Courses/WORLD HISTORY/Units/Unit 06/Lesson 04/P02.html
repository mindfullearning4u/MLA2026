Historical content summary

After World War II, exhausted European powers faced organized nationalist movements, economic costs, anti-imperial ideas, international pressure, colonial resistance, and changing global power. Independence transferred formal sovereignty, but foreign economic and political influence often continued.

In British India, Congress, the Muslim League, Gandhi, Nehru, Jinnah, mass participation, wartime change, and British decisions all mattered. Independence in 1947 came with partition, mass migration, displacement, and violence. Ghana’s movement used organization and negotiation; Kenya combined land conflict, protest, armed uprising, detention, and negotiation; Algeria fought a sustained war against French settler colonialism.

New states pursued their own interests, including nonalignment, and faced inherited borders, weak institutions, economic dependence, diversity, Cold War pressure, and post-independence choices. South Africa’s 1994 democratic transition was later reform against apartheid—not formal decolonization—and resulted from decades of resistance, international pressure, economic conditions, organization, and negotiation, not Mandela alone.
