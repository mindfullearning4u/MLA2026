Historical content summary

Under late Ottoman and then British rule, Arabic-speaking Palestinian communities and Jewish communities lived amid political change and European imperial interest. Zionist movements sought Jewish self-determination in response partly to European antisemitism; Arab and Palestinian nationalists sought self-government and opposed political displacement. British wartime promises, the Balfour Declaration, mandate rule, land disputes, immigration, and violence intensified competing expectations.

The Holocaust and displaced-person crisis increased support for Jewish statehood. The 1947 UN proposal divided the territory, but war produced different boundaries. Israel’s establishment and victory created statehood and security for many Jews while the conflict displaced large numbers of Palestinians—the Nakba. Jewish refugees also left or were expelled from several Arab and Muslim-majority countries; this distinct history must not erase Palestinian displacement.

In 1967 Israel captured the West Bank, East Jerusalem, Gaza, Sinai, and Golan Heights. Later conflict and diplomacy included the 1973 war, Camp David, the First Intifada, and Oslo. Religion and sacred sites matter, but nationalism, territory, security, refugees, sovereignty, and foreign intervention also matter. Fundamentalist movements can occur in several religions and must not be equated with an entire faith or with terrorism.
