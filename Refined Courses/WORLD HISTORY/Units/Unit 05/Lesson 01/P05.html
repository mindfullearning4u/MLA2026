P05 Guided Investigation

Guided question: How do historians explain why the Sarajevo assassination became a much larger war?

This page guides you through the reasoning. Follow each step in order. The claim is provided; your job is to see how evidence supports it.

Given claim

The 1914 crisis became a major war because long-term tensions made Europe vulnerable and government decisions during the July Crisis connected a regional conflict to a wider alliance system.

Step 1: Identify the trigger

The assassination of Franz Ferdinand on June 28 created the immediate crisis. Label it trigger, not “the only cause.” Ask: Would the assassination by itself explain mobilization by Russia, Germany’s declarations of war, or fighting in Belgium? No. More evidence is needed.

Step 2: Identify underlying conditions

  • Militarism: large armies and detailed war plans made quick mobilization seem necessary.
  • Alliances: leaders expected support from partners and feared opposing blocs.
  • Nationalism: Serbian and South Slavic nationalism challenged Austria-Hungary; major powers defended national prestige.
  • Imperialism: earlier rivalry had damaged trust among European powers.
  • Balkan tensions: Austria-Hungary and Russia competed for influence near Serbia.

Step 3: Select evidence

Evidence A—map: Germany stood between France and Russia; Serbia bordered Austria-Hungary and was connected culturally and strategically to Russian interests.

Evidence B—ultimatum: Austria-Hungary demanded that Serbia suppress anti-Austrian activity, showing how it framed the crisis as a threat to state security.

Evidence C—timeline: War widened through a sequence of decisions between July 28 and August 4, not in one automatic moment.

Step 4: Explain the evidence

The map explains where the pressure existed. The ultimatum explains one government’s position and demands. The timeline demonstrates chronology and reveals decision points. Together they support a multi-causal explanation.

Step 5: State limitations

The alliance map does not show what leaders thought. Austria-Hungary’s ultimatum does not present Serbia’s full perspective. A timeline shows sequence but cannot by itself prove which cause was most important.

Step 6: Connect to industrial warfare

Once escalation produced a major war, mobilization, railroads, artillery, machine guns, and mass production helped states fight on an unprecedented scale. Defensive firepower contributed to trench warfare and stalemate.

Write in your Historian’s Notebook

Write this concise, meaningful summary in your own words:

The assassination at Sarajevo triggered the July Crisis, but underlying militarism, alliances, nationalism, imperial rivalry, and Balkan tensions made escalation more likely. Government leaders then chose ultimatums, mobilization, and declarations of war. Maps establish the geographic pressures, primary sources reveal perspectives, and timelines show the decision sequence. No one piece of evidence proves the entire explanation, so historians corroborate multiple sources.

Also record one sentence explaining how industrial firepower contributed to trench warfare or stalemate. You do not need to copy the six guided steps.
