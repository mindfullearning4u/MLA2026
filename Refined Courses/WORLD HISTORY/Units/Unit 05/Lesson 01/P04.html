P04 Historical Thinking: Cause and Effect

This page is a complete worked example. Read it closely. You are not required to copy it into your Historian’s Notebook. You may take optional notes if they help you.

The process historians use

  1. Name the outcome: A regional Austro-Serbian crisis became a major European and global war.
  2. Separate conditions from the trigger: Long-term pressures existed before June 1914; the assassination triggered the immediate crisis.
  3. Build the chronology: Place each ultimatum, mobilization, and declaration in order.
  4. Identify human decisions: Explain what leaders chose and what alternatives remained.
  5. Show interaction: Explain how two or more causes strengthened one another.
  6. Use evidence and limits: Support each conclusion with a map, timeline, source, or visual and state what it cannot prove.

Worked example

Reasoning elementModel
ClaimGovernment decisions during the July Crisis best explain why the assassination developed into a much larger war.
Evidence 1Austria-Hungary issued a severe ultimatum and declared war after Serbia accepted many, but not all, demands.
Explanation 1The assassination created the crisis, but the ultimatum and declaration converted political tension into armed conflict.
Evidence 2Russia mobilized, Germany declared war on Russia and France, and Germany’s movement through Belgium brought Britain into the war.
Explanation 2Alliance relationships mattered because leaders connected military choices across borders; alliances did not operate by themselves.
Geographic evidenceGermany’s position between France and Russia shaped military planning, while Serbia’s Balkan location connected Austrian and Russian interests.
CounterpointWithout militarism, nationalism, imperial rivalry, and alliance tension, one assassination might have remained a limited dispute.
ReasoningThe strongest explanation is interactive: underlying conditions made Europe vulnerable, the assassination triggered the crisis, and government decisions produced escalation.

How technology changed warfare

Claim: Industrial technology increased killing power faster than armies developed safe methods of attack.

Evidence: Machine guns and artillery could strike exposed soldiers rapidly and at long range. Railroads delivered troops and ammunition, allowing states to sustain enormous armies. Armies dug trenches for protection.

Reasoning: Strong defensive firepower made frontal attacks extremely costly, helping create stalemate on the Western Front. Technology did not act alone; military tactics, geography, production, and command decisions also shaped results.

Vocabulary in action

The model accurately uses militarism, alliance, nationalism, imperialism, mobilization, ultimatum, escalation, trench warfare, artillery, and stalemate. Notice that vocabulary is used to explain relationships—not inserted as a list.
