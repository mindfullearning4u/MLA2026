Lesson Focus

Students investigate why the Great Depression developed into a global economic crisis and how different governments responded.

The lesson must connect economic instability after World War I to:

War debts
Reparations
Financial interdependence
Speculation
Banking weakness
Industrial overproduction

Students must understand that the 1929 U.S. stock market crash was an important event, but it was not the single cause of the global Great Depression.

The lesson also introduces different governmental responses and prepares students for Lesson 4 by examining how severe economic crisis contributed to political instability and declining confidence in democratic governments.

Required P04 Modeled Example

The 1929 stock market crash damaged wealth and confidence, but it became far more destructive because banks were already vulnerable, businesses depended on credit, agricultural incomes were weak, and international economies were connected through debt and trade. Bank failures reduced lending, businesses cut production, unemployment increased, and falling incomes reduced consumer spending. These feedback effects transformed a financial crisis into a prolonged depression.

Required P04 Common Misconceptions
Misconception 1

The stock market crash caused the Great Depression by itself.

Correction: The Depression resulted from several interacting weaknesses and feedback effects.

Misconception 2

The Great Depression affected only the United States.

Correction: International lending, trade, banking, and commodity markets spread the crisis globally.

Misconception 3

Every government responded the same way.

Correction: Governments adopted substantially different economic and political responses.

Misconception 4

Economic depression automatically created dictatorship.

Correction: Economic crisis contributed to instability, but authoritarian takeovers depended on additional political and ideological conditions.

Required P04 Modeled Example

Nazi Germany and Stalin's Soviet Union both used propaganda, secret police, censorship, imprisonment, and leader cults to suppress opposition and mobilize society. Their ideologies, however, were fundamentally different. Nazism centered on extreme nationalism, racial hierarchy, antisemitism, and territorial expansion, while Stalinist rule claimed Marxist-Leninist goals centered on class and state-controlled economic transformation. Similar methods of repression therefore do not make the two ideologies identical.

Required P04 Common Misconceptions
Misconception 1

Fascism, Nazism, and communism were the same ideology.

Correction: The regimes shared some authoritarian methods but had major ideological, economic, racial, and political differences.

Misconception 2

Hitler was elected dictator.

Correction: Nazi electoral success contributed to Hitler's appointment as chancellor, after which democratic institutions were systematically dismantled.

Misconception 3

The March on Rome was a complete military overthrow.

Correction: Fascist intimidation mattered, but political elites and the king played a major role in Mussolini's appointment.

Misconception 4

Authoritarian governments controlled every person's beliefs completely.

Correction: Regimes sought extensive control, but individuals responded in varied ways.
