Historical Inquiry, Timelines, and Cause and Effect

Guided Investigation

What You Will Do

This page shows exactly how a historian derives an answer from evidence. Read each completed step carefully. The claim, source classifications, evidence explanations, chronology, corroboration, reasoning, and conclusion are provided so you can see how the process works from beginning to end.

You do not need to copy every step. At the end of this page, you will record one concise Guided Investigation Summary as meaningful Historian's Notebook evidence.

Context You Need

Medieval Icelandic stories described Norse voyages west of Greenland around the year 1000. Stories alone could not prove that a physical Norse settlement existed in North America. Beginning in 1960, archaeologists investigated building remains at L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland. They compared the structures with Norse buildings in Iceland and Greenland and examined artifacts, ironworking remains, and scientifically dated wood.

The evidence set contains:

  • Evidence A: Archaeologist Birgitta Wallace's later recollection that she recognized building remains like those she had seen in Iceland.
  • Evidence B: A Parks Canada secondary interpretation explaining that excavators identified eight eleventh-century Norse buildings and related artifacts.
  • Evidence C: Archaeological findings, including a Norse-style cloak pin, forge remains, iron boat nails, and metal-cut wood dated to 1021 CE.

Provided Claim

Claim: Norse people established and used a base camp at L'Anse aux Meadows in North America during the early eleventh century.

Do not choose a different claim for this guided investigation. Follow how the provided claim is tested and supported.

Step 1: Identify and Classify the Evidence

Study the completed table. Notice that each item is named, classified, and connected to a specific fact.

How this step helps: Classifying the sources shows that the claim is supported by different kinds of evidence rather than one repeated account.

Step 2: Explain Usefulness and Limitations

Study these completed explanations:

  1. Evidence A: The primary-source recollection is useful because Wallace was present and had experience with Norse ruins. Its limitation is that a memory recorded decades later cannot date or identify the site by itself.
  2. Evidence B: The secondary-source interpretation is useful because it combines findings from several excavations. Its limitation is that the summary does not show every excavation record or dating procedure.
  3. Evidence C: The archaeological evidence is useful because several related findings connect the site to Norse buildings, artifacts, technology, and a date. Its limitation is that the evidence cannot name every person who occupied the site.

How this step helps: Evaluating usefulness and limitation prevents historians from asking one source to prove more than it can establish.

Step 3: Establish Chronology

Write these events in chronological order:

  1. Around 1000 CE: Norse voyages west are later described in medieval Icelandic stories.
  2. 1021 CE: People using metal tools cut wood at L'Anse aux Meadows.
  3. 1960: Investigators locate the building remains.
  4. 1961–1968: Excavations identify structures, artifacts, and ironworking evidence.

Then write: The 1021 activity occurred centuries before the modern excavation, so investigators did not create the archaeological evidence they later studied.

Step 4: Corroborate the Sources

Write three corroboration statements:

  1. Evidence A and Evidence B agree that the building forms resemble Norse structures found in Iceland or Greenland.
  2. Evidence B and Evidence C agree because the interpretation is supported by structures, artifacts, ironworking remains, and dated wood.
  3. The sources do not establish the name of every occupant or prove that every saga location was L'Anse aux Meadows.

This is corroboration: comparing evidence to identify agreement, differences, limitations, and stronger support.

Step 5: See How the Answer Is Derived

Read the completed guided conclusion:

The historical claim that Norse people used a base camp at L'Anse aux Meadows in the early eleventh century is strongly supported by corroborating evidence. In Evidence A, archaeologist Birgitta Wallace provided a primary-source recollection explaining that she recognized building forms like Norse ruins in Iceland. Evidence B, a secondary-source interpretation, connects those buildings with the results of later excavations. Evidence C adds archaeological evidence, including a Norse-style cloak pin, forge remains, iron boat nails, and metal-cut wood dated to 1021 CE. Chronologically, the dated activity occurred centuries before investigators located the site in 1960, so the archaeological record was not created by the modern excavation. The buildings, artifacts, technology, and date corroborate one another because different source types point to the same conclusion. A limitation is that the evidence cannot identify every person who lived at the site or prove that every place in the sagas refers to L'Anse aux Meadows. Taken together, the evidence supports the claim more strongly than any single source could.

How the answer was derived: The conclusion begins with the claim, uses specific evidence from three source types, establishes chronology, corroborates the evidence, acknowledges a limitation, and then explains why the combined evidence is strongest.

Required Guided Investigation Summary

Write only this meaningful summary in your Historian's Notebook:

Historians test a claim by identifying and classifying evidence, determining what each source establishes, and recognizing each source's usefulness and limitations. In this investigation, a primary-source recollection, a secondary-source interpretation, and archaeological evidence supported the claim that Norse people used a base camp at L'Anse aux Meadows in the early eleventh century. The 1021 date established chronology, while the buildings, artifacts, ironworking remains, and source accounts corroborated one another. Historians still acknowledged that the evidence could not name every occupant or prove that every saga location was this site. The conclusion was strongest because multiple source types supported it.
