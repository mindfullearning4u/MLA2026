Historical Inquiry, Timelines, and Cause and Effect

Guided Investigation

What You Will Do

On this page, you will apply the historical-inquiry process with step-by-step guidance. A claim is provided for you. You will identify the evidence, classify its source type, explain its usefulness and limitations, establish chronology, corroborate the sources, and connect the evidence to the claim.

This completed guided investigation is required Historian's Notebook evidence and must be submitted for grading.

Context You Need

Medieval Icelandic stories described Norse voyages west of Greenland around the year 1000. Stories alone could not prove that a physical Norse settlement existed in North America. Beginning in 1960, archaeologists investigated building remains at L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland. They compared the structures with Norse buildings in Iceland and Greenland and examined artifacts, ironworking remains, and scientifically dated wood.

The evidence set contains:

  • Evidence A: Archaeologist Birgitta Wallace's later recollection that she recognized building remains like those she had seen in Iceland.
  • Evidence B: A Parks Canada secondary interpretation explaining that excavators identified eight eleventh-century Norse buildings and related artifacts.
  • Evidence C: Archaeological findings, including a Norse-style cloak pin, forge remains, iron boat nails, and metal-cut wood dated to 1021 CE.

Write This Claim in Your Notebook

Claim: Norse people established and used a base camp at L'Anse aux Meadows in North America during the early eleventh century.

Do not choose a different claim for this guided investigation. Your job is to learn how to test and support the provided claim.

Step 1: Identify and Classify the Evidence

Copy the table headings below into your notebook. Then complete one row for each piece of evidence.

Step 2: Explain Usefulness and Limitations

For each source, write both what it helps establish and what it cannot establish alone.

  1. Evidence A: Write that the recollection is useful because Wallace was present and had experience with Norse ruins. Then write that memory recorded decades later cannot date or identify the site by itself.
  2. Evidence B: Write that the secondary source is useful because it combines findings from several excavations. Then write that the summary does not show every excavation record or dating procedure.
  3. Evidence C: Write that the physical evidence is useful because several related findings connect the site to Norse buildings, artifacts, technology, and a date. Then write that the evidence cannot name every person who occupied the site.

Use vocabulary such as primary source, secondary source, evidence, interpretation, usefulness, and limitation.

Step 3: Establish Chronology

Write these events in chronological order:

  1. Around 1000 CE: Norse voyages west are later described in medieval Icelandic stories.
  2. 1021 CE: People using metal tools cut wood at L'Anse aux Meadows.
  3. 1960: Investigators locate the building remains.
  4. 1961–1968: Excavations identify structures, artifacts, and ironworking evidence.

Then write: The 1021 activity occurred centuries before the modern excavation, so investigators did not create the archaeological evidence they later studied.

Step 4: Corroborate the Sources

Write three corroboration statements:

  1. Evidence A and Evidence B agree that the building forms resemble Norse structures found in Iceland or Greenland.
  2. Evidence B and Evidence C agree because the interpretation is supported by structures, artifacts, ironworking remains, and dated wood.
  3. The sources do not establish the name of every occupant or prove that every saga location was L'Anse aux Meadows.

This is corroboration: comparing evidence to identify agreement, differences, limitations, and stronger support.

Step 5: Connect Evidence to the Claim

Write a 6–8 sentence guided conclusion. Follow this exact order:

  1. Begin by restating the provided claim.
  2. Identify Evidence A and explain what it contributes.
  3. Identify Evidence B and explain what it contributes.
  4. Identify at least two specific archaeological findings from Evidence C.
  5. Explain the 1021 chronological relationship.
  6. Explain how at least two source types corroborate one another.
  7. State one limitation or remaining uncertainty.
  8. End by explaining why the combined evidence supports the claim more strongly than any single source could.

Use some of the vocabulary you learned, such as historical inquiry, evidence, primary source, secondary source, chronology, corroboration, historical claim, and limitation.
