Historical Inquiry, Timelines, and Cause and Effect

Learn the Historical Content

Investigation Question

How do historians use evidence to determine what most likely happened in the past?

1. Historical Inquiry Is a Process

Historians cannot directly observe the past. They investigate traces that people and events left behind. A strong investigation moves through five connected steps:

  1. Question: Ask a focused question that can be investigated.
  2. Context: Establish the time, place, conditions, beliefs, and circumstances.
  3. Evidence: Examine several relevant sources and identify what each can establish.
  4. Comparison: Corroborate the sources by locating agreement, disagreement, and gaps.
  5. Conclusion: Make a historical claim that fits the strongest available evidence.
Important: Inquiry is not always linear. New evidence may require a historian to revise the question, add context, or change a conclusion.

3. Primary and Secondary Sources

Source TypeDefinitionExamplesQuestion to Ask
Primary sourceEvidence created during the period being studied by a person or group connected to itLetter, law, diary, artifact, photograph, census entryWhat could this creator know, and why was it created?
Secondary sourceAn explanation or interpretation created later using historical evidenceHistory book, scholarly article, museum essay, documentary analysisWhich evidence and interpretation does the author use?

A primary source is not automatically more accurate, and a secondary source is not automatically less useful. Each answers different questions. A source's limitation is what it cannot establish by itself.

4. Chronology, Context, Cause, and Effect

Chronology is the order in which events occurred. It helps historians distinguish among different parts of a causal explanation:

Background condition
A longer-term circumstance that shapes what is possible.
Immediate cause
An action or event that directly contributes to what happens next.
Event
The occurrence being explained.
Effect
A result or consequence of the event.

Context includes the time, place, conditions, beliefs, and circumstances surrounding an event or source. Sequence alone does not prove causation. Historians must explain a mechanism: how and why one condition or action contributed to an outcome.

5. Corroboration and Responsible Conclusions

Corroboration means comparing multiple sources to identify agreement, disagreement, and stronger support. Historians ask:

  • Which facts appear in more than one independent source?
  • Where do sources disagree, and can their perspectives or purposes explain why?
  • Which source is useful for this question?
  • What important information is missing?

A responsible conclusion states what the evidence most strongly supports while acknowledging a limitation or remaining uncertainty. If new credible evidence conflicts with the conclusion, historians reconsider the claim.

Claim-Evidence-Reasoning: State a claim, cite specific evidence, and explain why that evidence supports the claim. Evidence does not speak for itself; reasoning makes the connection visible.
