Evidence Case

What conclusion about Norse activity in North America is best supported by the evidence from L’Anse aux Meadows?

Before You Examine the Evidence

Context: Medieval Icelandic stories described Norse voyages west of Greenland around the year 1000. For centuries, those stories could not establish a North American settlement by themselves. Beginning in 1960, investigators excavated building remains and artifacts at L’Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland.

Keep two initial explanations open:

  1. Claim A: Norse people established and used a base camp in North America around the early eleventh century.
  2. Claim B: The evidence shows only that later people told stories about Norse voyages; it does not establish a physical Norse presence in North America.

Evidence A: Primary-Source Recollection

Source: Archaeologist Birgitta Wallace, recalling her work at the excavation in 1964.

“I knew it was Norse because I had seen identical type ruins in Iceland.”

Sourcing note: Wallace participated in the excavation, so her account is useful evidence about what an investigator observed and recognized. It was recorded decades later and reflects memory; it cannot establish the site's date or identity by itself.

Repository: Parks Canada, L’Anse aux Meadows: The Saga of Vinland, ReCollections podcast transcript, page modified 2025. Official source page.

Evidence B: Secondary Interpretation

Source summary: Parks Canada explains that excavators identified the lower walls of eight eleventh-century Norse buildings. The buildings resembled structures used in Iceland and Greenland around 1000. Investigators also found Norse-style objects and evidence of ironworking.

Usefulness: This interpretation combines results from multiple excavations and types of evidence. Limitation: It summarizes the investigators' conclusions rather than presenting every excavation record and dating procedure.

Repository: Parks Canada, Culture and history—L’Anse aux Meadows National Historic Site, modified April 18, 2024. Official source page.

Historical Inquiry Process Summary

  1. Begin with a focused historical question.
  2. Examine the context: the time, place, conditions, and circumstances surrounding the event.
  3. Identify Evidence A as a primary-source recollection and record what the participant observed.
  4. Identify Evidence B as a secondary-source interpretation and record how later historians explain the evidence.
  5. Identify Evidence C as non-narrative archaeological evidence and record what the physical findings establish.
  6. Determine the usefulness and limitation of each source. A source may be useful even when it cannot answer every question.
  7. Place dated evidence in chronological order.
  8. Identify relevant causes, events, and effects without assuming that sequence alone proves causation.
  9. Corroborate the sources by recording where they agree, differ, or leave unanswered questions.
  10. Use the combined evidence to determine which historical conclusion is most strongly supported.

Completion check: Your notebook should contain the heading and all ten numbered steps.
