Historical Thinking

Worked Model: From Evidence to Conclusion

This model shows the thinking process. It does not replace your independent analysis on P06.

Step 1: Source Before You Accept

Evidence A: Wallace was an archaeologist at the site, so her recollection is useful for what she recognized during excavation. Because it was recalled later and expresses an initial judgment, it requires corroboration.

Evidence B: Parks Canada is responsible for interpreting the historic site. Its secondary account synthesizes excavation findings, but students should still inspect the specific evidence behind the interpretation.

Evidence C: Objects and dated wood are non-narrative evidence. They do not offer opinions, but their meaning depends on archaeological context and correct dating.

Step 2: Corroborate

ComparisonModel Reasoning
A + BBoth identify the building remains as comparable to Norse structures in Iceland or Greenland.
B + CThe interpretation is supported by several artifact types, ironworking evidence, buildings, and a date.
Remaining gapThe evidence does not prove the name of every occupant or that this exact location was every place described in the Vinland stories.

Step 3: Use Chronology and Causal Reasoning Carefully

The 1021 date occurs before the modern discovery and excavation, so the archaeological activity cannot have been created by the investigators. However, sequence alone does not identify the people. The connection becomes stronger when the date is combined with metal-cut wood, Norse-style buildings, a Norse cloak pin, and ironworking evidence.

Avoid this error: “It happened first, so it caused the next event.” Chronology establishes order. Cause and effect also require an explanation of contribution or mechanism.

Step 4: Build Claim-Evidence-Reasoning

Model claim: The evidence strongly supports the conclusion that Norse people used a base camp at L’Anse aux Meadows in the early eleventh century.

Model evidence: Excavators found Norse-style buildings, a ring-headed cloak pin, ironworking remains, and metal-cut wood dated to 1021.

Model reasoning: No single clue must carry the conclusion. Independent source types agree: an eyewitness archaeologist recognized Norse building forms, the later interpretation reports the excavation pattern, and physical evidence connects Norse material culture and technology to a precise period.

Model qualification: The evidence supports Norse presence, but it does not establish the identity of every traveler or match every saga location to a modern site.
