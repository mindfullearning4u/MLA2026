Lesson Identification
Field	Specification
Unit	3

Lesson Title	Reformation and Catholic Response
Lesson Type	Instructional Lesson
MLA Standards	MLA.WH.REF.01, MLA.WH.REF.02
Investigation Question	Why did religious reform divide Western Christianity and transform European society?
Primary Historical-Thinking Skill	Cause and Effect
Supporting Historical-Thinking Skills	Perspective, Contextualization, Claim-Evidence-Reasoning
Lesson Focus

Students investigate the religious, political, technological, intellectual, and economic conditions that contributed to the Protestant Reformation.

They examine how reform movements challenged established religious authority, how different reformers developed distinct theological and political positions, and how Catholic leaders responded through reform and institutional action.

Students must understand that the Reformation was not caused by one person or one event. It developed from multiple long-term and immediate causes.

The lesson must also distinguish among:

Protestant reform movements
Catholic reform
Political interests
Religious beliefs
Institutional responses
Social consequences

The lesson must use neutral academic language and must not evaluate the truth of competing religious doctrines.

Required Historical Content

Codex must teach all of the following.

1. Historical Context Before the Reformation

Teach:

Authority of the Roman Catholic Church in Western Europe
Importance of religion in political and social life
Church institutions
Clergy
Monasteries and religious orders
Religious education
Church wealth and landholding
Calls for reform before Martin Luther
Criticism of corruption or abuses
Humanist interest in original texts
Growing literacy
Printing and circulation of ideas
Political interests of rulers

Students must understand:

Criticism of Church practices existed before Luther, and reform movements developed within a broader religious and political context.

2. Indulgences and Reform Criticism

Teach:

Meaning of indulgence in Catholic practice
Historical abuses or controversies connected with the sale or promotion of indulgences
Public criticism of these practices
Connection to Luther's objections
Accuracy Requirement

Do not teach:

Catholics believed people could simply buy forgiveness for any sin.

Instead explain the historical controversy accurately and distinguish official theology from criticized practices.

3. Martin Luther

Teach:

German monk and theologian
Concerns about Church practices and doctrine
1517 and the Ninety-Five Theses
Debate over salvation
Scripture
Religious authority
Criticism of indulgence practices
Conflict with Church authorities
Excommunication
Diet of Worms where appropriate
Spread of his ideas through printing

Students must distinguish:

Luther's theological arguments
Political consequences
Public circulation of his ideas
4. Printing and the Reformation

Teach the connection to Lesson 3.

Students must understand that printing:

Increased speed of reproduction
Expanded circulation of arguments
Supported vernacular texts
Made pamphlet controversies possible
Increased the potential audience for religious debate
Accuracy Requirement

Do not teach:

Printing caused the Reformation.

Instead teach:

Printing accelerated and expanded the circulation of reform ideas that arose from deeper religious, political, and social conditions.

5. Protestantism

Teach Protestantism as a broad category rather than one uniform movement.

Students must understand that Protestant reformers disagreed among themselves.

Teach distinctions among representative movements.

6. John Calvin

Teach:

Reformed Protestant tradition
Geneva
Predestination in appropriate historical context
Religious discipline
Influence beyond Switzerland

Do not require advanced theological detail.

The goal is to show that the Reformation produced multiple Protestant traditions.

7. English Reformation

Teach:

Henry VIII
Conflict with papal authority
Act of Supremacy
Church of England
Political and dynastic factors
Religious consequences

Students must understand that the English Reformation developed differently from Luther's reform movement.

It must not be reduced solely to Henry VIII wanting a divorce.

Teach the dynastic issue within larger political and religious circumstances.

8. Political Interests and the Reformation

Teach:

Holy Roman Empire
German princes
Political decentralization
Opportunity for rulers to support reform
Control of land and Church property
Political rivalry
Charles V

Students must evaluate the relationship between:

Religious conviction
Political authority
Economic interests

Do not assume every ruler adopted reform for the same reason.

9. Peace of Augsburg

Teach:

1555
Relationship between political authority and Lutheran/Catholic religious affiliation in the Holy Roman Empire
Temporary political settlement
Limitations of the agreement

Do not present it as establishing modern religious freedom.

10. Catholic Reformation / Catholic Response

Teach:

Internal Catholic reform efforts
Council of Trent
Clarification of Catholic doctrine
Reform of clerical discipline
Education of clergy
Response to Protestant criticism
Renewal of religious orders

The term "Counter-Reformation" may be introduced, but students should understand that Catholic reform involved both internal reform and response to Protestantism.

11. Council of Trent

Teach:

1545–1563
Affirmation of Catholic doctrine
Reform of abuses
Clerical education and discipline
Institutional strengthening

Students must distinguish between:

Doctrinal continuity
Institutional reform
12. Jesuits

Teach:

Society of Jesus
Ignatius of Loyola
Education
Missionary work
Catholic renewal
Support for Catholic institutions

Students do not need extensive biographical detail.

13. Religious Conflict and Social Consequences

Teach at an appropriate level:

Religious divisions
Political instability
Wars of religion
Confessional identities
Education
Literacy
Religious migration
Changes in political authority

Do not turn the lesson into a detailed study of every European religious war.

14. Perspective

Students must examine at least two contrasting historical perspectives.

Possible perspectives include:

Protestant reformer
Catholic Church official
Political ruler
Urban resident
Religious dissenter

Students must understand:

Perspective influences what a historical actor considered a problem, a reform, or a threat.

Required Vocabulary

Students must copy all provided terms and definitions into the Historian's Notebook.

Term	Required Student-Friendly Definition
Reformation	A major religious movement that challenged and reshaped Western Christianity in the 1500s
Protestant	A broad term for Christians and churches that emerged from movements rejecting or reforming aspects of Roman Catholic authority
Indulgence	In Catholic teaching, a remission of temporal punishment associated with sin after forgiveness; its promotion became controversial during the Reformation
Martin Luther	German monk and theologian whose criticisms of Church practices and teachings helped launch the Protestant Reformation
Ninety-Five Theses	Luther's 1517 statements criticizing practices connected especially to indulgences and calling for theological debate
Excommunication	Formal exclusion from participation in the sacraments or full communion of a church
Calvinism	A Protestant tradition associated with John Calvin and Reformed Christianity
Predestination	In Calvinist theology, the belief that God has determined the ultimate salvation of the elect
Anglican	Related to the Church of England
Act of Supremacy	English law recognizing the monarch as head of the Church of England
Catholic Reformation	Catholic reform efforts that renewed institutions and responded to Protestant challenges
Council of Trent	A major Catholic council held from 1545–1563 that clarified doctrine and addressed reform
Jesuit	A member of the Society of Jesus, a Catholic religious order focused on education, missions, and service
Perspective	The viewpoint shaped by a person's position, experiences, beliefs, and circumstances
Religious authority	The power to define, interpret, or govern religious belief and practice

Required Source-Limitation Instruction

Students must understand:

Luther does not represent every Protestant reformer.
A Catholic council document represents institutional Catholic authority, not every Catholic person's experience.
A political ruler may have religious and political motives at the same time.
Propaganda or polemical images may exaggerate opponents.
Later outcomes should not be assumed to have been inevitable.
P04 Historical Thinking Specification
Primary Skill — Cause and Effect

P04 must teach students to distinguish:

Long-term conditions
Immediate causes
Accelerating factors
Political causes
Religious causes
Economic interests
Consequences

Students must understand that a historical event can have several interacting causes.

Required Cause-and-Effect Process
Step 1 — Define the Outcome

Outcome:

Religious division and reform in Western Christianity.

Step 2 — Identify Long-Term Conditions

Examples:

Calls for Church reform
Humanist scholarship
Political decentralization
Criticism of abuses
Growth of literacy
Step 3 — Identify Immediate Causes

Examples:

Indulgence controversy
Luther's Ninety-Five Theses
Conflict with Church authorities
Step 4 — Identify Accelerating Factors

Examples:

Printing
Political protection
Vernacular communication
Step 5 — Analyze Interaction

Ask:

How did political conditions affect religious reform?
How did printing amplify theological debate?
Why could similar criticism produce different results in different regions?
Step 6 — Distinguish Cause from Consequence

Examples of consequences:

New Protestant churches
Catholic institutional reform
Religious conflict
Political realignment
Required P04 Modeled Example

Codex must model reasoning such as:

Luther's criticism of indulgence practices was an immediate cause of the early Reformation controversy, but it does not explain why the movement spread so widely. Printing allowed reform arguments to circulate rapidly, while some German princes protected reformers or adopted reform for religious and political reasons. The Reformation therefore resulted from interaction among theological dispute, communication technology, and political conditions.

This models multiple causation without providing the checkpoint answer.

Required P04 Common Misconceptions
Misconception 1

Martin Luther caused the Reformation by himself.

Correction

Luther was a major figure, but reform developed from earlier criticism, political conditions, printing, theological disagreement, and broader social change.

Misconception 2

Protestantism was one single church with one set of beliefs.

Correction

Several Protestant traditions developed, including Lutheran, Reformed/Calvinist, Anglican, and others.

Misconception 3

The Catholic Church did nothing but oppose reform.

Correction

Catholic leaders defended doctrine while also addressing abuses, strengthening education, and reforming institutions.

Misconception 4

Printing caused the Reformation.

Correction

Printing accelerated communication and expanded audiences but did not create the underlying religious and political disputes.

Independent Investigation — P06
Investigation Task

Students answer:

Which factor was most important in allowing the Reformation to transform from a religious controversy into a major European movement?

Students evaluate:

Religious criticism and reform ideas
Printing
Political support
Economic/institutional interests
Required Product
Part A — Evidence for Each Factor

For each factor students identify:

One specific historical example
One piece of evidence
How the factor contributed
Part B — Ranking

Rank the four factors from most to least influential.

Part C — Interaction

Students identify two factors that depended on one another and explain the relationship.

Example structure:

Printing was more influential because ______, but it became especially powerful when combined with ______.

Part D — Alternative Perspective

Students identify one reason another historian might rank a different cause first.

Part E — Conclusion

Students write a short evidence-based conclusion defending their ranking.
