Lesson Title	Renaissance Contributions in Art, Literature, and Technology
Lesson Type	Instructional Lesson
MLA Standards	MLA.WH.REN.03
Investigation Question	Which Renaissance developments most significantly changed European culture and communication?
Primary Historical-Thinking Skill	Historical Significance
Supporting Historical-Thinking Skills	Comparison, Continuity and Change, Claim-Evidence-Reasoning
Lesson Focus

Students examine major Renaissance developments in art, literature, architecture, printing, and communication and determine which developments had the greatest historical significance.

The lesson moves beyond identifying famous people or inventions. Students must evaluate developments using explicit significance criteria:

Scale
Reach
Duration
Degree of change
Influence on later developments

Students should also understand that Renaissance innovation frequently involved adapting or building upon earlier knowledge and technologies rather than creating developments with no historical precedent.

Required Historical Content

New techniques

6. Realism and Human Anatomy

Teach:

Increased observation of human form
Study of proportion
Anatomical investigation
Representation of movement and emotion

Avoid implying that realistic representation began only in the Renaissance.

Frame it as a shift in emphasis and technique.

Classical subjects
Greek and Roman forms

Students must distinguish:

from

8. Renaissance Literature

Teach the growing importance of literature written in vernacular languages.

Possible authors or works may include:

Dante as a transition figure
Petrarch where relevant
Boccaccio
Machiavelli
Shakespeare only if used as later Renaissance context and without displacing the lesson's continental European focus

Emphasize:

Vernacular writing
Wider potential readership
Human behavior
Politics
Individual experience
Social commentary
9. Machiavelli

Use Machiavelli as an example of Renaissance political writing where appropriate.

Teach:

Political analysis
Power
Leadership
Statecraft
Historical context of politically fragmented Italy

Do not reduce The Prince to the simplistic idea that "the ends justify the means."

Students should analyze an actual contextualized excerpt rather than rely on that phrase.

10. Printing and Gutenberg

Teach:

Johannes Gutenberg
European use of movable metal type
Printing press
Mid-15th-century European context
Increased speed of book production
Lower production costs relative to manuscript copying
Increased circulation of texts
Growth of reading markets
Spread of ideas
Critical Accuracy Requirement

Do not teach:

Gutenberg invented printing or movable type.

Instead teach:

Printing and movable type existed earlier in East Asia. Gutenberg's innovations contributed to the development and expansion of movable-type printing in Europe.

This distinction is mandatory.

11. Manuscripts and Printed Books

Students must compare:

Manuscript production
Hand copying
Time-intensive production
Limited number of copies
Scribal and institutional networks
Printing
Reproducibility
Increased production
Broader circulation
Greater consistency across copies
Expansion of book markets

Students should not conclude that manuscripts disappeared immediately after printing developed.

12. Literacy and Communication

Teach carefully.

Printing contributed to:

Expanded availability of texts
Broader circulation of information
Educational opportunities
Religious and political communication
Intellectual exchange

But Codex must not claim printing instantly made most Europeans literate.

Printing and literacy influenced one another over time.

13. Long-Term Influence

Students should connect Renaissance cultural and technological developments to later changes such as:

Reformation
Scientific Revolution
Growth of print culture
Wider intellectual exchange
Political communication

These connections should be taught as historical relationships rather than deterministic outcomes.

Required Vocabulary

Students must copy the terms and provided definitions into the Historian's Notebook.

Term	Required Student-Friendly Definition
Linear perspective	An artistic method that creates the appearance of depth on a flat surface using lines that appear to meet at a vanishing point
Realism	Artistic representation intended to appear lifelike or closely observed
Vernacular	The everyday language spoken by people in a particular region rather than a scholarly language such as Latin
Printing press	A mechanical system used to reproduce printed text and images more efficiently
Movable type	Individual reusable characters that can be arranged to print words and pages
Innovation	A new or significantly improved idea, method, process, or technology
Classical influence	Influence derived from ancient Greek or Roman ideas, styles, literature, or architecture
Literacy	The ability to read and write
Dissemination	The spreading of information, ideas, or knowledge
Patronage	Financial or political support for artistic, literary, or intellectual work
Historical significance	The importance of a person, event, development, or idea based on criteria such as reach, duration, scale, and consequences
Cultural change	Change in a society's ideas, practices, artistic expression, or patterns of communication
Manuscript	A text written or copied by hand
Vanishing point	The point in a perspective drawing where parallel lines appear to meet

Required Source Limitation Instruction

Students should understand examples such as:

One artwork cannot represent all Renaissance art.
A political writer represents one perspective.
Surviving printed books disproportionately reflect literate groups.
Famous elite works tell less about people who left few written records.
Later historical influence cannot be established from one contemporary source alone.
P04 Historical Thinking Specification
Primary Skill — Historical Significance

Codex must explicitly teach that historical significance is judged using criteria.

"Famous" and "important" are not sufficient explanations.

Students must evaluate developments.

Required Historical Significance Process
Step 1 — Identify the Development

Examples:

Linear perspective
Vernacular literature
Printing
Classical architectural revival
Step 2 — Identify Immediate Effects

Ask:

What changed at the time?
Who was affected?
Step 3 — Evaluate Scale and Reach

Ask:

Did the change affect a small group or a broad population?
Did it remain local or spread?
Step 4 — Evaluate Duration

Ask:

Were the effects temporary?
Did the development influence later generations?
Step 5 — Evaluate Degree of Change

Ask:

Did this slightly modify an existing practice?
Did it substantially change how people created, communicated, or exchanged ideas?
Step 6 — Connect to Later Developments

Ask:

Did this development influence later cultural, religious, political, scientific, or intellectual change?
Step 7 — Compare

Historical significance is strongest when a student can explain:

Development A was more significant than Development B because...

using criteria and evidence.

Required P04 Modeled Example

Codex must model a comparison rather than giving a vague statement.

Example:

Linear perspective significantly influenced European visual art because it changed how artists represented three-dimensional space and remained influential for centuries. Printing, however, had a broader reach because it changed how texts could be reproduced and distributed across regions. Using reach and later influence as criteria, a historian might argue that printing had greater historical significance even though both developments transformed culture.

This is a reasoning model, not the checkpoint answer.

Required P04 Common Misconceptions
Misconception 1

If a Renaissance person is famous today, that automatically means their contribution was historically significant.

Correction

Historical significance must be supported using criteria and evidence.

Misconception 2

Gutenberg invented movable type and printing.

Correction

Printing and movable type existed earlier in East Asia. Gutenberg's innovations were significant in the European context.

Misconception 3

Printing immediately caused everyone in Europe to become literate.

Correction

Printing expanded access and circulation, but literacy grew unevenly over time.

Independent Investigation — P06
Investigation Task

Students answer:

Which Renaissance development had the greatest long-term historical significance?

Students must evaluate at least three developments.

Required categories include:

Artistic innovation
Literature or vernacular writing
Printing and communication

Students may identify a more specific development within one category.

Required Product
Part A — Evidence Collection

For each development, students identify:

Specific example
Contemporary effect
Evidence
Later influence
Part B — Significance Ratings

Students rate each development from 1–5 for:

Scale
Reach
Duration
Degree of change
Later influence

Students must justify ratings with evidence.

Part C — Ranking

Rank the three developments from:

Most historically significant
Second
Third
Part D — Final Argument

Students write a short notebook conclusion explaining:

Which development ranks first
Which significance criteria matter most
Which historical evidence best supports the ranking
Why another development ranks lower

Mastery Checkpoint — P07
Investigation Question

Which Renaissance development most significantly changed European culture and communication?

Required Task

Students construct a historical argument comparing major Renaissance developments and defend which one had the greatest historical significance.

Students may select:

Artistic development
Literary development
Printing/communication development

but they must compare their selected development against at least two alternatives.

Response Format
225–275 words
Two or three developed paragraphs

Recommended structure:

Paragraph 1

Claim + historical context + significance criteria

Paragraph 2

Evidence supporting the selected development

Paragraph 3

Comparison with alternatives + conclusion

Alternative structures are acceptable if all requirements are met.
