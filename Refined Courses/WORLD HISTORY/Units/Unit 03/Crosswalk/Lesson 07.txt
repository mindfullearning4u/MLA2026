Lesson Title	Colonial Systems, Slavery, and Forced Labor
Lesson Type	Instructional Lesson
MLA Standards	MLA.WH.COL.01, MLA.WH.COL.02
Investigation Question	How did colonial systems use labor, race, and economic power to reshape societies across the Atlantic world?

Lesson Focus

Students investigate how European colonial powers organized labor and production in the Americas and Atlantic world.

The lesson examines:

Colonization
Encomienda
Repartimiento
Plantation systems
Forced Indigenous labor
Atlantic slavery
African forced migration
Racial hierarchy
Mercantilism
Cash crops
Colonial extraction
Resistance
Demographic and social consequences

Students must understand that colonial labor systems were not interchangeable. They developed in different places, under different legal structures, and affected Indigenous peoples and Africans in different ways.

The lesson must also distinguish between:

Slavery
Forced labor
Tribute labor
Wage labor
Contract labor

where historically appropriate.

Required Historical Content

Codex must teach all of the following.

Students must connect Lesson 6 to Lesson 7:

2. Indigenous Societies Before Colonial Labor Systems

Teach:

Agriculture

Do not begin the lesson with Europeans as if the Americas were historically empty.

3. Spanish Colonial Labor Systems

Teach the historical development and function of systems such as:

Encomienda

Explain:

Crown-granted rights to demand tribute and labor from Indigenous communities
Claimed obligations of protection and Christian instruction
Frequent exploitation and abuse
Not legally identical to chattel slavery
Repartimiento

Explain:

Rotating or allocated Indigenous labor drafts
Colonial state regulation
Continued coercion and abuse

Where appropriate, distinguish these from earlier Indigenous labor systems that colonizers adapted or exploited.

4. Portuguese Colonial Economy

Teach:

Portuguese Brazil
Sugar production
Plantation agriculture
Early use of Indigenous labor
Increasing dependence on enslaved African labor
Atlantic commercial connections

Brazil should be used to demonstrate the relationship among:

Land
cash crops
labor demand
slavery
Atlantic trade
5. Plantation System

Teach:

Large-scale agricultural production
Cash crops
Land concentration
Labor-intensive production
Export markets
Colonial ownership
Coercive labor systems

Representative crops may include:

Sugar
Tobacco
Coffee where chronologically appropriate
Cotton only where appropriate to broader later context

For Unit 3, sugar should be a major example because of its importance in the early Atlantic system.

6. Atlantic Slave Trade

Teach:

Forced capture and sale of Africans
African participation and political complexity without implying equal responsibility for the Atlantic system
European demand for labor
Atlantic shipping networks
Forced migration
Middle Passage
Sale in American markets
Plantation labor
Chattel slavery
Hereditary status in many colonial systems
Legal and racial systems developed around slavery

Students must understand:

Atlantic slavery became a large-scale racialized system of hereditary chattel slavery.

7. African Societies and the Slave Trade

Teach:

African societies were politically, economically, and culturally diverse
Some African rulers and merchants participated in slave trading
Others resisted
European coastal trade affected regional political relationships
Demand for enslaved labor altered some African societies
Population losses and insecurity affected communities unevenly
Critical Accuracy Requirement

Do not present Africa as a passive continent from which Europeans simply collected people.

Also do not present African participation in slave trading in a way that minimizes European colonial demand, shipping, plantation systems, or racialized slavery.

8. Middle Passage

Teach:

Forced Atlantic transport
Confinement
Disease
Violence
Mortality
Family separation
Resistance aboard ships

Use age-appropriate but historically accurate language.

Do not sanitize the system, but avoid gratuitous graphic detail.

9. Chattel Slavery

Teach:

People treated legally as property
Forced labor
Sale and transfer
Limited or denied legal rights
Hereditary status in many Atlantic colonial systems
Racialization of slavery

Students must distinguish Atlantic chattel slavery from other historical forms of coerced labor.

10. Development of Racial Hierarchies

Teach:

Colonial legal categories
Race as a historical social construction
Use of racial classifications to organize power and labor
Legal distinctions among Europeans, Africans, Indigenous peoples, and mixed-ancestry populations
Casta categories where appropriate in Spanish America
Accuracy Requirement

Do not imply modern racial categories existed unchanged throughout all earlier history.

Explain that colonial racial classifications developed historically and varied by region.

11. Mercantilism and Colonial Extraction

Teach:

Colonies as sources of raw materials
Markets for imperial goods
Regulation of trade
State competition
Bullion where appropriate
Export agriculture
Extraction of wealth

Students must understand the relationship:

Colonial control → Labor extraction → Commodity production → Atlantic trade → Imperial wealth

12. Cash Crops and Labor Demand

Teach the causal relationship among:

European demand
Plantation production
Sugar and other commodities
Labor shortages
Coercive labor
Expansion of slavery

Students must not treat slavery as an accidental side effect of plantation agriculture.

They should examine how economic incentives and colonial power reinforced the system.

13. Indigenous Population Decline and Labor Transformation

Connect with Lesson 6.

Teach interacting factors:

Epidemic disease
Warfare
displacement
forced labor
food-system disruption
colonial exploitation

Students should understand how population decline affected labor systems without implying that this was the only reason African slavery expanded.

14. Resistance

Resistance is required content.

Teach examples such as:

Escaping
Maroon communities
Revolts
sabotage
cultural preservation
negotiation
legal petitions
Indigenous resistance
preservation of language, religion, family, and community

Students must not encounter enslaved or colonized people only as victims.

Agency and resistance are essential.

15. Long-Term Consequences

Teach:

Demographic transformation
African diaspora
cultural exchange
racial hierarchy
wealth accumulation
social inequality
political power
plantation economies
long-lasting consequences of slavery and colonialism

Do not carry the lesson too far into modern history; Lesson 8 will synthesize Unit 3.

Required Vocabulary

Students must copy all provided terms and definitions into the Historian's Notebook.

Term	Required Student-Friendly Definition
Colony	A territory controlled by an outside political power
Colonization	The process of establishing political control and settlement over another territory
Encomienda	A Spanish colonial system granting colonists rights to demand tribute or labor from Indigenous communities under specified obligations
Repartimiento	A Spanish colonial labor system requiring Indigenous communities to provide rotating labor to colonial authorities
Plantation	A large agricultural estate producing crops for commercial sale, often using coerced labor
Cash crop	A crop grown primarily for sale rather than local consumption
Mercantilism	An economic approach emphasizing state control of trade and accumulation of wealth through regulated commerce and colonies
Chattel slavery	A system in which enslaved people are legally treated as property and slavery can be hereditary
Middle Passage	The forced Atlantic voyage that transported enslaved Africans to the Americas
Forced labor	Work demanded through coercion rather than freely chosen employment
Racial hierarchy	A social and legal system ranking groups according to constructed racial categories
African diaspora	The dispersal of African peoples and their descendants to regions outside Africa
Resistance	Actions taken to oppose, survive, undermine, or escape systems of power or coercion
Maroon community	A community established by people who escaped slavery
Colonial extraction	Removal of labor, resources, or wealth from a colony for the benefit of an imperial power or colonial elite

Required Source-Limitation Instruction

Students must understand:

Colonial officials often documented systems from the perspective of administrators or owners.
Reform critics may emphasize abuses for persuasive purposes.
Enslaved-person testimony reflects lived experience but may not represent every location or period.
Quantitative records may undercount people or omit illegal activity.
Maps simplify routes, legal systems, and regional differences.
Surviving evidence is uneven because powerful institutions preserved more records.

Required P04 Modeled Example

Codex must model reasoning such as:

European demand for sugar helped increase the profitability of plantation agriculture. Plantation owners required large amounts of labor, and colonial governments created legal systems that allowed coerced labor and slavery to expand. Atlantic shipping networks then transported enslaved Africans to plantation regions. The growth of slavery therefore resulted from interaction among economic demand, colonial political power, and legal coercion rather than from a single cause.

The modeled example should not become the student's checkpoint answer.

Required P04 Common Misconceptions
Misconception 1

Encomienda and slavery were exactly the same system.

Correction

Both could involve severe coercion and exploitation, but they had different legal structures and historical development.

Misconception 2

Africans were naturally or historically destined to become enslaved.

Correction

Atlantic racialized slavery developed through historical economic, political, legal, and colonial choices.

Misconception 3

Europeans simply went into Africa and captured every enslaved person themselves.

Correction

African political systems and commercial networks played different roles in supplying captives, while European colonial demand, Atlantic shipping, plantation economies, and legal systems drove the expansion of the Atlantic slave system.

Misconception 4

Enslaved and Indigenous peoples did not resist.

Correction

Resistance took many forms, including escape, revolt, negotiation, cultural preservation, legal action, and community formation.

Independent Investigation — P06
Investigation Task

Students answer:

Which factor was most important in the expansion of forced labor and slavery in the Atlantic colonial system?

Students must evaluate at least four factors:

European demand for cash crops and resources
Colonial political and legal power
Labor demands in plantations and mines
Atlantic trade and shipping networks

Students may also use Indigenous demographic decline as an additional factor, but they must not treat it as a complete explanation.

Required Product
Part A — Factor Evidence

For each factor, students identify:

One specific historical example
One source, map, data, or lesson detail
How the factor contributed to coercive labor
Part B — Ranking

Rank the factors from most to least influential.

Part C — System Comparison

Students explain one important difference between:

Encomienda or repartimiento
and
Chattel slavery
Part D — Resistance

Students identify one example of resistance and explain what it reveals about agency.

Part E — Final Conclusion

Students write a short evidence-based conclusion explaining:

Which factor mattered most
How it interacted with at least one other factor
Why no single cause fully explains the system
