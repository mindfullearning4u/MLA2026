Lesson Title	Scientific Revolution: New Methods and Challenges
Lesson Type	Instructional Lesson
MLA Standards	MLA.WH.SCI.01, MLA.WH.SCI.02
Investigation Question	How did evidence and new methods change European understanding of the natural world?
Primary Historical-Thinking Skill	Claim-Evidence-Reasoning
Supporting Historical-Thinking Skills	Continuity and Change, Comparison, Contextualization
Lesson Focus

Students investigate how new observations, mathematical models, experimentation, and systematic reasoning contributed to major changes in European understandings of the natural world.

The lesson must emphasize that the Scientific Revolution was not a simple story of "old ideas being replaced by correct ones." Instead, students examine:

Continuity with earlier classical, medieval, and Islamic scholarship
New observations
New instruments
Mathematical reasoning
Revision of earlier scientific models
Debate among scholars
Institutional and religious context
Gradual development of scientific practices

Students must learn to distinguish:

Claim
Evidence
Reasoning

and apply this structure to historical scientific debates.

Required Historical Content

Codex must teach all of the following.

1. Historical Context of the Scientific Revolution

Teach:

Growth of Renaissance learning
Increased circulation of books and scientific ideas
Recovery and study of classical texts
Influence of medieval European scholarship
Contributions preserved and developed through Islamic scholarship
Universities and intellectual networks
Improvements in scientific instruments
Increased use of observation and mathematics

Students must understand:

The Scientific Revolution developed from earlier traditions while also introducing important new methods, models, and explanations.

Do not teach the Scientific Revolution as beginning with no prior scientific tradition.

2. Geocentric Model

Teach:

Earth-centered model
Association with Ptolemaic astronomy
Long historical influence
Use of observation and mathematical calculation within the model

Students should not be told that earlier scholars were simply irrational.

Explain that historical models were constructed from available observations, assumptions, and inherited knowledge.

3. Heliocentric Model

Teach:

Sun-centered planetary model
Nicolaus Copernicus
De revolutionibus orbium coelestium
Publication in 1543
Mathematical reasoning
Historical significance of proposing a different structure of the cosmos

Students must understand that Copernicus's model did not immediately settle all astronomical questions.

4. Johannes Kepler

Teach:

Mathematical analysis of planetary motion
Elliptical planetary orbits
Revision of earlier assumptions about perfect circular motion
Importance of mathematical evidence

Students should understand Kepler as refining heliocentric astronomy rather than simply repeating Copernicus.

5. Galileo Galilei

Teach:

Use of telescope for astronomical observation
Observations of the Moon
Moons of Jupiter
Phases of Venus where appropriate
Sunspots where appropriate
Relationship between observations and existing astronomical claims

Students must analyze how observational evidence could support or challenge scientific models.

6. Scientific Instruments

Teach development and use of instruments such as:

Telescope
Microscope where appropriate as broader context
Improved measuring instruments
Devices supporting more precise observation

Students should understand:

Instruments extended human observation but still required interpretation.

7. Francis Bacon and Empiricism

Teach:

Francis Bacon
Emphasis on observation and systematic investigation
Empiricism
Collection of evidence
Testing ideas against experience

Do not state that Bacon single-handedly invented "the scientific method."

8. René Descartes

Where appropriate, teach:

Rational inquiry
Systematic doubt
Role of reason
Mathematical thinking

Use Descartes to demonstrate that new scientific inquiry included multiple approaches rather than one identical procedure followed by every scientist.

9. Isaac Newton

Teach:

Laws of motion
Universal gravitation
Mathematical description of physical relationships
Synthesis of earlier astronomical and physical work
Importance of Newtonian science for later understandings of the natural world

Detailed physics calculations are not required.

The focus is historical significance and the role of evidence and mathematical reasoning.

10. Developing Scientific Methods

Teach scientific practice as including:

Observation
Question formation
Hypothesis or proposed explanation
Measurement
Experimentation where appropriate
Mathematics
Evidence
Replication or repeated observation
Revision of explanations
Critical Accuracy Requirement

Do not teach:

One scientist invented the scientific method.

Instead explain that scientific methods developed gradually through the work of many scholars.

11. Scientific Debate and Institutions

Teach that scientific ideas developed within historical institutions, including:

Universities
Courts
Churches
Scientific societies
Patronage networks
Print culture

Students must recognize that scientific change occurred within broader political, social, and religious contexts.

12. Galileo and Religious Authority

Teach carefully.

Students should understand:

Galileo supported heliocentric interpretations
His ideas became involved in institutional and theological disputes
He was tried by the Roman Inquisition
Historical conflict involved scientific claims, interpretation of scripture, institutional authority, politics, and personality
Critical Accuracy Rule

Do not teach:

The Scientific Revolution was simply "science versus religion."

The relationship was more complex.

Many early modern scientists were religious, and religious institutions could both support and oppose particular scientific developments depending on context.

13. Continuity and Change

Students must identify both.

Continuities

Examples:

Use of earlier Greek knowledge
Continued importance of mathematics
Universities
Religious frameworks
Patronage
Earlier astronomical observation
Changes

Examples:

New astronomical models
Improved instruments
Greater emphasis on systematic observation
Experimental practices
Increased mathematical modeling
Greater willingness to revise inherited explanations
Required Vocabulary

Students must copy all provided terms and definitions into the Historian's Notebook.

Term	Required Student-Friendly Definition
Scientific Revolution	A period of major change in scientific ideas and methods in early modern Europe
Geocentric	Describing a model in which Earth is placed at the center of the universe
Heliocentric	Describing a model in which the planets orbit the Sun
Observation	Careful examination used to gather information or evidence
Experiment	A controlled investigation used to test an idea or relationship
Hypothesis	A proposed explanation that can be examined using evidence
Empiricism	An approach emphasizing knowledge gained through observation and experience
Evidence	Information used to support, challenge, or evaluate a claim
Scientific model	A representation or explanation used to describe how a natural system works
Natural law	A principle describing a consistent pattern or relationship in nature
Claim	A statement or explanation that can be supported with evidence
Reasoning	The explanation connecting evidence to a claim
Continuity	A feature or practice that remains substantially present over time
Change	A meaningful difference that develops over time
Contextualization	Placing an event, idea, or development within the broader circumstances of its historical period

Required Source-Limitation Instruction

Students should understand:

A scientific observation may support a claim without proving every part of a theory.
One scientist's writing represents a particular historical perspective.
A diagram may simplify more complicated evidence.
Later scientific success should not cause students to assume earlier scientists already knew the final outcome.
Secondary historians interpret evidence and may emphasize different causes or consequences.
P04 Historical Thinking Specification
Primary Skill — Claim-Evidence-Reasoning

Codex must explicitly teach the difference among:

Claim

What is being argued or explained?

Evidence

What observation, document, measurement, or fact supports the claim?

Reasoning

Why does that evidence support the claim?

Students must not treat evidence and reasoning as the same thing.

Required CER Process
Step 1 — Identify the Scientific Claim

Example:

Earth moves around the Sun.

Step 2 — Identify Relevant Evidence

Examples:

Phases of Venus
Jupiter's moons
Mathematical planetary observations
Step 3 — Evaluate the Evidence

Ask:

Is it relevant?
Is it reliable?
What does it actually show?
What are its limits?
Step 4 — Explain the Reasoning

Students answer:

Why does this evidence support or challenge the claim?

Step 5 — Compare Competing Explanations

Ask:

Does the evidence better support one model?
Could the evidence be interpreted differently?
Step 6 — Revise the Claim if Necessary

Students must understand that strong historical and scientific reasoning allows claims to change when evidence changes.

Required P04 Modeled Example

Codex must model reasoning such as:

Galileo observed moons orbiting Jupiter. The geocentric worldview was often understood as placing Earth at the center of all celestial motion. Jupiter's moons demonstrated that at least some objects orbited another celestial body. This evidence did not by itself prove every part of the heliocentric model, but it challenged a simple claim that all celestial objects revolved around Earth.

This is an example of careful CER reasoning because it also identifies a limitation.

Required P04 Common Misconceptions
Misconception 1

New scientific evidence instantly proved the heliocentric model and ended debate.

Correction

Scientific change was gradual. Evidence accumulated over time and was interpreted within competing models.

Misconception 2

Galileo invented the telescope.

Correction

The telescope existed before Galileo's astronomical use of improved versions of the instrument.

Misconception 3

Bacon invented the scientific method.

Correction

Scientific methods developed gradually through the work of many scholars.

Misconception 4

The Scientific Revolution was simply science fighting religion.

Correction

Scientific developments occurred within complex religious, political, institutional, and intellectual contexts.
