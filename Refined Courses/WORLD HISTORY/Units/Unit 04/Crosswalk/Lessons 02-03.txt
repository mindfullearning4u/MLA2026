Lesson Focus

Students investigate major Enlightenment ideas concerning political authority, natural rights, law, liberty, religious toleration, and the relationship between governments and the governed.

The lesson centers on representative thinkers including:

Thomas Hobbes
John Locke
Baron de Montesquieu
Voltaire
Jean-Jacques Rousseau

Students compare their arguments rather than treating "the Enlightenment" as one uniform political philosophy.

Students must understand that Enlightenment thinkers often disagreed about:

Human nature
Government
Political participation
Religion
Equality
Liberty

The lesson should also establish connections to Unit 4 Lesson 1 by showing how Enlightenment thinkers evaluated absolutism and limited government.

5. Montesquieu

Teach:

The Spirit of the Laws

Political liberty
Prevention of excessive concentration of authority
Legislative, executive, and judicial functions
Accuracy Requirement

Do not imply Montesquieu invented every form of separated government.

Teach his significance in systematically arguing for institutional division and balance of political power.

6. Voltaire

Teach:

Religious toleration
Freedom of thought
Criticism of intolerance
Criticism of censorship
Criticism of abuses of political/religious authority

Avoid reducing Voltaire to the fabricated or misattributed phrase:

"I disapprove of what you say, but I will defend to the death your right to say it."

Do not use it as a quotation from Voltaire.

7. Rousseau

Teach:

Social contract
Popular sovereignty
General will
Political community
Civic participation
Tension between individual freedom and collective authority

Students should compare Rousseau with Locke rather than treating both as saying the same thing.

8. Natural Rights

Teach natural rights as rights understood to belong to people by nature rather than because a ruler grants them.

Students must distinguish:

Natural rights
Legal privileges
Government-granted benefits
9. Social Contract

Teach social contract as a broad political concept.

Different thinkers used it differently.

Comparison:

Thinker	Why People Form Government	Political Result
Hobbes	Security and protection from disorder	Strong sovereign
Locke	Protection of natural rights	Limited government by consent
Rousseau	Creation of legitimate political community	Popular sovereignty/general will

Students must not assume "social contract" automatically means democracy.

10. Popular Sovereignty

Teach:

Political authority ultimately derived from the people
Consent
Legitimacy
Contrast with divine-right theory

Connect explicitly to Lesson 1.

11. Separation of Powers

Teach:

Division of government authority
Prevention of concentration
Checks on political power
Institutional structure

This concept should prepare students for later revolutionary governments.

12. Religious Toleration and Freedom of Thought

Teach:

Opposition to persecution
Criticism of censorship
Religious toleration
Intellectual freedom

Students should understand that Enlightenment writers differed in how broadly they applied these principles.

13. Limitations and Contradictions of Enlightenment Thought

Required.

Students must examine the difference between universal language and historical practice.

Teach examples such as:

Limited political participation
Women's exclusion
Continuing slavery
Colonialism
Social hierarchy
Property qualifications
Religious prejudice

This prepares students for later revolutionary contradictions.

P04 Historical Thinking Specification
Primary Skill â€” Comparison

Students must compare thinkers using identical categories.

Required process:

Identify the thinkers.
Establish historical context.
Compare their views of political authority.
Compare their views of rights/liberty.
Identify similarities.
Identify meaningful differences.
Explain why the differences matter.
Required P04 Modeled Example

Both Hobbes and Locke used social-contract reasoning, but they reached different conclusions about political authority. Hobbes emphasized security and argued for strong sovereign power, while Locke emphasized natural rights and government by consent. The comparison shows that sharing a political concept does not mean thinkers supported the same type of government.

Required P04 Common Misconceptions
Misconception 1

All Enlightenment thinkers supported democracy.

Correction: Enlightenment thinkers disagreed significantly about political participation and authority.

Misconception 2

Hobbes opposed the social contract.

Correction: Hobbes used social-contract theory but argued it justified strong sovereign authority.

Misconception 3

Enlightenment rights were immediately applied equally to everyone.

Correction: Enlightenment language could be broad, while historical political rights remained restricted.

Misconception 4

Locke, Montesquieu, and Rousseau had identical political philosophies.

Correction: Their arguments overlapped but differed substantially.

Required P04 Modeled Example

Both the American Declaration of Independence and the French Declaration of the Rights of Man used universal language about rights and political legitimacy. However, the American Revolution primarily sought independence from British imperial rule, while the French Revolution directly challenged France's internal social hierarchy and monarchy. Similar Enlightenment principles therefore operated within different historical circumstances and contributed to different revolutionary paths.

Required P04 Common Misconceptions
Misconception 1

The Enlightenment caused both revolutions by itself.

Correction: Enlightenment ideas shaped revolutionary arguments, but economic, political, social, and imperial conditions were also major causes.

Misconception 2

The American and French Revolutions were basically the same.

Correction: They shared important political ideas but differed in context, social structure, goals, radicalization, and outcomes.

Misconception 3

Revolutionary declarations immediately created equal rights for everyone.

Correction: Slavery, gender exclusion, colonial inequality, and restricted political participation continued.

Misconception 4

The storming of the Bastille alone caused the French Revolution.

Correction: It was an important symbolic event within a much larger political and social crisis.
