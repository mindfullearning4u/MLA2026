Lesson Focus

Students investigate two major approaches to monarchy and political authority in early modern Europe:

Absolutism
Constitutional monarchy

Students examine how rulers attempted to centralize political power, why some monarchies became more absolute, and why political developments in England increasingly placed legal and institutional limits on royal authority.

The lesson centers on representative cases:

Louis XIV and France
English monarchy and Parliament
English Civil War
Restoration
Glorious Revolution
English Bill of Rights

Students must compare political systems using evidence rather than reducing the lesson to the claim that one system had "unlimited power" and the other had "democracy."

The major conceptual distinction is:

Both systems were monarchies, but political authority was organized and limited differently.

Even rulers commonly described as absolute faced:

Teach absolutism as a claim and system of concentrated royal authority rather than perfect total control.

7. Constitutional Monarchy

Teach constitutional monarchy as a political system in which:

A monarch remains head of state
Political authority is limited through laws and institutions
Representative institutions participate in governance
The monarch cannot exercise all governing authority independently

For this period, England serves as the central case.

Do not equate constitutional monarchy in the 1600s with modern universal democracy.

Required P04 Modeled Example

Codex must model comparison using the same category.

Example:

Both France and England had monarchs, but the monarch's relationship with lawmaking institutions differed. Louis XIV concentrated substantial authority in the French crown and managed noble political influence through royal institutions and court culture. In England, conflict between monarchs and Parliament ultimately strengthened Parliament's authority, particularly after the Glorious Revolution and English Bill of Rights. The key difference was therefore not the existence of a monarch, but the institutional limits placed on that monarch.

This models comparison without becoming the checkpoint answer.

Required P04 Common Misconceptions
Misconception 1

An absolute monarch had completely unlimited power.

Correction

Absolute monarchs sought highly centralized authority but continued to face financial, institutional, social, and practical constraints.

Misconception 2

Constitutional monarchy meant England became a modern democracy.

Correction

Royal authority became more legally and institutionally limited, but political participation remained restricted.

Misconception 3

Louis XIV used Versailles only because he liked luxury.

Correction

Versailles also served political functions involving court ritual, patronage, status, access, and management of elite competition.

Misconception 4

The Glorious Revolution immediately gave everyone political rights.

Correction

It strengthened Parliament and limited royal authority, but rights and political participation were still unequal and restricted.
