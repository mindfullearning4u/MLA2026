# Phase 3A-A: English I Mapping Framework

## Executive Summary

This document establishes the official English I mapping rules for distributing standards across the approved MLA structure:

- 6 units
- 8 lessons per unit
- Lessons 1-7 as instructional lessons
- Lesson 8 as Putting It All Together with the Unit Assessment
- Unit Pretest
- Guided Practice
- Notebook Evidence
- Checkpoint Submission
- Lesson Quiz
- Unit Assessment

This framework does not create the English I mapping. It does not assign individual standards to specific lessons, create lessons, create assessments, create HTML files, create quizzes, or create GIFT files.

The central finding from the workload analysis is that the future mapping must protect Units 2 and 3 from overload. Unit 2 contains seven mastery standards, and Unit 3 contains six mastery standards. Both can remain viable only if future mapping combines naturally related standards, sequences prerequisite skills carefully, and preserves MLA's self-paced instructional design standards.

## English I Mapping Rules

### 1. Mapping Philosophy

English I mapping must be designed for independent student mastery. The curriculum must teach, model, scaffold, and verify the target skill without assuming live teacher instruction.

Future mapping must preserve the approved unit blueprint and crosswalk placements while distributing standards in a way that supports:

- Remedial learners who need explicit instruction and additional modeling.
- Standard learners who need complete instruction and guided practice.
- Accelerated learners who need efficient pathways without skipped expectations.

### 2. Primary Standards

A primary standard is the main standard for which a lesson or unit is responsible for direct mastery evidence.

Primary standards must:

- Receive explicit instruction.
- Include modeling or worked examples.
- Include guided practice before independent application.
- Include a mastery check aligned to the standard.
- Be taught deeply enough for independent student mastery.

Primary status does not mean a standard appears only once. It means that its major instructional ownership belongs to the assigned unit.

### 3. Support Standards

A support standard enables success with one or more primary standards. Support standards may include paraphrase, academic vocabulary, conventions, revision, evidence use, discussion, and related reading/writing habits.

Support standards must:

- Be explicitly taught when first introduced.
- Be reinforced in later units.
- Appear in authentic reading, writing, research, or communication contexts.
- Support mastery of primary standards without taking over the lesson.

Support status does not mean optional. Support standards still require instruction and evidence, but they should not overload every lesson as if each were a separate primary target.

### 4. Spiral Standards

The following skills must spiral across English I:

- Language
- Vocabulary
- Revision
- Paraphrase
- Evidence
- Discussion

Spiral reinforcement should appear as targeted, purposeful reuse of previously taught skills. Spiral standards should not become hidden new instruction unless the skill has not yet been taught.

### 5. Mastery Standard Distribution

Mastery standards should be distributed so that each instructional lesson has enough room for the full MLA instructional sequence:

1. Teach the concept.
2. Model the concept.
3. Show worked examples.
4. Show common mistakes.
5. Provide guided practice.
6. Provide independent practice.
7. Verify mastery.

When multiple mastery standards appear together, they must be naturally connected. Artificial grouping is not acceptable because it weakens independent learning.

### 6. Support Standard Reinforcement

Support standards should be reinforced through brief, targeted use in the context of the primary skill.

Examples of acceptable support use:

- Paraphrasing before analyzing theme.
- Academic vocabulary during reading and writing.
- Conventions during written response.
- Evidence selection during argument or literary analysis.
- Discussion during interpretation or claim evaluation.
- Revision after drafting.

Examples of unacceptable support use:

- Adding multiple support standards to a lesson without instructional purpose.
- Treating every support standard as a separate mastery demand in every unit.
- Assuming students can use support skills without prior explicit instruction.

## Lesson Load Rules

### 1. Maximum Mastery Standards Per Lesson

Default rule:

- A standard instructional lesson should target **one primary mastery standard**.

Conditional exception:

- A lesson may include **two primary mastery standards** only when the standards are inseparable or naturally paired.

Examples of naturally paired standards:

- Literary elements and theme development.
- Text structure and central idea support.
- Author purpose and rhetorical choices.
- Argument evaluation and argument writing preparation.

Prohibited:

- Three or more primary mastery standards in one instructional lesson.
- Pairing unrelated mastery standards to fit schedule pressure.
- Introducing a full writing mode while also introducing multiple new reading-analysis standards.

### 2. Maximum Support Standards Per Lesson

Default rule:

- A lesson may include up to **two support standards** when they directly help students master the primary skill.

Conditional exception:

- A third support standard may appear only if it is lightweight, previously taught, and does not require new instruction.

Prohibited:

- Loading a lesson with support standards that require separate teaching.
- Listing support standards that are not visible in instruction or evidence.
- Treating support standards as decorative alignment tags.

### 3. Appropriate Lesson Complexity

Each lesson must remain teachable for self-paced learners. A lesson is appropriately complex when:

- The target skill is clear.
- The cognitive demand is manageable.
- Examples are sufficient for struggling learners.
- Guided practice prepares students for independent work.
- The lesson quiz checks the taught skill, not an untaught extension.
- Accelerated learners can move efficiently without unnecessary repetition.

A lesson is overloaded when:

- Students must learn several unrelated skills at once.
- Reading demand, writing demand, and vocabulary demand are all high in the same lesson.
- The lesson requires background knowledge that has not been taught.
- The lesson asks for analysis, writing, research, or revision before showing how to do it.

### 4. Unit 2 Load Rules

Unit 2 requires special protection because it contains the heaviest mastery-standard count.

Future mapping should:

- Combine literary elements with theme only when the same text supports both.
- Combine narrator perspective with figurative language/mood only when the analysis is coherent.
- Protect epic poetry from being compressed into a brief genre mention.
- Protect adaptation analysis from being reduced to summary comparison.
- Keep narrative writing connected to literary craft without allowing it to crowd out literary analysis.

Future mapping should avoid:

- Treating short stories, poetry, epic poetry, adaptation, perspective, theme, and narrative writing as separate mini-units.
- Assigning one full mastery benchmark to every lesson without room for synthesis.
- Asking students to analyze irony, satire, epic poetry, and adaptation without sufficient models.

### 5. Unit 3 Load Rules

Unit 3 also requires special protection because it combines informational analysis, rhetoric, argument evaluation, and argument writing.

Future mapping should:

- Teach informational structure and central idea support before argument evaluation.
- Teach author purpose and rhetoric before asking students to evaluate rhetorical effectiveness.
- Teach argument reading before argument writing.
- Use shared texts where possible to reduce reading load.
- Distinguish central idea support, claim support, evidence credibility, and reasoning.

Future mapping should avoid:

- Moving too quickly from identifying claims to writing full arguments.
- Compressing counterclaim and rebuttal instruction.
- Treating rhetoric as vocabulary only.
- Combining rhetorical analysis and full argument writing without guided practice.

## Unit Structure Rules

### Lessons 1-7

Lessons 1-7 are instructional lessons. Each should have a clear primary instructional target and should include the full MLA self-paced learning sequence at an appropriate scale.

Each instructional lesson should include:

- Explicit concept instruction.
- Model or worked example.
- Common mistake or misconception support when relevant.
- Guided Practice.
- Independent application.
- Notebook Evidence or other visible learning evidence when appropriate.
- Checkpoint Submission when the task requires reviewable student work.
- Lesson Quiz aligned to the taught skill.

### Lesson 8

Lesson 8 is Putting It All Together.

Lesson 8 should:

- Synthesize the unit's major skills.
- Reinforce connections among standards already taught in the unit.
- Prepare students to demonstrate mastery on the Unit Assessment.
- Avoid introducing major new mastery standards.

Lesson 8 contains the Unit Assessment. It should not become a catch-all location for standards that did not fit earlier.

### Unit Pretest

The Unit Pretest should measure readiness and prior knowledge for the unit's major skills.

The Unit Pretest should:

- Inform pacing and intervention.
- Identify prerequisite gaps.
- Preview the unit's skill domain without replacing instruction.

The Unit Pretest should not:

- Serve as instruction.
- Count as the only evidence for a standard.
- Introduce standards not taught in the unit.

### Guided Practice

Guided Practice must appear before independent application.

Guided Practice should:

- Use prompts, frames, annotations, models, charts, or step-by-step questions.
- Help students practice the target skill with support.
- Make the thinking process visible.
- Reduce support gradually as independence increases.

### Notebook Evidence

Notebook Evidence should capture student thinking and progress toward mastery.

Notebook Evidence may include:

- Annotated text responses.
- Vocabulary reasoning.
- Evidence selection.
- Short written explanations.
- Planning notes.
- Revision reflections.
- Research notes.

Notebook Evidence should not become busywork. It must connect directly to the lesson's instructional target.

### Checkpoint Submission

Checkpoint Submission should be used when student work needs review, feedback, or staged development.

Checkpoint Submission is especially appropriate for:

- Multi-step writing.
- Research planning.
- Source evaluation.
- Argument development.
- Revision work.
- Presentation planning.

Checkpoint Submission should not be added to every lesson automatically.

### Lesson Quiz

Lessons 1-7 contain Lesson Quizzes.

Lesson Quizzes should:

- Check the lesson's taught skill.
- Reflect the cognitive rigor of the target standard.
- Avoid testing untaught extensions.
- Provide useful evidence of readiness for the next lesson.

### Unit Assessment

The Unit Assessment belongs in Lesson 8.

The Unit Assessment should:

- Measure unit-level mastery.
- Reflect the unit's primary standards.
- Include support standards only where they naturally support the unit's mastery expectations.
- Avoid assessing standards that were only lightly reinforced.

## Workload Protection Rules

### 1. Benchmark Overload Protection

No instructional lesson should be responsible for more standards than it can teach, model, practice, and verify.

Future mapping must flag any lesson that includes:

- More than two primary mastery standards.
- More than two support standards requiring active instruction.
- Reading, writing, research, and communication demands all at once.

### 2. Lesson Overload Protection

A lesson should not require students to master a new reading-analysis skill and a new full writing skill at the same time unless the writing is a short, scaffolded response directly supporting the reading skill.

Writing-heavy lessons should reduce new reading load.

Reading-heavy lessons should use writing to explain analysis, not to introduce a full writing mode.

### 3. Reading Overload Protection

Reading load should be controlled by:

- Using shorter texts when the analytical skill is new.
- Reusing texts across related skills when appropriate.
- Avoiding excessive background reading.
- Providing vocabulary and context supports.
- Separating first comprehension from advanced interpretation when needed.

### 4. Writing Overload Protection

Writing load should be controlled by:

- Moving from sentence-level response to paragraph-level response to extended writing.
- Modeling structure before independent writing.
- Separating planning, drafting, revising, and polishing when the task is complex.
- Avoiding full essays before students have practiced claim, evidence, explanation, organization, and transitions.

### 5. Research Overload Protection

Research load should be controlled by:

- Teaching research questions before source gathering.
- Teaching source reliability, validity, credibility, and bias before synthesis.
- Separating source notes from source integration.
- Avoiding unsupported directions to "conduct research."

### 6. Communication Overload Protection

Communication load should be controlled by:

- Introducing discussion and oral explanation in small ways before Unit 6.
- Teaching audience and purpose before presentation production.
- Separating presentation organization from digital design.
- Reusing research and evidence skills in communication tasks.

### 7. Support-Standard Overload Protection

Spiraled standards should strengthen learning without crowding the lesson.

Support standards should be included when they are:

- Necessary for the primary skill.
- Previously taught or explicitly reviewed.
- Visible in student work.
- Reasonable within the lesson's time and cognitive demand.

## Recommendations for Phase 3A-B

Phase 3A-B should create the draft mapping only after applying these rules.

Recommended Phase 3A-B controls:

- Start with Unit 2 and Unit 3 workload checks before assigning lesson-level standards.
- Limit most instructional lessons to one primary mastery standard.
- Allow two primary mastery standards only for natural, defensible pairings.
- Ensure Lesson 8 does not become a place for untaught standards.
- Require early reinforcement of language, vocabulary, revision, paraphrase, evidence, and discussion.
- Treat Unit 4 as higher workload than its benchmark count suggests because research and expository writing require multi-step instruction.
- Keep all future mapping auditable by making primary standards, support standards, and spiral standards clearly distinguishable.

## Stop Point

This document establishes English I mapping rules only. It does not create the English I mapping, assign standards to individual lessons, create lessons, create assessments, create HTML files, create quizzes, or create GIFT files.
