# English I Phase 1C-A Benchmark Analysis and Clustering

## Scope Control

This report is benchmark analysis only.

The approved MLA structure is acknowledged but not developed here:

- 6 Units
- 8 Lessons per Unit
- Lesson 8 = Putting It All Together
- Lesson 8 contains Unit Assessment
- Lessons 1-7 contain Lesson Quizzes
- Unit Pretest
- Guided Practice
- Notebook Evidence
- Checkpoint Submission

This report does not recommend structural changes.

This report does not create lessons, assessments, benchmark-to-unit mappings, crosswalks, lesson sequences, assessment items, quiz items, HTML files, or course architecture.

This report does not assign benchmarks to units.

## Executive Summary

English I requires mastery of the Florida B.E.S.T. Grade 9 ELA benchmark set across Reading, Communication, and Vocabulary. Within curriculum design, those benchmarks function as six instructional classifications:

- Reading
- Writing
- Language
- Vocabulary
- Research
- Communication

The Grade 9 benchmark load is reading-heavy, with 12 Reading benchmarks, 8 Communication benchmarks, and 3 Vocabulary benchmarks. Writing, research, language, oral presentation, digital presentation, and collaborative publication are coded within the Communication strand in Florida B.E.S.T.

The benchmarks naturally cluster into eight curriculum skill groups:

| Cluster | Benchmark Count | Primary Skill Emphasis |
|---|---:|---|
| Foundational Reading and Academic Response | 3 | Paraphrase, academic vocabulary, standard English foundations |
| Literary Analysis and Craft | 6 | Literary elements, theme, perspective, epic poetry, figurative language, adaptation |
| Informational Text and Rhetoric | 4 | Text structure, central idea, purpose, rhetoric |
| Argument Analysis and Argument Writing | 2 | Opposing arguments, claims, evidence, counterclaims |
| Narrative Writing and Literary Craft Application | 1 | Narrative techniques, point of view, transitions |
| Research and Expository Communication | 3 | Research, multi-source explanation, collaborative publication |
| Language, Vocabulary, and Revision | 4 | Conventions, revision, etymology, context, word relationships |
| Oral and Digital Communication | 2 | Oral presentation and digital presentation |

The biggest curriculum challenge is that many English I benchmarks are compound skills. For example, argument writing requires claims, reasoning, credible evidence, multiple sources, counterclaims, organization, elaboration, transitions, and tone. Research requires question development, source reliability, validity, multiple sources, and scope refinement. These should be treated as high-rigor mastery benchmarks that need repeated support.

No benchmark is optional. Some benchmarks should be treated as direct mastery benchmarks because they represent major end-of-course skills. Others should be treated as support benchmarks because they enable performance across many tasks and must spiral throughout the course.

- Moderate: skill requires comprehension, explanation, accurate application, or controlled production.

## Benchmark Cluster Report

### Cluster 1: Foundational Reading and Academic Response

Included benchmarks:

- ELA.9.R.3.2
- ELA.9.C.3.1
- ELA.9.V.1.1

Shared skills:

- Accurate comprehension.
- Paraphrase without changing meaning.
- Academic vocabulary use.
- Standard English control.
- Evidence-ready written and spoken response.

Why these benchmarks belong together:

These benchmarks are the foundation for all other English I work. Students cannot sustain literary analysis, informational analysis, argument, research, revision, or presentation without accurate paraphrase, academic vocabulary, and basic command of conventions.

### Cluster 2: Literary Analysis and Craft

Included benchmarks:

- ELA.9.R.1.1
- ELA.9.R.1.2
- ELA.9.R.1.3
- ELA.9.R.1.4
- ELA.9.R.3.1
- ELA.9.R.3.3

Shared skills:

- Literary element analysis.
- Universal theme development.
- Narrator perspective.
- Irony and satire.
- Epic poetry analysis.
- Figurative language and mood.
- Adaptation comparison.
- Evidence-based literary interpretation.

Why these benchmarks belong together:

These benchmarks all require students to analyze how literary texts create meaning through craft, structure, perspective, theme, genre tradition, and language. They should be clustered because they reinforce the same interpretive habits at increasing complexity.

### Cluster 3: Informational Text and Rhetoric

Included benchmarks:

- ELA.9.R.2.1
- ELA.9.R.2.2
- ELA.9.R.2.3
- ELA.9.R.3.4

Shared skills:

- Central idea analysis.
- Text structure and feature analysis.
- Author purpose.
- Rhetorical appeals.
- Figurative language in nonfiction.
- Evaluation of support.
- Explanation of rhetorical choices.

Why these benchmarks belong together:

These benchmarks focus on how informational and rhetorical texts are built. They require students to connect structure, support, purpose, and rhetoric rather than read nonfiction only for facts.

### Cluster 4: Argument Analysis and Argument Writing

Included benchmarks:

- ELA.9.R.2.4
- ELA.9.C.1.3

Shared skills:

- Claims.
- Evidence.
- Reasoning.
- Validity.
- Opposing arguments.
- Counterclaims.
- Credible sources.
- Logical organization.
- Argumentative tone.

Why these benchmarks belong together:

Argument writing should be grounded in argument reading. Students need to compare and evaluate opposing arguments before writing their own arguments with claims, evidence, reasoning, counterclaim rebuttal, and appropriate tone.

### Cluster 5: Narrative Writing and Literary Craft Application

Included benchmarks:

- ELA.9.C.1.2

Shared skills:

- Narrative techniques.
- Clearly established point of view.
- Varied transitions.
- Sequencing.
- Writing craft.
- Connection between author craft and student writing.

Why these benchmarks belong together:

Narrative writing is a distinct required writing mode. It belongs near literary craft conceptually because students use point of view, pacing, transitions, and narrative techniques to understand how authors shape narrative meaning and to produce controlled narrative writing.

### Cluster 6: Research and Expository Communication

Included benchmarks:

- ELA.9.C.4.1
- ELA.9.C.1.4
- ELA.9.C.5.2

Shared skills:

- Research question development.
- Source reliability and validity.
- Question scope refinement.
- Multi-source explanation.
- Source integration.
- Logical organization.
- Publication-ready writing.
- Audience awareness.

Why these benchmarks belong together:

Research and expository writing are connected because students must gather, evaluate, and synthesize information before explaining it clearly. Collaborative publication belongs with this cluster because it requires audience-aware, polished, source-informed writing.

### Cluster 7: Language, Vocabulary, and Revision

Included benchmarks:

- ELA.9.C.1.5
- ELA.9.C.3.1
- ELA.9.V.1.2
- ELA.9.V.1.3

Shared skills:

- Revision for clarity and cohesion.
- Grammar.
- Usage.
- Punctuation.
- Capitalization.
- Spelling.
- Etymology.
- Derivations.
- Context clues.
- Figurative language.
- Word relationships.
- Reference materials.
- Connotation and denotation.

Why these benchmarks belong together:

These benchmarks improve the precision and correctness of student communication. They belong together because language control, vocabulary acquisition, and revision all affect how clearly students read, write, speak, and present ideas.

### Cluster 8: Oral and Digital Communication

Included benchmarks:

- ELA.9.C.2.1
- ELA.9.C.5.1

Shared skills:

- Oral presentation.
- Digital presentation.
- Logical organization.
- Coherent focus.
- Clear perspective.

Why these benchmarks belong together:

Both benchmarks require students to communicate ideas beyond traditional written response. They depend on organization, evidence, perspective, clarity, and audience awareness.

### High-Density Areas

The highest-density areas are:

- Literary analysis and craft.
- Informational text and rhetoric.
- Writing from evidence and sources.
- Language/vocabulary/revision as ongoing support.

Curriculum implications:

- Literary analysis cannot be reduced to theme only.
- Informational analysis must include central idea, structure, support, purpose, rhetoric, and argument.
- Writing instruction must cover narrative, argument, expository, revision, and publication-quality communication.
- Vocabulary and conventions must spiral because they support all other performance areas.

### Low-Density Areas

The lowest-density areas are:

- Research, if counted only by official benchmark number.
- Oral presentation.
- Digital presentation.
- Collaborative publication.
- Grammar/conventions, if counted only by official benchmark number.

Curriculum implications:

- Low benchmark count does not mean low instructional importance.
- Research is a single benchmark but contains multiple complex skills.
- Oral and digital presentation each require direct performance readiness.
- Conventions are a single benchmark but affect every writing and communication task.

### Potential Curriculum Challenges

1. Reading benchmark density is high.

Students must analyze literature, informational text, rhetoric, argument, figurative language, adaptation, central ideas, and text structures. The curriculum must avoid treating these as isolated one-day skills.

2. Several benchmarks are compound.

Argument writing, expository writing, research, oral presentation, and collaborative publication each contain multiple subskills. These benchmarks require sustained development.

3. Support benchmarks can be under-taught.

Paraphrase, conventions, academic vocabulary, and revision may look simpler than high-rigor analysis standards, but failure in these areas weakens performance across the course.

4. Communication strand includes several different instructional functions.

Florida codes writing, language, research, oral presentation, digital presentation, and collaborative publication under Communication. Curriculum design must separate these functions for instruction while preserving their relationship as communication skills.

5. Vocabulary must support both reading and production.

Vocabulary benchmarks require students to determine meaning and use academic vocabulary in speaking and writing. Word study should connect to real texts, analysis, writing, and communication.

## Curriculum Design Considerations

The following considerations should guide Phase 1C-B and Phase 2 but do not assign benchmarks to units:

- Treat literary analysis and informational analysis as separate high-density clusters.
- Build argument writing from argument reading and evidence evaluation.
- Pair research with expository communication because both depend on source evaluation and synthesis.
- Spiral paraphrase, conventions, academic vocabulary, and revision across the full course.
- Use language instruction to improve authentic writing, not as isolated grammar coverage.
- Use vocabulary instruction to support comprehension, analysis, and precise communication.
- Ensure oral and digital communication receive direct attention, not only informal use.
- Preserve the approved MLA structure without changing unit count, lesson count, lesson 8 function, or required instructional components.
- Do not create a crosswalk until this benchmark analysis and clustering report is approved.
