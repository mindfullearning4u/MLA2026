# English I Course Overview and Expectations

**Status:** Finalized after AI and human audit

**Course code:** ENG1

**Credit:** 1.0

**Delivery:** Online, asynchronous, mastery-based

**Teacher support:** Teacher of Record (TOR)

English I develops close reading, literary and informational analysis, research, argument, writing, language, oral communication, and digital presentation skills. Moodle is the official instructional sequence. This file is the GitHub backup of the finalized course organization and expectations.

## Course organization

English I contains **6 units and 45 lessons**. Unit 1 contains five lessons. Units 2–6 contain eight lessons each. Every unit begins with an ungraded pretest and ends with a graded unit assessment.

### Unit 1 — Literary and Informational Foundations

1. Figurative Language
2. Rhetoric
3. Rhetorical Devices
4. Types of Logical Reasoning
5. Fallacies in Reasoning

Assessment sequence: Unit 1 Pretest; Cluster Quiz 1 for Lessons 1–2; Cluster Quiz 2 for Lessons 3–4; Unit 1 Assessment after Lesson 5.

### Unit 2 — Literary Analysis and Narrative Craft

1. Literary Elements and Layers of Meaning
2. Theme Development Through Literary Elements
3. Narrator Perspective, Irony, and Satire
4. Figurative Language and Mood
5. Epic Poetry: Character, Structure, and Theme
6. Literary Adaptation Comparison
7. Narrative Craft Connected to Literary Analysis
8. Putting It All Together: Unit 2 Synthesis

### Unit 3 — Informational Text, Rhetoric, and Argument

1. Informational Text Structure and Features
2. Central Idea Support and Evidence Quality
3. Author Purpose and Rhetorical Appeals
4. Rhetorical Choices and Effect
5. Opposing Arguments, Claims, and Validity
6. Argument Writing: Claims, Evidence, and Reasoning
7. Argument Writing: Counterclaims, Organization, and Tone
8. Putting It All Together: Unit 3 Synthesis

### Unit 4 — Research and Expository Writing

1. Research Questions and Inquiry Focus
2. Source Reliability, Validity, Credibility, and Bias
3. Note-Taking, Paraphrase, and Source Synthesis
4. Expository Organization from Research Findings
5. Source Integration, Transitions, and Task-Appropriate Tone
6. Collaborative Publication and Audience Awareness
7. Publication-Ready Source-Based Communication
8. Putting It All Together: Unit 4 Synthesis

### Unit 5 — Imagined Futures, Survival, and Speculative Writing

1. Standard English Conventions in Authentic Writing
2. Etymology, Derivations, and Word Families
3. Context, Connotation, Denotation, and Tone

5. Feedback and Revision for Clarity
6. Cohesion, Transitions, and Sentence-Level Control
7. Style, Precision, and Publication Polish
8. Putting It All Together: Unit 5 Synthesis

### Unit 6 — Synthesis, Presentation, and Digital Communication

1. Oral Communication Purpose, Audience, and Perspective
2. Organizing Oral Information with Credible Evidence
3. Presenting a Clear Perspective with Coherent Focus
4. Digital Presentation Organization and Coherent Ideas
5. Digital Presentation Evidence, Clarity, and Audience Awareness
6. Integrated Oral and Digital Communication
7. Full-Course Synthesis for Presentation Readiness
8. Putting It All Together: Unit 6 Synthesis

For Units 2–6, Lessons 1–7 use lesson quizzes. Lesson 8 is the unit synthesis and does not have a separate lesson quiz. Each unit includes a pretest and unit assessment.

## Six-page lesson sequence

Every lesson uses the following page order:

1. P01 — Lesson Overview
2. P02 — Vocabulary and Definitions
3. P03 — Lesson
4. P04 — Worked Example
5. P05 — Independent Work
6. P06 — Checkpoint

The lesson overview identifies the lesson title, standards covered, learning goals, why the lesson matters, what students will do, how students will show mastery, and how to contact the TOR.

The vocabulary page is the notebook task. Students record every lesson-specific term and its complete definition in their notebook.

The lesson page provides direct instruction, clear steps, multiple examples, common mistakes, and TOR help. The worked example provides a relevant passage, model, or scenario with a step-by-step explanation. Independent Work and the Checkpoint use substantial lesson-aligned readings or tasks and include all questions directly in Moodle.

## Required submissions

### Notebook Evidence Submission

Notebook Evidence contains only:

- every lesson vocabulary term with its complete definition; and
- the completed Independent Work.

### Checkpoint Submission

Checkpoint answers are submitted separately. Checkpoint work is not part of Notebook Evidence.

### What to include

At the top of every Notebook Evidence and Checkpoint submission:

- Student Name
- Course Name
- Unit and Lesson

Example:

John Smith

English 1 — Unit 1, Lesson 1

### Submission format

- PDF (preferred)
- Microsoft Word (.doc or .docx)
- Images: JPG, JPEG, or PNG

If using Google Docs, students must download and submit the work as a PDF or Word file. Links are not accepted.

## Mastery and TOR support

The minimum mastery threshold is **80%**. If a student earns below 80%, the student must review TOR feedback, correct mistakes, and resubmit through the designated activity. The student should meet with the TOR for extra help and guidance when needed.

Rubric criteria are visible to students. Lesson-specific answer guides are stored in TOR-only rubric descriptions and are not student-visible.

## Student-facing rendering standard

All student-facing lesson, Notebook Evidence, Checkpoint, and orientation pages must:

- render as formatted student content rather than exposed HTML;
- use the approved colored visual blocks on a light background;
- contain no black-background content boxes;
- contain no empty placeholders;
- include visible Next and Previous navigation where applicable; and
- include TOR Help.

## Academic integrity

Students submit their own work and use sources responsibly. Copied answers, shared assessment content, plagiarism, fabricated evidence or sources, unauthorized answer sites, and unapproved AI-generated submissions violate course expectations.
