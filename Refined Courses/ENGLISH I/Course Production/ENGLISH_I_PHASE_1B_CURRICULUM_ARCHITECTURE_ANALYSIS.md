## Executive Summary

English I should be organized as a skills-forward, text-rich Grade 9 ELA course that builds the foundation for high school literary analysis, informational text analysis, argument, expository writing, research, language control, vocabulary development, oral communication, and digital communication.

Florida B.E.S.T. Grade 9 places the strongest architectural pressure on seven recurring strands:

- Literary Analysis
- Informational Text Analysis
- Writing
- Research
- Language
- Vocabulary
- Communication

The optimal MLA English I architecture is a six-unit model that begins with foundational close reading and evidence-based response, moves into literary analysis, then informational/rhetorical analysis and argument, then research and expository writing, then language/style/revision, and ends with synthesis and communication.

The course should not be organized around isolated genres alone. Genres should serve skill development. Students need repeated exposure to literary, informational, argumentative, and communication tasks so that analysis, evidence, clarity, and conventions compound across the year.

The recommended six-unit architecture is:

| Unit | Recommended Title | Primary Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Unit 01 | Foundations of Close Reading and Evidence-Based Writing | Build baseline comprehension, annotation, paraphrase, evidence use, academic vocabulary, and conventions. |
| Unit 02 | Literary Analysis: Theme, Character, Perspective, and Craft | Develop analysis of literary elements, universal theme, narrator perspective, mood, adaptation, and epic/classical connections. |
| Unit 03 | Informational Text, Rhetoric, and Argument | Build central idea analysis, text structure, rhetorical analysis, opposing-argument comparison, claims, evidence, and counterclaims. |
| Unit 04 | Research and Expository Writing | Develop research questions, source reliability, source synthesis, expository organization, source-based explanation, and publication-quality writing. |
| Unit 05 | Language, Vocabulary, Style, and Revision | Strengthen grammar, usage, punctuation, sentence variety, vocabulary acquisition, tone, cohesion, revision, and editing. |
| Unit 06 | Synthesis, Presentation, and Digital Communication | Apply reading, writing, research, vocabulary, and communication skills through oral and digital presentation of a coherent perspective. |

This architecture preserves Florida-specific emphases while also supporting Common Core, SAT, ACT, college readiness, and workforce communication expectations.

## Skill Strand Recommendations

### 1. Literary Analysis

Why it belongs:

Literary analysis is required by Florida Grade 9 Reading benchmarks focused on literary elements, universal themes, narrator perspective, irony, satire, epic poetry, figurative language, and adaptation of mythical, classical, or religious texts. Common Core Grade 9-10 similarly expects analysis of theme, character, structure, point of view, word choice, text complexity, and source adaptation.

What students should master:

- Explain how literary elements add meaning or style.
- Analyze universal theme development across a literary text.
- Analyze narrator perspective and how it shapes irony or satire.
- Analyze epic poetry through characters, structure, and theme.
- Explain how figurative language creates mood.
- Compare adaptations of classical, mythical, or religious source material.
- Support literary claims with precise textual evidence.

How skills should progress:

Begin with literal comprehension, paraphrase, and evidence selection. Move to single-element analysis such as character, setting, conflict, and point of view. Then develop multi-element analysis connecting theme, structure, figurative language, narrator perspective, irony, and adaptation. By the end of the course, students should explain how several craft choices work together to create meaning.

### 2. Informational Text Analysis

Why it belongs:

Florida Grade 9 requires students to analyze text structures and features, evaluate support for central ideas, analyze purpose through rhetoric and figurative language, compare opposing arguments, and explain rhetoric. Common Core Grade 9-10 informational standards reinforce central idea, structure, rhetoric, argument evaluation, reasoning, evidence, and seminal nonfiction. SAT and ACT both test informational comprehension, rhetorical analysis, evidence, source integration, and author purpose.

What students should master:

- Identify and explain central ideas and supporting details.
- Analyze how text structures and features convey purpose and meaning.
- Evaluate the quality and relevance of support.
- Explain an author's rhetorical choices.
- Compare opposing arguments on the same topic.
- Evaluate claim validity, reasoning, evidence, and counterclaims.
- Interpret informational graphics when paired with text.

How skills should progress:

Start with main idea, details, paraphrase, and structure recognition. Progress to central idea development and author purpose. Then build rhetorical analysis, evidence evaluation, and comparison of arguments. Later work should require students to synthesize information across multiple texts and formats.

### 3. Writing

Why it belongs:

Florida Grade 9 writing expectations are housed in the Communication strand: narrative writing, argumentative writing, expository writing, revision, research, oral presentation, digital presentation, and collaborative publication. Common Core Grade 9-10 requires arguments, informative/explanatory writing, narratives, production/distribution of writing, research, evidence use, and technology-supported publication. SAT and ACT writing-related domains emphasize revision, rhetorical goals, standard English conventions, organization, cohesion, precision, and style.

What students should master:

- Write short evidence-based responses to literary and informational texts.
- Write literary analysis that explains how textual evidence supports an interpretation.
- Write expository texts using multiple sources and logical organization.
- Write arguments with claims, credible evidence, reasoning, counterclaims, transitions, tone, and organization.
- Write narratives with clear point of view, narrative techniques, and transitions.
- Revise writing for clarity, cohesion, audience, purpose, tone, and conventions.

How skills should progress:

Begin with paragraph-level evidence-based responses. Expand to multi-paragraph literary analysis. Move into structured argument and expository writing. Add research-based synthesis and source integration. End with independent revision and audience-aware communication.

### 4. Research

Why it belongs:

Florida Grade 9 requires students to conduct research to answer a question, use multiple reliable and valid sources, and refine the question to align with findings. Common Core Grade 9-10 strengthens this through short and sustained research, authoritative sources, advanced searches, usefulness evaluation, selective integration, citation, and avoiding plagiarism. College and workforce readiness require locating, evaluating, integrating, and communicating information clearly.

What students should master:

- Generate and refine research questions.
- Locate multiple reliable and valid sources.
- Distinguish relevance, credibility, accuracy, and usefulness.
- Integrate source information without losing the writer's own structure.
- Use source evidence to explain, argue, or present a coherent perspective.
- Avoid plagiarism and prepare for formal citation expectations.

How skills should progress:

Begin with source awareness and reliability checks. Move to research-question refinement and note-taking. Then add synthesis across sources. Later tasks should require source-based writing and presentation, but Phase 1B does not design those tasks.

### 5. Language

Why it belongs:

Florida Grade 9 requires standard English grammar, punctuation, capitalization, spelling, sentence variety, parallel structure, phrases, clauses, and usage choices that create flow. Common Core Grade 9-10 explicitly includes parallel structure, phrases and clauses, punctuation, spelling, language choice, style manuals, and vocabulary. SAT and ACT both heavily assess grammar, usage, punctuation, sentence structure, precision, concision, style, tone, cohesion, and rhetorical effectiveness.

What students should master:

- Apply standard English grammar and usage in writing and speaking.
- Use capitalization, punctuation, and spelling correctly.
- Use parallel structure appropriately.
- Use phrases and clauses to add sentence variety and precision.
- Revise sentence structure for clarity, flow, and emphasis.
- Maintain consistent tone and style for audience and purpose.

How skills should progress:

Start with convention accuracy in sentence and paragraph writing. Add sentence variety and usage control. Then apply language choices to voice, tone, clarity, cohesion, and rhetorical effectiveness. Language should be embedded in writing and revision rather than isolated from authentic communication.

### 6. Vocabulary

Why it belongs:

Florida Grade 9 requires academic vocabulary integration, etymology and derivations, context clues, figurative language, word relationships, reference materials, connotation, and denotation. Common Core Grade 9-10 aligns closely through context, word-change patterns, reference materials, figurative language, word relationships, nuances, and academic/domain-specific vocabulary. SAT and ACT both require vocabulary in context and precise interpretation of words and phrases.

What students should master:

- Use grade-level academic vocabulary in speaking and writing.
- Determine word meaning through context, reference materials, background knowledge, and word relationships.
- Apply etymology, derivations, roots, and affixes.
- Distinguish connotative and denotative meaning.
- Explain figurative language and rhetorical word choice.
- Use precise words to support analysis, explanation, argument, and presentation.

How skills should progress:

Begin with academic vocabulary for reading and writing tasks. Add context and connotation. Then build morphology, etymology, derivation, and figurative-language analysis. End with independent vocabulary acquisition and intentional use in formal speaking and writing.

### 7. Communication

Why it belongs:

Florida Grade 9 requires oral presentation, digital presentations, and online collaborative publication-quality writing. Common Core Grade 9-10 speaking/listening standards require collaborative discussions, evidence-based participation, evaluating sources and speakers, clear presentations, strategic digital media, and adapting speech to purpose and audience. Workforce readiness depends strongly on clear oral communication, digital collaboration, audience awareness, evidence use, and professional tone.

What students should master:

- Present information orally with logical organization, coherent focus, credible evidence, and clear perspective.
- Use digital presentation tools to communicate coherent ideas.
- Use online collaborative platforms to produce audience-appropriate writing.
- Participate in evidence-based academic discussion.
- Adapt communication to audience, purpose, and format.

How skills should progress:

Begin with structured discussion and short evidence-based speaking. Move to organized oral explanation and collaborative writing. Later work should require students to create coherent digital presentations and communicate a clear perspective using credible evidence.

## Genre Recommendations

English I should use genres as vehicles for standards mastery. The text set should include a deliberate balance of literary, informational, argumentative, and communication-rich genres.

Recommended annual text balance:

| Genre/Text Type | Recommended Role | Suggested Emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| Short stories | Core literary analysis vehicle | High |
| Poetry | Figurative language, mood, theme, style | Medium-high |
| Epic poetry/classical excerpts | Required by Florida Grade 9 literary expectations | Medium |
| Drama excerpts | Perspective, rhetoric, structure, performance language | Medium |
| Novel or extended literary work | Sustained theme, character, structure, and perspective | Medium |
| Informational texts | Central idea, structure, evidence, purpose | High |
| Literary nonfiction | Rhetoric, voice, perspective, civic and historical literacy | High |
| Speeches | Rhetoric, appeals, audience, purpose, style | High |
| Argumentative texts | Claims, counterclaims, reasoning, evidence validity | High |
| Digital/multimodal texts | Presentation, publication, source evaluation | Medium |

Recommended approximate reading distribution:

| Category | Approximate Share |
|---|---:|
| Literary texts, including short fiction, poetry, drama, epic/classical excerpts, and one extended work or equivalent excerpts | 45% |
| Informational and literary nonfiction texts, including essays, speeches, historical documents, articles, and source-based texts | 45% |
| Digital, visual, quantitative, or multimodal texts paired with print texts | 10% |

Rationale:

- Florida B.E.S.T. Grade 9 demands strong literary and informational analysis rather than a one-sided literature survey.
- SAT and ACT readiness require frequent informational, argumentative, rhetorical, and vocabulary-in-context work.
- College and career readiness require sustained nonfiction, evidence evaluation, research, and communication.
- A 45/45/10 balance allows literature to remain central while preventing underdevelopment of informational, rhetorical, and source-based skills.

## Writing Progression Recommendations

Writing should progress from controlled evidence-based response to independent source-based communication. The sequence should develop clarity, organization, evidence, reasoning, revision, and audience control.

### Text-Based Responses

Progression:

1. Accurate paraphrase of grade-level text.
2. Short response with claim, evidence, and explanation.
3. Multi-sentence analysis of how evidence supports meaning.
4. Comparative response across two texts or perspectives.
5. Integrated response using source evidence and academic vocabulary.

Mastery target:

Students can answer a text-based question with a clear claim, accurate evidence, and explanation that connects the evidence to the claim.

### Literary Analysis

Progression:

1. Identify key literary elements.
2. Explain how one element contributes to meaning.
3. Analyze theme development through details.
4. Analyze narrator perspective, irony, satire, mood, and figurative language.
5. Compare adaptation or transformation of classical, mythical, or religious source material.

Mastery target:

Students can explain how author choices create layered literary meaning and support interpretation with textual evidence.

### Informative/Expository Writing

Progression:

1. Explain information from a single source.
2. Organize an explanation around a clear central idea.
3. Use multiple sources to explain and analyze information.
4. Use transitions, academic vocabulary, and formal tone.
5. Revise for clarity, cohesion, and source integration.

Mastery target:

Students can write a logically organized expository text that explains and analyzes information from multiple sources.

### Argument Writing

Progression:

1. Identify claim, evidence, and reasoning.
2. Evaluate claims and support in model texts.
3. Compare opposing arguments on the same topic.
4. Write a claim supported by credible evidence and logical reasoning.
5. Rebut counterclaims with relevant evidence and maintain appropriate tone.

Mastery target:

Students can write an organized argument that supports a position, uses credible evidence, addresses counterclaims, and maintains logical development.

### Narrative Writing

Progression:

1. Establish point of view.
2. Use narrative techniques such as pacing, dialogue, description, and reflection.
3. Use varied transitions and coherent sequence.
4. Develop character, conflict, or event through purposeful detail.
5. Connect narrative craft to literary analysis where appropriate.

Mastery target:

Students can write a narrative with clear point of view, purposeful sequencing, narrative techniques, and control of transitions.

### Research Writing

Progression:

1. Generate and refine a research question.
2. Gather reliable and valid sources.
3. Take notes and distinguish useful evidence.
4. Synthesize information across sources.
5. Write or present findings with coherent organization and source-based support.

Mastery target:

Students can use multiple reliable sources to answer a research question and communicate findings clearly.

## Language Recommendations

Language instruction should be embedded across the course, with a dedicated intensification point before final synthesis work.

Recommended progression:

| Stage | Language Focus | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Early course | Sentence boundaries, punctuation, capitalization, spelling, basic usage | Establish accuracy in evidence-based responses. |
| Literary analysis stage | Sentence variety, phrases, clauses, precise verbs, quotation integration | Support analytical clarity. |
| Argument/informational stage | Parallel structure, transitions, logical relationships, formal tone | Support reasoning and cohesion. |
| Research/expository stage | Source integration, pronoun clarity, concision, consistency, citation readiness | Support source-based writing. |
| Revision stage | Style, flow, punctuation, usage, cohesion, audience-appropriate tone | Improve finished writing. |
| Final synthesis stage | Formal presentation language, oral fluency, digital communication conventions | Support academic and workforce communication. |

Priority language skills:

- Standard English grammar, punctuation, capitalization, and spelling.
- Parallel structure.
- Phrases and clauses for sentence variety.
- Usage choices that create flow.
- Sentence-level revision for precision, concision, and emphasis.
- Consistent tone and style.
- Audience-appropriate formal English in writing and speaking.

## Vocabulary Recommendations

Vocabulary should be developed as a reading, writing, speaking, and research tool rather than as a separate memorization strand.

Recommended progression:

| Stage | Vocabulary Focus | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Early course | Academic vocabulary for reading, evidence, analysis, and discussion | Build common language for English I work. |
| Literary analysis stage | Figurative language, mood, connotation, denotation, literary terms | Support interpretation of literary craft. |
| Informational/rhetoric stage | Rhetoric, appeals, claim, evidence, validity, structure, purpose | Support argument and nonfiction analysis. |
| Research stage | Source, credibility, validity, relevance, synthesis, scope | Support research and source evaluation. |
| Language/revision stage | Etymology, derivation, word relationships, precision, tone | Support independent vocabulary acquisition and revision. |
| Final synthesis stage | Audience-specific academic and professional vocabulary | Support communication and presentation. |

Priority vocabulary practices:

- Integrate academic vocabulary into authentic speaking and writing.
- Use context clues and background knowledge to infer meaning.
- Use etymology, derivation, roots, and affixes.
- Use reference materials strategically.
- Distinguish connotation and denotation.
- Analyze figurative language and rhetorical word choice.
- Use vocabulary intentionally to improve precision and tone.

## Recommended 6-Unit Course Architecture

### Unit 01: Foundations of Close Reading and Evidence-Based Writing

Unit purpose:

Establish the reading, writing, vocabulary, and language routines students will use all year.

Major skills:

- Close reading of grade-level texts.
- Annotation and comprehension monitoring.
- Paraphrasing.
- Evidence selection.
- Academic vocabulary integration.
- Foundational conventions.
- Short evidence-based written response.

Reading focus:

Short literary and informational texts that allow students to practice comprehension, paraphrase, evidence, and initial analysis without excessive genre complexity.

Writing focus:

Short text-based responses using claim, evidence, and explanation.

Language focus:

Standard English sentence control, punctuation, capitalization, spelling, and basic usage in formal written responses.

Vocabulary focus:

Academic vocabulary used across English I: analyze, explain, evidence, claim, central idea, theme, inference, paraphrase, structure, purpose, and context.

Architecture rationale:

Students need a controlled foundation before advanced literary, rhetorical, research, and presentation tasks. This unit should not become a general study-skills unit; every routine should connect directly to text comprehension and evidence-based communication.

### Unit 02: Literary Analysis: Theme, Character, Perspective, and Craft

Unit purpose:

Develop core literary-analysis skills required for Grade 9 literature standards.

Major skills:

- Literary elements and layered meaning.
- Universal theme development.
- Character development and conflict.
- Narrator perspective.
- Irony and satire.
- Figurative language and mood.
- Epic poetry structures, characters, and themes.
- Adaptation of mythical, classical, or religious source texts.
- Narrative writing as craft application.

Reading focus:

Short stories, poetry, drama excerpts, epic/classical excerpts, and selected literary nonfiction or adaptation pairings where appropriate.

Writing focus:

Literary analysis paragraphs and short essays; narrative writing with clear point of view and narrative techniques.

Language focus:

Sentence variety, precise analytical verbs, quotation integration, and transitions for literary explanation.

Vocabulary focus:

Theme, motif, narrator, perspective, irony, satire, mood, figurative language, epic, adaptation, archetype, connotation, and denotation.

Architecture rationale:

Florida Grade 9 literary benchmarks require more than theme identification. Students must explain how craft choices create meaning. This unit should build literary analysis deeply enough to support later synthesis.

### Unit 03: Informational Text, Rhetoric, and Argument

Unit purpose:

Develop nonfiction analysis, rhetorical understanding, and argument readiness.

Major skills:

- Central idea development.
- Text structures and features.
- Author purpose.
- Rhetorical appeals and rhetorical devices.
- Figurative language in informational texts.
- Opposing arguments on the same topic.
- Validity of claims.
- Evidence quality and relevance.
- Argument writing with counterclaims.

Reading focus:

Speeches, essays, articles, historical documents, opinion pieces, and paired argumentative texts.

Writing focus:

Argument writing with claims, logical reasoning, credible evidence, counterclaims, transitions, elaboration, and appropriate tone.

Language focus:

Transitions, parallel structure, logical sentence relationships, concision, and formal argumentative tone.

Vocabulary focus:

Claim, counterclaim, reasoning, evidence, validity, credibility, relevance, rhetoric, ethos, pathos, logos, purpose, structure, tone, and fallacy.

Architecture rationale:

This unit is the center of SAT, ACT, college, and workforce alignment because it teaches students to read arguments, judge evidence, and communicate a position clearly.

### Unit 04: Research and Expository Writing

Unit purpose:

Develop research, source evaluation, source synthesis, and explanatory writing.

Major skills:

- Research question development.
- Refining research scope.
- Reliable and valid source selection.
- Source usefulness evaluation.
- Note-taking and evidence sorting.
- Synthesis across sources.
- Expository writing from multiple sources.
- Publication-quality collaborative writing.

Reading focus:

Informational texts, source sets, reference texts, articles, reports, and digital sources.

Writing focus:

Expository writing that explains and analyzes information from multiple sources using logical organization, varied transitions, and appropriate tone.

Language focus:

Source integration, clarity, cohesion, pronoun/reference clarity, punctuation in source-based sentences, and formal explanatory style.

Vocabulary focus:

Research, source, validity, reliability, credibility, relevance, scope, synthesis, evidence, quotation, paraphrase, summary, citation, and audience.

Architecture rationale:

Research should come after students have practiced both literary and informational analysis. This timing lets research writing build on central idea, evidence, rhetoric, and organization rather than becoming a disconnected library-skills unit.

### Unit 05: Language, Vocabulary, Style, and Revision

Unit purpose:

Strengthen the language, vocabulary, usage, and revision skills that improve reading precision and writing quality across genres.

Major skills:

- Grammar and usage control.
- Punctuation and spelling accuracy.
- Parallel structure.
- Phrases and clauses.
- Word choice for tone and precision.
- Etymology and derivation.
- Context clues and word relationships.
- Connotation and denotation.
- Feedback-based revision.
- Clarity and cohesion.

Reading focus:

Short literary and informational texts selected for style, diction, sentence structure, tone, and vocabulary analysis.

Writing focus:

Revision of prior writing types and targeted style improvement across analytical, expository, argumentative, and narrative writing.

Language focus:

Grade 9 convention expectations, sentence variety, flow, cohesion, punctuation, usage, and editing.

Vocabulary focus:

Academic vocabulary, morphology, etymology, derivations, reference materials, context clues, figurative language, word relationships, connotation, and denotation.

Architecture rationale:

Language and vocabulary should occur all year, but a dedicated unit before final synthesis gives students time to refine the quality of their communication before independent presentation and digital publication work.

### Unit 06: Synthesis, Presentation, and Digital Communication

Unit purpose:

Apply the full English I skill set through synthesis, oral communication, digital presentation, and audience-aware publication.

Major skills:

- Synthesis across texts and sources.
- Coherent perspective.
- Oral presentation with logical organization and credible evidence.
- Digital presentation with coherent ideas.
- Audience-aware writing.
- Collaborative platform use.
- Formal language and academic vocabulary in speaking and writing.

Reading focus:

Short source clusters that require synthesis across literary, informational, rhetorical, or research-based materials.

Writing focus:

Synthesis writing or presentation-support writing that communicates a clear perspective with credible evidence.

Language focus:

Formal oral language, presentation clarity, sentence flow, cohesion, style, tone, and editing for publication quality.

Vocabulary focus:

Synthesis, perspective, audience, publication, presentation, coherence, credibility, collaboration, evidence, and domain-specific academic vocabulary.

Architecture rationale:

The final unit should require students to apply the course's major skills without introducing a new instructional burden that would dilute synthesis. Communication is the correct culminating focus because it integrates reading, writing, research, vocabulary, and language.

## Crosswalk Development Recommendations

Future crosswalk development should happen after approval of the Phase 1B architecture. It should not be embedded in this report as a completed mapping.

Recommended crosswalk structure:

### 1. Master Benchmark Inventory

Create a locked list of all required English I benchmarks:

- Benchmark code.
- Official benchmark language.
- Strand category.
- Skill family.
- Cognitive demand.
- Student performance expectation.
- Required evidence of mastery.

### 2. Primary Coverage Matrix

After units are approved, assign each benchmark one primary unit for focused instruction and mastery evidence.

Recommended fields:

- Unit number.
- Unit title.
- Primary benchmark code.
- Why this unit is the primary location.
- Expected student product type.
- Evidence type.

### 3. Spiral/Supporting Coverage Matrix

Because English I skills recur, each benchmark should also have supporting appearances where appropriate.

Recommended fields:

- Supporting unit.
- Supporting skill role.
- Depth of use: exposure, practice, reinforcement, mastery extension.
- Notes on increasing complexity.

### 4. External Alignment Crosswalk

Create a separate crosswalk connecting Florida B.E.S.T. benchmarks to:

- Common Core Grade 9-10 ELA standards.
- SAT Reading and Writing domains.
- ACT English and Reading domains.
- College readiness expectations.
- Workforce communication expectations.

This should remain a standards-alignment document, not a lesson map.

### 5. Assessment Readiness Matrix

Only after future assessment design is authorized, create a matrix showing how benchmark mastery could be evidenced. Do not create assessment items during crosswalk development unless separately requested.

Recommended fields:

- Benchmark.
- Acceptable evidence type.
- Performance criteria.
- Reading/writing/communication mode.
- Alignment notes.

### 6. Quality-Control Rules

The crosswalk should enforce these rules:

- Every Florida B.E.S.T. English I benchmark receives one primary location.
- Every benchmark receives recurring support where skill development requires it.
- No unit is overloaded with too many first-time benchmark introductions.
- Reading, writing, language, vocabulary, research, and communication are integrated.
- Benchmark language remains unchanged.
- Crosswalk notes distinguish recommendation from implemented curriculum.

## Design Cautions

- Do not make English I a literature-only course. Florida B.E.S.T., SAT, ACT, college readiness, and workforce expectations require informational text, rhetoric, argument, research, and communication.
- Do not isolate grammar into disconnected worksheets. Language should be embedded in writing, revision, speaking, and editing.
- Do not treat vocabulary as memorization only. Vocabulary must support comprehension, analysis, rhetorical interpretation, and precise communication.
- Do not place research too early. Research works best after students can analyze central ideas, evidence, claims, and credibility.
- Do not let Unit 06 become a new-skills unit. It should synthesize and apply the English I skill set.
- Do not build lessons or assessments from this report without an approved crosswalk.
