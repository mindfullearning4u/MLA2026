P03 Notebook Task Part 2

Now decide whether evidence is strong enough to support a central idea.

Direct Teaching

Evidence quality matters. A detail can be true but still weak if it is too general or unrelated. For example, if the central idea is that libraries support community learning, the detail "libraries have doors" is true but weak. The detail "libraries offer free reading programs and research help" is stronger because it directly supports learning.

Strong evidence should answer the question: How does this detail help prove the central idea?

Notebook Directions

Central idea: Public libraries help people access learning opportunities.

Evidence Set: Libraries have shelves. Libraries provide free books, databases, internet access, and homework help. Many libraries are open several days each week.

  1. Identify the strongest evidence.
  2. Identify the weakest evidence.
  3. Explain why the strongest evidence supports the central idea better.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: "Libraries have shelves" is the strongest evidence because shelves are in libraries.

This is weak reasoning. The detail is true, but it does not show how libraries create learning opportunities.

Correct Thinking

Correct: The strongest evidence is that libraries provide free books, databases, internet access, and homework help because those services directly support learning.

This answer connects the evidence to the central idea.

Deeper Learning Support

Lesson focus: Central idea support and evidence quality

Mapped standard focus: MLA.ENG1.R.06

Use this second-pass routine to check the quality of your thinking before you submit your notebook work.

  1. Identify the main point or purpose before analyzing details. Informational texts are organized to help the reader understand an idea.
  2. Notice how the structure works. Headings, order, examples, comparisons, and cause-effect relationships all guide meaning.
  3. Connect evidence to the author's purpose. A detail matters when it helps explain, persuade, clarify, or emphasize the central idea.
  4. Evaluate the effect of the author's choices. Explain how the structure, evidence, or rhetoric changes the reader's understanding.
