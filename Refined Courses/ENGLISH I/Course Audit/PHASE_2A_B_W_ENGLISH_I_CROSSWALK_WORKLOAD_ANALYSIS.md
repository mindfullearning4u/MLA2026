# Phase 2A-B-W: English I Crosswalk Workload Analysis

## Executive Summary

This workload analysis reviews whether the English I Crosswalk Draft can realistically fit within the approved MLA structure of 6 units and 8 lessons per unit while preserving MLA instructional design requirements.

Final workload decision: **WORKLOAD CONCERNS IDENTIFIED**

The overall crosswalk is structurally plausible, but Unit 2 and Unit 3 present significant workload risk because both contain dense clusters of high-rigor mastery standards. The risk is not that the standards are misplaced in this phase; this report does not validate or revise placements. The risk is that future mapping must be extremely careful to combine related skills, sequence prerequisites tightly, and avoid treating every standard as a separate full lesson sequence.

The MLA instructional model requires explicit instruction, modeling, worked examples, common mistakes, guided practice, independent practice, and mastery verification for remedial, standard, and accelerated learners. Under that model, benchmark density must be evaluated more conservatively than it would be in a teacher-led course.

### Unit 1 Workload

Unit 1 has a low primary benchmark count but a high instructional importance. `MLA.ENG1.R.10` and `MLA.ENG1.V.01` are support standards that enable success across the entire course. Although the formal count is light, the unit must establish paraphrase, academic vocabulary, evidence habits, and basic independent learning routines.

Workload judgment: acceptable, provided Unit 1 does not become a general orientation unit and does include real reading and evidence-based response practice.

### Unit 2 Workload

Unit 2 has seven primary standards, all classified as mastery standards. Six are reading standards, and one is a writing standard. This is the heaviest unit by mastery-standard count.

Workload judgment: high risk. It can fit only if future mapping combines related literary craft skills and avoids treating each benchmark as a fully separate instructional arc.

### Unit 3 Workload

Unit 3 has six primary standards, all mastery standards. It combines informational analysis, rhetoric, argument evaluation, rhetorical explanation, and argument writing. The reading-to-writing transfer demand is substantial.

Workload judgment: high risk. It can fit only if future mapping sequences informational reading before argument writing and uses shared texts/examples across related skills.

### Unit 4 Workload

Unit 4 has three primary mastery standards, but the cognitive load is higher than the count suggests. Research, expository writing, and collaborative publication each require multi-step instruction.

Workload judgment: moderate-high risk. The unit is feasible if research and expository writing are treated as one connected source-based communication process.

### Unit 5 Workload

Unit 5 has four primary standards: two mastery and two support. The workload is manageable by count, but the instructional design challenge is significant because language, vocabulary, and revision must be taught through authentic reading and writing rather than isolated drills.

Workload judgment: manageable with integration risk.

### Unit 6 Workload

Unit 6 has two primary mastery standards. The primary count is manageable, but the unit depends heavily on spiraled preparation: evidence, research, vocabulary, revision, discussion, and synthesis must already be familiar.

Workload judgment: manageable if communication is reinforced earlier.

## Unit 2 Deep Analysis

Current Unit 2 standards:

- `MLA.ENG1.R.01` Analyze Literary Elements
- `MLA.ENG1.R.02` Analyze Theme Development
- `MLA.ENG1.R.03` Analyze Narrator Perspective
- `MLA.ENG1.R.04` Analyze Epic Poetry
- `MLA.ENG1.R.09` Explain Figurative Language and Mood
- `MLA.ENG1.R.11` Compare Literary Adaptations
- `MLA.ENG1.W.01` Write Narrative Texts

### Realism Within 8 Lessons

Unit 2 is possible within 8 lessons, but only with careful consolidation. The workload is not realistic if each standard receives a separate full lesson plus independent mastery application. MLA lessons require explicit instruction, modeling, worked examples, common mistakes, guided practice, independent practice, and mastery verification; seven mastery standards inside eight lessons leaves almost no margin.

### Mastery Density

Unit 2 contains seven mastery standards and zero support-only standards. This is the highest mastery density in the course.

The workload concern is amplified because literary analysis skills are often difficult for remedial and standard learners to perform independently. Students must not merely identify literary elements; they must explain how elements develop meaning, style, theme, perspective, irony, satire, mood, and adaptation.

### Standards Requiring the Most Instructional Time

| Standard | Time Demand | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| `MLA.ENG1.R.02` Analyze Theme Development | Very high | Theme development requires tracking abstract ideas across a text and supporting claims with evidence. |
| `MLA.ENG1.R.03` Analyze Narrator Perspective | Very high | Perspective, irony, and satire require explicit modeling and multiple examples. |
| `MLA.ENG1.R.04` Analyze Epic Poetry | High | Epic poetry brings genre-specific structure, characters, themes, and unfamiliar conventions. |
| `MLA.ENG1.R.11` Compare Literary Adaptations | High | Comparative analysis requires students to read across versions and explain adaptation choices. |
| `MLA.ENG1.W.01` Write Narrative Texts | High | Writing production requires instruction in point of view, transitions, techniques, and coherence. |

### Standards That Naturally Combine

- `MLA.ENG1.R.01` and `MLA.ENG1.R.02` naturally combine because literary elements often support theme development.
- `MLA.ENG1.R.03` and `MLA.ENG1.R.09` naturally combine when narrator perspective, diction, figurative language, and mood shape interpretation.
- `MLA.ENG1.R.04` and `MLA.ENG1.R.11` may combine if epic/classical source material is connected to adaptation analysis.
- `MLA.ENG1.W.01` can reinforce `MLA.ENG1.R.03` by having students understand narrative point of view before producing narrative writing, but future mapping must avoid turning this into a full creative-writing detour.

### Overload Risks

- Too many distinct literary genres may be introduced at once.
- Epic poetry may require more background support than the unit can afford.
- Narrative writing could crowd out literary analysis if treated as a full independent writing unit.
- Theme, perspective, and adaptation each require evidence-based explanation, which can overwhelm students if paraphrase and quotation integration are weak.
- Remedial learners may need substantial modeling to distinguish summary from analysis.

### Unit 2 Workload Finding

Unit 2 is the highest workload concern in the crosswalk. It should proceed to Phase 2B validation with a warning that future mapping must consolidate standards around shared texts and carefully scaffold analysis before independent application.

## Unit 3 Deep Analysis

Current Unit 3 standards:

- `MLA.ENG1.R.05` Analyze Text Structure
- `MLA.ENG1.R.06` Evaluate Central Idea Support
- `MLA.ENG1.R.07` Analyze Author Purpose and Rhetoric
- `MLA.ENG1.R.08` Evaluate Opposing Arguments
- `MLA.ENG1.R.12` Explain Rhetorical Choices
- `MLA.ENG1.W.02` Write Arguments with Evidence

### Realism Within 8 Lessons

Unit 3 is possible within 8 lessons, but it is a high-risk unit. It contains six mastery standards, each with substantial cognitive demand. The unit must move from informational reading to rhetorical analysis to argument evaluation to argument writing. That progression is sound, but time-intensive for independent learners.

### Mastery Density

Unit 3 contains six mastery standards and zero support-only standards. It is slightly lighter than Unit 2 by count, but its reading-to-writing transfer demand may make it equally difficult.

### Standards Requiring the Most Instructional Time

| Standard | Time Demand | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| `MLA.ENG1.R.08` Evaluate Opposing Arguments | Very high | Students must compare arguments, evaluate claims, judge validity, and explain effectiveness. |
| `MLA.ENG1.W.02` Write Arguments with Evidence | Very high | Students must write claims, use reasoning, integrate credible evidence, rebut counterclaims, organize logically, and maintain tone. |
| `MLA.ENG1.R.07` Analyze Author Purpose and Rhetoric | High | Rhetorical appeals and figurative language require explicit modeling across nonfiction texts. |
| `MLA.ENG1.R.12` Explain Rhetorical Choices | High | Students must move beyond identification to explanation of rhetorical effect. |
| `MLA.ENG1.R.06` Evaluate Central Idea Support | High | Evaluating support requires evidence quality judgments, not just identifying details. |

### Standards That Naturally Combine

- `MLA.ENG1.R.05` and `MLA.ENG1.R.06` naturally combine because structure and support both contribute to informational meaning.
- `MLA.ENG1.R.07` and `MLA.ENG1.R.12` naturally combine because both focus on author purpose, rhetoric, and effect.
- `MLA.ENG1.R.08` and `MLA.ENG1.W.02` naturally connect because students should analyze arguments before writing arguments.
- `MLA.ENG1.R.06`, `MLA.ENG1.R.08`, and `MLA.ENG1.W.02` can share evidence-quality instruction.

### Overload Risks

- Argument writing may be introduced before students can evaluate argument quality.
- Rhetorical appeals, author purpose, rhetorical choices, and figurative language may blur unless terms are explicitly separated and modeled.
- Students may confuse central idea support with argument evidence.
- Counterclaim and rebuttal instruction can become too compressed.
- Independent learners may need many worked examples to distinguish valid evidence, relevant evidence, credible evidence, and logical reasoning.

### Unit 3 Workload Finding

Unit 3 is a high workload concern. It remains feasible only if future mapping sequences reading before writing, combines rhetoric standards efficiently, and uses repeated modeled analysis before students write full arguments independently.

## Communication Analysis

Communication standards reviewed:

- `MLA.ENG1.C.01` Present Ideas Orally
- `MLA.ENG1.C.02` Create Digital Presentations
- `MLA.ENG1.C.03` Publish Collaborative Writing

### Primary Placement Sufficiency

Unit 6 is sufficient as the primary placement for `MLA.ENG1.C.01` and `MLA.ENG1.C.02` if it functions as the culminating application unit. These standards require students to present information, organize ideas, use credible evidence, create a clear perspective, and communicate digitally.

`MLA.ENG1.C.03` has primary placement in Unit 4 in the current crosswalk draft because publication-ready collaborative writing is tied to research and expository communication. This is workload-reasonable because it avoids overloading Unit 6 with all communication standards.

### Earlier Reinforcement Need

Communication must receive earlier reinforcement. Unit 6 should not be the first meaningful exposure to oral explanation, discussion, audience awareness, or digital communication.

Earlier reinforcement should occur through:

- Evidence-based discussion in Unit 1.
- Literary interpretation discussion in Unit 2.
- Claims/evidence discussion in Unit 3.
- Collaborative source-based writing and publication awareness in Unit 4.
- Feedback and revision communication in Unit 5.

### Spiral Plan Adequacy

The current spiral plan is adequate as a draft but requires validation. It provides broad exposure for `MLA.ENG1.C.01`, targeted exposure for `MLA.ENG1.C.02`, and publication/revision reinforcement for `MLA.ENG1.C.03`.

The main risk is implementation: if future mapping treats communication as only a final presentation task, Unit 6 workload becomes too steep for independent learners.

## Self-Paced Learning Analysis

### Most Difficult Units for Independent Learners

| Unit | Difficulty Level | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Unit 2 | Very high | Literary analysis requires abstract interpretation, evidence, figurative language, perspective, genre knowledge, and narrative craft. |
| Unit 3 | Very high | Informational analysis, rhetoric, argument evaluation, and argument writing require many connected reasoning steps. |
| Unit 4 | High | Research requires source evaluation, synthesis, organization, and expository writing without daily teacher guidance. |
| Unit 5 | Moderate-high | Grammar, vocabulary, and revision transfer only if students can apply concepts to authentic writing. |
| Unit 6 | Moderate-high | Presentation and digital communication require synthesis of prior skills. |
| Unit 1 | Moderate | Foundational skills are manageable but essential for later independence. |

### Units Requiring the Most Scaffolding

Unit 2 requires the most literary-analysis scaffolding:

- How to move from observation to inference.
- How to explain theme development.
- How to identify narrator perspective and its effect.
- How to interpret irony, satire, figurative language, and mood.
- How to compare adaptations.

Unit 3 requires the most reasoning scaffolding:

- How to identify claims and evidence.
- How to evaluate validity.
- How to distinguish central idea support from argumentative support.
- How to analyze rhetoric.
- How to write counterclaims and rebuttals.

Unit 4 requires procedural scaffolding:

- How to develop and refine a research question.
- How to evaluate source reliability and validity.
- How to synthesize rather than summarize.
- How to organize source-based explanation.

### Units Requiring the Most Examples and Modeling

| Unit | Required Modeling Emphasis |
|---|---|
| Unit 2 | Annotated literary analysis, theme tracking, perspective analysis, figurative-language interpretation, adaptation comparison, narrative craft models |
| Unit 3 | Annotated informational texts, rhetorical analysis models, argument comparison charts, claim/evidence/reasoning examples, counterclaim/rebuttal models |
| Unit 4 | Research-question refinement examples, source evaluation models, note-taking examples, synthesis examples, expository organization models |
| Unit 5 | Sentence revision models, vocabulary-in-context examples, common grammar errors, before/after writing revisions |
| Unit 6 | Presentation organization models, digital presentation examples, audience-awareness examples, credible-evidence presentation models |

## Overload Risks

1. Unit 2 has seven mastery standards in one 8-lesson unit.
2. Unit 3 has six mastery standards with heavy reading-to-writing transfer.
3. Unit 4 may be undercounted because research and expository writing are multi-step processes.
4. Unit 5 may seem moderate by count but can fail if language and vocabulary are isolated from authentic reading and writing.
5. Unit 6 is manageable only if communication has earlier reinforcement.
6. Support standards may require more time than their classification suggests because MLA lessons must still teach and verify them directly.
7. Remedial learners will require more examples and guided practice than a traditional pacing model may assume.

## Recommendations

These recommendations do not modify the crosswalk.

1. Flag Unit 2 and Unit 3 for focused Phase 2B validation.
2. In Phase 2B, review whether Unit 2 standards can be combined around shared literary texts without weakening mastery evidence.
3. In Phase 2B, review whether Unit 3 can sequence informational reading, rhetoric, argument evaluation, and argument writing without compressing writing instruction.
4. Treat Unit 4 workload as moderate-high despite its lower benchmark count because research requires explicit procedural instruction.
5. Require earlier communication reinforcement before Unit 6 in future mapping.
6. Require language, vocabulary, revision, paraphrase, evidence, and discussion to spiral in future mapping.
7. Preserve the approved 6-unit, 8-lesson structure, but validate workload carefully before authorizing lesson development.

## Final Decision

**WORKLOAD CONCERNS IDENTIFIED**

The English I Crosswalk Draft can potentially fit within 6 units and 8 lessons per unit, but only with disciplined future mapping and strong instructional consolidation. Unit 2 and Unit 3 are the primary workload risks. The crosswalk should proceed to Phase 2B validation with explicit attention to benchmark density, mastery evidence, and self-paced learner support.
