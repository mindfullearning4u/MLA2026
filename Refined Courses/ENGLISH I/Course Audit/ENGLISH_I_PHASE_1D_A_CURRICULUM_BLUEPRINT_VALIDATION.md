## Executive Summary

The English I blueprint is valid for progression into Phase 1D-B: MLA English Instructional Design Standards.

Validation status: APPROVED WITH REVISIONS

The six-unit progression is logical, standards-aligned, and appropriate for Florida B.E.S.T. Grade 9 English I. The sequence moves from foundational comprehension and evidence use to literary analysis, informational/rhetorical analysis, research and expository writing, language/vocabulary/revision refinement, and final synthesis through oral and digital communication.

The blueprint does not show benchmark conflicts. The major hidden risk is not structural; it is implementation-based. Several support benchmarks must spiral throughout the course even though Unit 5 remains the dedicated language, vocabulary, style, and revision unit. If conventions, vocabulary, and revision are delayed until Unit 5, the course would weaken SAT/ACT readiness, writing development, and mastery pacing. Therefore, Unit 5 should remain dedicated, but language, vocabulary, and revision must also be reinforced across all six units.

The cluster model is sound after the Phase 1C-A correction that added the Narrative Writing and Literary Craft Application cluster. No cluster needs to be merged or removed. One cluster should be monitored during future crosswalk development: Foundational Reading and Academic Response overlaps with Language, Vocabulary, and Revision because ELA.9.C.3.1 functions both as an early foundation and an all-course support skill. This overlap is appropriate and should be preserved as a spiral, not treated as duplication.

Final recommendation:

APPROVED WITH REVISIONS for progression into Phase 1D-B, with the required revision that Phase 1D-B instructional design standards explicitly require spiraling of language, vocabulary, revision, paraphrase, evidence, and academic discussion across all units.

### Progression Logic

Finding: The progression is logical.

The sequence follows a defensible instructional build:

1. Students first learn to read accurately, paraphrase, use evidence, and control basic academic language.
2. Students then apply those foundations to literary meaning, craft, theme, perspective, figurative language, adaptation, and narrative writing.
3. Students transfer analytical habits to informational text, rhetoric, claims, opposing arguments, evidence, validity, and argument writing.
4. Students apply evidence and source evaluation to research and expository writing.
5. Students refine language, vocabulary, style, and revision after generating enough writing to revise meaningfully.
6. Students synthesize reading, writing, research, language, and vocabulary through oral and digital communication.

Validation judgment: Strong.

### Hidden Gaps

Finding: No fatal gaps are present, but several implementation gaps must be controlled in Phase 1D-B.

Potential hidden gaps:

- Oral and digital communication could be underdeveloped if Unit 6 is the first substantial presentation exposure.

Required Phase 1D-B control:

## Unit 5 Decision

### Unit Under Review

Unit 5: Language, Vocabulary, Style, and Revision

Decision options:

- Option A: Maintain Unit 5 as a dedicated unit.
- Option B: Distribute Language, Vocabulary, and Revision throughout all six units.

### Option A: Maintain Unit 5 as a Dedicated Unit

Advantages:

- Provides a clear mastery home for the Language, Vocabulary, and Revision cluster.
- Gives students a concentrated refinement point after they have written in multiple modes.
- Supports self-paced learning because students can receive explicit remediation and mastery work on conventions, vocabulary, clarity, cohesion, and style before final synthesis.
- Strengthens final communication quality before Unit 6 presentation and digital communication.
- Creates a natural location for revision across narrative, literary analysis, argument, expository, and research writing.
- Supports SAT Writing and ACT English readiness through focused attention to conventions, precision, cohesion, and sentence-level control.

Disadvantages:

- Could be misread as permission to delay grammar, vocabulary, and revision until late in the course.
- Could become disconnected grammar/vocabulary work if not anchored in authentic reading and writing.
- Could feel less text-rich unless style, diction, syntax, and vocabulary are studied in real literary and informational texts.
- Could overload students if Unit 5 attempts to remediate all prior language and writing issues for the first time.

Benchmark coverage impact:

Option A supports direct mastery of:

- ELA.9.C.1.5
- ELA.9.C.3.1
- ELA.9.V.1.2
- ELA.9.V.1.3

It also reinforces:

- ELA.9.V.1.1
- ELA.9.R.3.1
- ELA.9.R.3.4
- ELA.9.C.1.2
- ELA.9.C.1.3
- ELA.9.C.1.4

Student learning impact:

Positive if Unit 5 is a refinement unit built on prior writing. Students can see language and vocabulary as tools for improving meaning, precision, tone, and cohesion.

Self-paced learning impact:

Positive. A dedicated unit provides a clear checkpoint for students who need targeted remediation before synthesis and presentation.

Assessment impact:

Positive for future assessment design because Unit 5 can validate language, vocabulary, and revision mastery through applied writing and editing evidence. No assessments are created in this phase.

Mastery impact:

Strong if spiraled earlier. Weak if treated as first exposure.

### Option B: Distribute Language, Vocabulary, and Revision Throughout All Six Units

Advantages:

- Reflects how language, vocabulary, and revision actually function in reading and writing.
- Prevents late-course delay of conventions and vocabulary.
- Supports continuous SAT/ACT readiness.
- Keeps writing improvement active across the course.
- Reduces the risk that Unit 5 becomes isolated grammar practice.

Disadvantages:

- Does not provide a clear concentrated mastery point for a high-need support cluster.
- May dilute explicit instruction if every unit assumes language and revision will happen informally.
- Makes self-paced remediation harder because students may not encounter a focused sequence of language and revision expectations.
- Could make future crosswalk and mastery evidence less clear.
- Could weaken final preparation for Unit 6 if no dedicated refinement occurs before synthesis and presentation.

Benchmark coverage impact:

Option B supports recurring reinforcement of:

- ELA.9.C.3.1
- ELA.9.C.1.5
- ELA.9.V.1.1
- ELA.9.V.1.2
- ELA.9.V.1.3

However, it risks weaker direct mastery visibility unless Phase 2 crosswalk and future assessment planning are very precise.

Student learning impact:

Positive for transfer, but potentially weaker for students who need explicit language intervention.

Self-paced learning impact:

Mixed. Distributed support helps continuous learning, but it can make remediation less visible and less efficient.

Assessment impact:

Mixed. Distributed evidence can be authentic, but it may be harder to verify complete mastery without a dedicated unit focus. No assessments are created in this phase.

Mastery impact:

Strong for reinforcement, weaker for direct mastery unless paired with a dedicated focus.

### Recommended Option

Recommendation: Option A, maintain Unit 5 as a dedicated unit, with required spiraling across all six units.

Rationale:

This is not a structural change. It validates the approved blueprint while clarifying implementation requirements. Unit 5 should remain the dedicated mastery and refinement point for language, vocabulary, style, and revision. However, Phase 1D-B must require distributed reinforcement in every unit so Unit 5 is not the first exposure.

The strongest model is:

- Units 1-4: introduce and reinforce language, vocabulary, and revision in context.
- Unit 5: concentrate, deepen, remediate, and validate mastery of language, vocabulary, style, and revision.
- Unit 6: apply refined language and vocabulary in oral and digital communication.

Validation judgment: Unit 5 is approved as dedicated, with mandatory spiraling.

### Incorrectly Grouped Clusters

Finding: No cluster is incorrectly grouped.

Rationale:

- Foundational skills are correctly grouped because paraphrase, academic vocabulary, and conventions support all later work.
- Literary analysis benchmarks share interpretation of craft, theme, perspective, language, epic structure, and adaptation.
- Informational text and rhetoric benchmarks share structure, central idea, purpose, support, and rhetorical choices.
- Argument reading and argument writing are correctly linked because writing arguments depends on evaluating arguments.
- Narrative writing is correctly separated as a distinct writing mode while still conceptually paired with literary craft.
- Research and expository communication are correctly paired because both require source use, synthesis, explanation, organization, and audience awareness.
- Language, vocabulary, and revision are correctly grouped as communication-precision skills.
- Oral and digital communication are correctly grouped because both require organization, evidence, coherent perspective, and audience awareness.

### Missing Clusters

Finding: No required cluster is missing after adding Narrative Writing and Literary Craft Application in Phase 1C-A.

Validation note:

The earlier omission of narrative writing from the cluster list has been corrected. The current cluster model accounts for all 24 English I benchmarks.

### Merge Review

Finding: No clusters should merge.

Rationale:

- Merging Literary Analysis and Craft with Narrative Writing would obscure that narrative writing is a distinct production benchmark.
- Merging Informational Text and Rhetoric with Argument would overload one cluster and hide the difference between nonfiction analysis and argument production.
- Merging Research with Argument would blur the difference between evaluating claims and conducting research to explain information.
- Merging Oral/Digital Communication with Research would hide performance communication expectations.

### Split Review

Finding: No cluster requires an immediate split for blueprint validation.

Potential future crosswalk watchpoints:

- Language, Vocabulary, and Revision may need subcategories in Phase 2 because it includes conventions, revision, etymology, context clues, figurative language, connotation, and denotation.
- Informational Text and Rhetoric may need subcategories for central idea/support, structure/features, and rhetoric.
- Literary Analysis and Craft may need subcategories for theme, narrator perspective, epic poetry, figurative language, and adaptation.

Validation judgment: Cluster model approved.
