# MLA English Instructional Design Standards

## Phase 1D-B Official Standards Document

## Scope Control

This document establishes official MLA English instructional design standards for future English curriculum development.

These standards apply to:

- English I
- English II
- English III
- English IV
- Future MLA English courses

This document does not create lessons, assessments, crosswalks, mappings, unit files, HTML files, quizzes, GIFT files, or curriculum content.

This document defines standards that future curriculum developers must follow.

## Source Documents

These standards are based on:

- Approved Phase 1B Architecture Analysis
- Approved Phase 1C-A Benchmark Analysis and Clustering
- Approved Phase 1C-B Curriculum Blueprint
- Approved Phase 1D-A Blueprint Validation

## Executive Summary

MLA English courses must be designed for independent student mastery in an online, mastery-based academy. The curriculum itself is the primary instructional delivery mechanism. The Teacher of Record monitors progress, grades work, provides feedback, intervenes, coaches, and supports students, but is not the primary source of first instruction.

Therefore, every English lesson must teach clearly enough for a student to learn the skill without live teacher explanation. Lessons must be explicit, detailed, sequential, scaffolded, model-rich, and mastery-focused. They must serve remedial, standard, and accelerated learners without assuming strong reading skills, strong writing skills, prior mastery, parent help, background knowledge beyond prerequisites, or familiarity with academic vocabulary.

The core MLA English lesson design sequence is:

1. Teach the concept.
2. Model the concept.
3. Show worked examples.
4. Show common mistakes.
5. Provide guided practice.
6. Provide independent application.
7. Verify mastery.

Future English curriculum must teach complex reading, writing, research, language, vocabulary, revision, and communication skills in small, visible steps before asking students to demonstrate mastery. Students must never be asked to analyze, write, research, revise, or present before the curriculum has taught them how.

Unit 5 remains the dedicated Language, Vocabulary, Style, and Revision unit in the English I blueprint. However, language, vocabulary, style, revision, paraphrase, evidence use, and academic discussion must spiral across all units and all English courses.

## 1. Self-Directed Learning Standards

### 1.1 Core Requirement

Every MLA English lesson must be learnable by an independent student using the lesson materials alone.

This means each lesson must include:

- Clear purpose.
- Explicit skill instruction.
- Definitions of academic and content vocabulary.
- Step-by-step explanation of the thinking process.
- Modeled examples.
- Annotated examples.
- Common mistake warnings.
- Guided practice before independent work.
- Mastery checks that verify the target skill.
- Directions that tell students exactly what to do and why.

### 1.2 What Students Need to Learn Independently

Students need:

- A clear statement of what they are learning.
- A plain-language explanation of why the skill matters.
- Prerequisite concepts reviewed before new skill demands.
- New terms defined before use.
- Examples before application.
- Models that show both product and process.
- Prompts broken into steps.
- Visible thinking: how to choose evidence, interpret meaning, organize writing, revise, or evaluate a source.
- Checks for understanding before high-stakes application.
- Clear criteria for success.
- Opportunities to revise, self-correct, and resubmit when needed.

### 1.3 Required Support Structures Inside Lessons

Every English lesson must include support structures appropriate to the target skill:

- Vocabulary boxes for new academic terms.
- Skill breakdowns for complex tasks.
- Reading supports such as guiding questions, annotation prompts, paragraph summaries, or structure maps.
- Writing supports such as sentence frames, planning charts, paragraph models, and revision checklists.
- Research supports such as source evaluation checklists and note-taking models.
- Visual supports such as diagrams, flowcharts, text structure maps, argument maps, or evidence charts.
- Common mistake examples that show what weak work looks like and why it is weak.
- Mastery criteria written in student-facing language.

### 1.4 Required Level of Explanation

Lessons must explain not only what to do, but how and why to do it.

For example:

- Do not only say "analyze the theme." Teach what theme is, how it develops, how to find evidence, and how to explain the connection.
- Do not only say "use credible sources." Teach what credibility means, how to check it, and how to reject weak sources.
- Do not only say "revise for clarity." Teach how to identify unclear wording, weak transitions, missing explanation, vague pronouns, and unsupported claims.

### 1.5 Required Level of Modeling

Every major skill must be modeled before students apply it independently.

Models must show:

- The completed example.
- The thinking process used to create it.
- Why the example meets expectations.
- How evidence or language choices support the purpose.
- What would make the example weaker.

For writing, models must include annotated samples that label structure, evidence, explanation, transitions, tone, and revision choices.

For reading, models must include annotated text or excerpt analysis showing how a reader moves from observation to inference to explanation.

### 1.6 Required Level of Scaffolding

Scaffolding must begin with high support and gradually remove support.

Minimum scaffold sequence:

1. Define the concept.
2. Show the concept in a simple example.
3. Model how to apply the concept.
4. Let students complete part of the task with guidance.
5. Let students complete a similar task independently.
6. Ask students to explain or reflect on how they completed the task.

Complex skills must be broken into smaller components. A lesson may not ask students to complete a full essay, full research task, full analysis, or full presentation unless the component skills have already been taught and practiced.

### 1.7 Preventing Students from Becoming Lost

Lessons must prevent confusion by:

- Using consistent terminology.
- Stating each step in order.
- Avoiding large unexplained jumps.
- Explaining where students are in the learning process.
- Using short checks after each major explanation.
- Providing examples before practice.
- Warning students about common errors.
- Providing enough context for texts, tasks, and vocabulary.
- Keeping directions specific and observable.

### 1.8 Remedial Learner Support

Lessons must support remedial learners by:

- Reviewing prerequisites.
- Defining all essential academic vocabulary.
- Breaking tasks into small steps.
- Providing multiple examples.
- Including sentence frames or response starters where appropriate.
- Modeling how to think through the task.
- Providing common mistake examples.
- Giving guided practice before independent practice.
- Using clear visual supports.
- Allowing revision and resubmission when mastery is not yet shown.

### 1.9 Standard Learner Support

Lessons must support standard learners by:

- Providing grade-level text and tasks.
- Teaching the full target skill explicitly.
- Providing enough practice to build confidence.
- Requiring independent application after modeling.
- Making mastery criteria clear.
- Building toward increasing complexity across the course.

### 1.10 Accelerated Learner Support

Lessons must support accelerated learners by:

- Avoiding unnecessary barriers or excessive repetition once mastery is demonstrated.
- Providing efficient pathways through review material.
- Including optional depth, challenge, or extension where appropriate.
- Allowing students to move from guided work to independent application when ready.
- Maintaining rigor through complex texts, synthesis, precision, and independent reasoning.

Accelerated support must not remove required instruction or required mastery evidence.

## 2. Student Population Design Standards

### 2.1 Non-Assumption Standard

MLA English lessons must not assume:

- Prior mastery of the target skill.
- Teacher explanation.
- Parent assistance.
- Strong reading skills.
- Strong writing skills.
- Familiarity with academic vocabulary.
- Background knowledge beyond prerequisite standards.
- Ability to infer task expectations from vague directions.
- Ability to write essays without explicit instruction.
- Ability to conduct research without explicit instruction.
- Ability to revise meaningfully without models.

### 2.2 Remedial Learners

For remedial learners, every lesson must include:

- Plain-language explanation.
- Vocabulary support.
- Short chunks of instruction.
- Modeled thinking.
- Guided examples.
- Practice with feedback opportunities.
- Clear success criteria.
- Support for self-correction.

Remedial support must preserve grade-level expectations. It should scaffold access to mastery, not lower the target skill.

### 2.3 Standard Learners

For standard learners, every lesson must include:

- Grade-level content.
- Clear instruction.
- Structured practice.
- Independent application.
- Mastery checks aligned to the target skill.
- Opportunities to revise and improve.

### 2.4 Accelerated Learners

For accelerated learners, lessons should include:

- Efficient review.
- Opportunities for deeper analysis.
- More complex text or synthesis when appropriate.
- Less repetitive support once mastery is evident.
- Clear paths to independent mastery.

Accelerated students still need explicit expectations and clear mastery criteria.

## 3. Reading Instruction Standards

### 3.1 General Reading Standards

Reading lessons must teach students how to read actively and analytically.

Every reading skill lesson must include:

- Definition of the target skill.
- Explanation of why the skill matters.
- Short model text or excerpt.
- Think-aloud or visible reasoning model.
- Annotated example.
- Guided practice with prompts.
- Independent application.
- Evidence-based mastery check.

### 3.2 Literary Analysis

Must be explicitly taught:

- Difference between summary and analysis.
- How authors create meaning through choices.
- How to identify key elements such as character, setting, conflict, structure, perspective, mood, and theme.
- How to connect textual evidence to interpretation.
- How to explain layered meaning or style.

Must be modeled:

- Moving from observation to inference to explanation.
- Selecting evidence that proves an interpretation.
- Explaining how a literary choice affects meaning.

Examples required:

- Strong literary analysis.
- Weak summary-only response.
- Annotated paragraph showing claim, evidence, and explanation.
- Common mistake example where evidence is quoted but not explained.

Scaffolding required:

- Identify the element.
- Find evidence.
- Ask what the detail suggests.
- Explain how the detail affects meaning.
- Connect to theme, character, perspective, or style.

Guided practice required:

- Short excerpts with targeted analysis prompts.
- Partial explanations students complete.
- Evidence-matching activities.

Independent application:

- Students independently explain how a literary element contributes to meaning or style using evidence.

### 3.3 Informational Text Analysis

Must be explicitly taught:

- Central idea.
- Supporting details.
- Text structure.
- Text features.
- Author purpose.
- Relationship between structure, evidence, and meaning.

Must be modeled:

- How to identify central idea.
- How to trace development across a text.
- How to evaluate whether details support the central idea.
- How structure helps the author communicate.

Examples required:

- Annotated informational paragraph.
- Central idea and support chart.
- Text structure example.
- Weak central idea statement and corrected version.

Scaffolding required:

- Identify topic.
- Identify repeated ideas.
- Determine central idea.
- Locate supporting details.
- Evaluate support.
- Explain structure and purpose.

Guided practice required:

- Short passages with central idea prompts.
- Structure identification.
- Evidence evaluation.

Independent application:

- Students independently analyze how structure, features, and support develop meaning or purpose.

### 3.4 Theme

Must be explicitly taught:

- Theme is a message or insight, not a topic.
- Universal theme applies beyond one text.
- Theme develops through character, conflict, choices, consequences, setting, imagery, and recurring ideas.

Must be modeled:

- Turning a topic into a theme statement.
- Tracking theme development across a text.
- Explaining evidence without overgeneralizing.

Examples required:

- Topic versus theme examples.
- Weak theme statement and revised theme statement.
- Theme development chart.

Scaffolding required:

- Identify repeated topic.
- Identify what characters learn or reveal.
- Identify conflict and consequences.
- Draft a theme statement.
- Support it with evidence.
- Explain development.

Guided practice required:

- Theme statement revision.
- Evidence sorting.
- Short theme analysis.

Independent application:

- Students independently analyze a universal theme and its development.

### 3.5 Character

Must be explicitly taught:

- Direct and indirect characterization.
- Character motivation.
- Character change.
- Relationship between character, conflict, structure, and theme.

Must be modeled:

- Inferring character traits from actions, dialogue, thoughts, and interactions.
- Explaining how character development supports theme.

Examples required:

- Character evidence chart.
- Annotated character analysis paragraph.
- Common mistake: naming a trait without evidence.

Scaffolding required:

- Identify action or dialogue.
- Infer trait or motivation.
- Explain significance.
- Connect to conflict or theme.

Independent application:

- Students independently explain how character contributes to meaning.

### 3.6 Perspective

Must be explicitly taught:

- Narrator perspective.
- Point of view.
- Reliability.
- Bias.
- How perspective shapes information, irony, satire, tone, and meaning.

Must be modeled:

- Identifying who tells the story.
- Noticing what the narrator knows, withholds, misunderstands, or emphasizes.
- Explaining how perspective affects the reader.

Examples required:

- Same event from different perspectives.
- Reliable versus unreliable narration.
- Irony or satire created by perspective.

Scaffolding required:

- Identify narrator or speaker.
- Identify what the narrator knows or believes.
- Identify reader effect.
- Explain author purpose.

Independent application:

- Students independently analyze how perspective influences meaning.

### 3.7 Central Idea

Must be explicitly taught:

- Difference between topic, main idea, and central idea.
- How central ideas develop through supporting details and structure.
- How to evaluate support.

Must be modeled:

- Finding central idea across a full passage.
- Distinguishing important support from minor details.
- Explaining support quality.

Examples required:

- Topic/main idea/central idea comparison.
- Annotated central idea paragraph.
- Weak central idea example and corrected version.

Independent application:

- Students independently evaluate how an author supports central ideas.

### 3.8 Text Structure

Must be explicitly taught:

- Common structures: chronology, compare/contrast, cause/effect, problem/solution, claim/support, description, sequence.
- How structure affects meaning and purpose.
- How text features support structure.

Must be modeled:

- Identifying signal words.
- Mapping structure.
- Explaining why the structure fits the author's purpose.

Examples required:

- Structure chart.
- Annotated passage showing structure.
- Common mistake: naming structure without explaining effect.

Independent application:

- Students independently analyze how structure or features convey meaning or purpose.

### 3.9 Rhetoric

Must be explicitly taught:

- Rhetoric as purposeful language.
- Ethos, pathos, logos where appropriate.
- Audience, purpose, tone, appeals, repetition, diction, figurative language, and emphasis.
- Difference between identifying a device and explaining its effect.

Must be modeled:

- How to identify rhetorical choices.
- How to connect rhetoric to author purpose.
- How to explain impact on audience.

Examples required:

- Annotated speech or argument excerpt.
- Rhetorical device/effect chart.
- Weak response that lists devices without analysis.

Independent application:

- Students independently explain an author's use of rhetoric.

### 3.10 Evidence Analysis

Must be explicitly taught:

- Evidence must be relevant, accurate, sufficient, and explained.
- Evidence can support central idea, theme, claim, inference, or research finding.
- Quoting evidence is not the same as analyzing evidence.

Must be modeled:

- Selecting strong evidence.
- Rejecting weak evidence.
- Explaining how evidence supports a claim.

Examples required:

- Strong and weak evidence comparison.
- Evidence-explanation model.
- Common mistake: dropped quote.

Independent application:

- Students independently select and explain evidence for reading and writing tasks.

## 4. Writing Instruction Standards

### 4.1 General Writing Standards

Writing lessons must teach writing as a process, not assign writing as a product.

Every major writing lesson must include:

- Purpose and audience.
- Structure of the writing type.
- Planning process.
- Model response.
- Annotated exemplar.
- Common mistake example.
- Guided writing.
- Independent writing.
- Revision step.
- Mastery criteria.

### 4.2 Text-Based Responses

Must be explicitly taught:

- Restating or answering the prompt.
- Making a clear claim or answer.
- Selecting relevant evidence.
- Explaining evidence.
- Using academic vocabulary.
- Writing in complete sentences.

Must be modeled:

- Claim-evidence-explanation structure.
- How to avoid summary-only responses.
- How to embed or introduce evidence.

Exemplars required:

- Strong short response.
- Weak response with missing explanation.
- Annotated response.

Scaffolding required:

- Prompt breakdown.
- Evidence selection chart.
- Sentence frames early in learning.
- Explanation stems.

Guided writing:

- Students complete parts of a response before writing independently.

Independent writing:

- Students write a complete text-based response using evidence and explanation.

### 4.3 Literary Analysis Writing

Must be explicitly taught:

- Difference between literary summary and literary analysis.
- Analytical claim.
- Evidence selection.
- Explanation of author choice.
- Connection to theme, character, perspective, mood, or structure.

Must be modeled:

- How to build an analysis paragraph.
- How to explain evidence in relation to meaning.
- How to revise vague analysis.

Exemplars required:

- Annotated literary analysis paragraph.
- Weak summary paragraph and improved analysis version.
- Evidence explanation model.

Independent writing:

- Students write literary analysis that explains how textual evidence supports interpretation.

### 4.4 Narrative Writing

Must be explicitly taught:

- Point of view.
- Narrative sequence.
- Character, conflict, setting, and event development.
- Narrative techniques such as dialogue, pacing, description, reflection, and transitions.
- Purposeful detail.

Must be modeled:

- How point of view shapes a narrative.
- How transitions guide sequence.
- How details develop character or conflict.

Exemplars required:

- Annotated narrative excerpt.
- Weak narrative with unclear point of view.
- Revised narrative section.

Independent writing:

- Students write a narrative with clear point of view, narrative techniques, and varied transitions.

### 4.5 Informative/Explanatory Writing

Must be explicitly taught:

- Topic and controlling idea.
- Logical organization.
- Use of multiple sources.
- Explanation versus listing facts.
- Transitions.
- Formal tone.
- Source integration.

Must be modeled:

- Planning an explanatory response.
- Combining information from sources.
- Explaining evidence.
- Maintaining objective tone.

Exemplars required:

- Annotated explanatory paragraph or short essay.
- Source integration example.
- Weak fact-list response and improved version.

Independent writing:

- Students write expository text that explains and analyzes information from multiple sources.

### 4.6 Argument Writing

Must be explicitly taught:

- Claim.
- Reasoning.
- Credible evidence.
- Counterclaim.
- Rebuttal.
- Logical organization.
- Elaboration.
- Purposeful transitions.
- Appropriate tone.

Must be modeled:

- Evaluating a prompt.
- Building a claim.
- Selecting credible evidence.
- Explaining reasoning.
- Rebutting a counterclaim.

Exemplars required:

- Annotated argument paragraph or essay.
- Claim/evidence/reasoning chart.
- Weak claim example.
- Weak counterclaim rebuttal and corrected version.

Independent writing:

- Students write an argument supporting a position with reasoning, credible evidence, counterclaim rebuttal, organization, transitions, and appropriate tone.

### 4.7 Research Writing

Must be explicitly taught:

- Research question.
- Source evaluation.
- Note-taking.
- Paraphrase and summary.
- Synthesis.
- Source integration.
- Avoiding plagiarism.
- Organized explanation or argument from sources.

Must be modeled:

- How to move from question to notes to source-based writing.
- How to combine sources without copying.
- How to explain source evidence.

Exemplars required:

- Research question examples.
- Note-taking model.
- Source synthesis paragraph.
- Weak patchwriting example and corrected version.

Independent writing:

- Students produce source-based writing that answers a research question using reliable and valid sources.

## 5. Research Instruction Standards

### 5.1 Research Must Be Taught

Students must never be told simply to conduct research. Lessons must teach each component of research before students apply it independently.

### 5.2 Research Question Development

Must be explicitly taught:

- Difference between topic and research question.
- Open-ended versus closed questions.
- Scope.
- Refinement based on findings.

Must be modeled:

- Turning a broad topic into a focused question.
- Revising a question that is too broad, too narrow, or not researchable.

Examples required:

- Strong and weak research questions.
- Question refinement chart.

### 5.3 Source Evaluation

Must be explicitly taught:

- Reliability.
- Validity.
- Credibility.
- Relevance.
- Accuracy.
- Currency when appropriate.
- Bias and perspective.
- Purpose and audience.

Must be modeled:

- How to inspect author, publisher, evidence, date, purpose, and bias.
- How to reject a weak source.
- How to compare two sources on the same issue.

Examples required:

- Source evaluation checklist.
- Annotated source page or citation information.
- Reliable versus unreliable source comparison.

### 5.4 Note-Taking

Must be explicitly taught:

- Difference between quoting, paraphrasing, and summarizing.
- How to record source information.
- How to sort notes by idea.
- How to avoid copying too much.

Must be modeled:

- Reading a source excerpt and creating notes.
- Turning a quote into a paraphrase.
- Labeling notes by claim, support, or category.

Examples required:

- Good note example.
- Weak copied-note example.
- Organized note chart.

### 5.5 Source Integration

Must be explicitly taught:

- Introducing source information.
- Blending evidence into a sentence.
- Explaining source evidence.
- Maintaining the writer's own structure.

Must be modeled:

- Lead-in, evidence, explanation.
- Paraphrase with explanation.
- Quote with explanation.

Examples required:

- Integrated source sentence.
- Dropped quote example.
- Corrected source integration.

### 5.6 Source Synthesis

Must be explicitly taught:

- Synthesis means combining ideas, not stacking summaries.
- How to compare, connect, contrast, and group source ideas.
- How to use multiple sources to answer a question.

Must be modeled:

- Two-source synthesis.
- Source agreement/disagreement.
- Synthesis paragraph.

Examples required:

- Source synthesis chart.
- Summary-only paragraph and revised synthesis paragraph.

## 6. Language, Vocabulary, Style, and Revision Standards

### 6.1 Unit 5 and Spiraling Requirement

Unit 5 remains a dedicated Language, Vocabulary, Style, and Revision unit.

However, language, vocabulary, style, and revision must spiral across every unit and every English course.

Minimum spiral requirement:

- Unit 1 and early course work must include academic vocabulary, sentence control, punctuation, and basic revision.
- Literary units must include diction, figurative language, tone, sentence variety, and analytical language.
- Informational and argument units must include transitions, formal tone, logic, precision, and evidence language.
- Research units must include source integration, paraphrase, summary, clarity, and citation readiness.
- Unit 5 must deepen, remediate, and validate language/vocabulary/revision mastery.
- Final communication units must apply polished language in oral and digital formats.

### 6.2 Grammar Instruction

Grammar must be taught as a tool for communication, not as isolated rule memorization.

Grammar instruction must:

- Use examples from authentic reading and writing.
- Explain how grammar affects clarity and meaning.
- Include correct and incorrect examples.
- Require students to apply grammar in their own writing.
- Spiral across lessons.

Grammar topics should be connected to:

- Sentence boundaries.
- Punctuation.
- Capitalization.
- Spelling.
- Usage.
- Parallel structure.
- Phrases and clauses.
- Sentence variety.
- Pronoun clarity.
- Agreement.
- Consistent tone and style.

### 6.3 Vocabulary Instruction

Vocabulary must be taught in context and used in speaking and writing.

Vocabulary instruction must:

- Define academic and content vocabulary before students need it.
- Show words in context.
- Teach connotation and denotation.
- Teach context clues.
- Teach word relationships.
- Teach etymology, roots, affixes, and derivations where appropriate.
- Require students to use vocabulary accurately in reading responses, writing, discussion, and presentation.

Vocabulary must not be limited to memorized lists.

### 6.4 Style Instruction

Style instruction must teach students how language choices affect meaning, tone, flow, and audience.

Style instruction must include:

- Diction.
- Tone.
- Sentence variety.
- Transitions.
- Precision.
- Concision.
- Formal versus informal language.
- Audience awareness.
- Purposeful emphasis.

### 6.5 Revision Instruction

Revision must be taught as meaning improvement, not only correction.

Revision instruction must include:

- Revising for clarity.
- Revising for cohesion.
- Revising for evidence.
- Revising for explanation.
- Revising for organization.
- Revising for tone.
- Editing for conventions after meaning is improved.

Lessons must model revision by showing a weak draft, identifying problems, and improving it.

## 7. MLA English Scaffolding Framework

### 7.1 Required Scaffold Sequence

Every complex English skill must follow this instructional release:

| Stage | Student Support | Developer Requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Explicit Instruction | Full support | Define and explain the concept clearly. |
| Modeling | Full support | Show how an expert reader/writer thinks through the skill. |
| Guided Examples | High support | Provide annotated examples and partial analysis. |
| Guided Practice | Moderate support | Students practice with prompts, frames, charts, or checkpoints. |
| Independent Practice | Low support | Students apply the skill without step-by-step help. |
| Mastery Application | Independent | Students demonstrate the skill in a new or more complex context. |

### 7.2 Increasing Independence

Independence must increase gradually:

- First, students recognize the skill.
- Then, students explain the skill with support.
- Then, students apply the skill with guidance.
- Then, students apply the skill independently.
- Finally, students transfer the skill to a new text, writing task, research task, or communication task.

### 7.3 Scaffold Removal

Scaffolds should be removed only after students have had enough modeled and guided practice.

Examples:

- Sentence frames may appear early, then become optional.
- Evidence charts may be provided early, then students create their own.
- Annotated models may be detailed early, then lighter later.
- Step lists may be reduced as students internalize the process.

## 8. Visual and Example Requirements

### 8.1 Visual Supports

Lessons must use visual supports when they clarify thinking, organization, or process.

Acceptable visual supports include:

- Text structure charts.
- Theme development charts.
- Character analysis charts.
- Argument maps.
- Evidence explanation charts.
- Research source evaluation checklists.
- Writing outlines.
- Revision checklists.
- Vocabulary word maps.
- Process flowcharts.

Visuals must teach the skill, not decorate the lesson.

### 8.2 Reading Supports

Reading supports must include:

- Purpose-setting questions.
- Chunked text where appropriate.
- Annotation guidance.
- Vocabulary support.
- Think-aloud examples.
- Evidence prompts.
- Summary and analysis distinctions.

### 8.3 Writing Supports

Writing supports must include:

- Prompt breakdowns.
- Planning charts.
- Structure models.
- Sentence frames when useful.
- Annotated exemplars.
- Revision checklists.
- Common mistake examples.

### 8.4 Anchor Examples

Each major skill should have an anchor example students can return to.

Anchor examples must:

- Be clear.
- Be annotated.
- Show the expected thinking.
- Use the target vocabulary.
- Connect directly to the skill.

### 8.5 Annotated Examples

Annotated examples must label:

- Skill being shown.
- Evidence.
- Reasoning.
- Organization.
- Vocabulary.
- Style choices.
- Revision choices where applicable.

### 8.6 Sample Responses

Sample responses must include:

- Strong response.
- Weak or incomplete response.
- Explanation of why the strong response works.
- Explanation of why the weak response does not meet mastery.

### 8.7 Worked Examples

Worked examples must show the process, not only the final answer.

For reading:

- Show how to notice details.
- Show how to infer meaning.
- Show how to select evidence.
- Show how to explain evidence.

For writing:

- Show planning.
- Show drafting.
- Show revision.
- Show final response.

For research:

- Show question refinement.
- Show source evaluation.
- Show note-taking.
- Show synthesis.

### 8.8 Common Mistake Examples

Lessons must include common mistake examples for complex skills.

Common mistake examples should show:

- Summary instead of analysis.
- Evidence without explanation.
- Claim without support.
- Vague theme statement.
- Weak central idea.
- Unreliable source.
- Patchwriting.
- Counterclaim without rebuttal.
- Grammar correction that does not improve clarity.
- Vocabulary used inaccurately.

## 9. Mastery-Based Learning Requirements

### 9.1 Mastery Over Completion

MLA English instruction must support mastery, not task completion.

Students should demonstrate:

- Understanding of the concept.
- Accurate application.
- Ability to explain reasoning.
- Ability to revise mistakes.
- Transfer to a new example or task when appropriate.

### 9.2 Revision and Resubmission

Lessons must be designed so students can use feedback to improve.

Instruction should support:

- Identifying what was wrong.
- Understanding why it was wrong.
- Revising the work.
- Resubmitting when required by course policy.
- Applying feedback to future work.

### 9.3 Student Self-Correction

Lessons must teach students how to self-correct.

Self-correction supports may include:

- Checklists.
- Error-spotting examples.
- Reflection prompts.
- Compare-your-response prompts.
- Revision questions.
- Criteria-based self-review.

### 9.4 Student Reflection

Reflection should help students understand their learning process.

Reflection prompts should ask students to identify:

- What skill they used.
- What evidence supports their answer.
- What mistake they corrected.
- What revision improved their work.
- What strategy they will use next time.

### 9.5 AI-Supported Remediation

AI-supported remediation may be used to provide extra explanation, practice, feedback, or review, but it must not replace the lesson's core instruction.

AI-supported remediation should:

- Reinforce the lesson skill.
- Help students identify errors.
- Provide practice at the correct level.
- Encourage revision and explanation.
- Avoid giving students final answers to submit as their own work.

### 9.6 Multiple Opportunities for Mastery

Lessons should support mastery through:

- Initial instruction.
- Guided practice.
- Independent practice.
- Feedback.
- Revision.
- Reattempts where course policy allows.
- Spiral review in later lessons.

### 9.7 Long-Term Retention

Instruction must support retention by:

- Spiraling key skills.
- Revisiting vocabulary.
- Applying reading skills across genres.
- Applying writing skills across modes.
- Using cumulative practice.
- Connecting new skills to prior skills.

## 10. English Lesson Design Guardrails

Future curriculum developers must avoid the following design errors:

- Assuming teacher explanation.
- Assuming parent support.
- Assuming prior mastery.
- Assuming strong reading skills.
- Assuming strong writing skills.
- Assuming academic vocabulary knowledge.
- Assigning analysis without teaching analysis.
- Assigning essays without teaching essay structure.
- Assigning research without teaching research.
- Assigning revision without modeling revision.
- Assigning presentations without teaching organization, evidence, audience, and delivery expectations.
- Asking students to evaluate evidence before teaching evidence quality.
- Asking students to write arguments before teaching claims, reasoning, evidence, counterclaims, and rebuttal.
- Asking students to identify theme without teaching topic versus theme.
- Asking students to interpret figurative language without teaching literal meaning, figurative meaning, and effect.
- Asking students to use sources without teaching credibility, reliability, validity, bias, and relevance.
- Providing insufficient examples.
- Providing examples without annotations.
- Providing only final products without showing process.
- Providing practice before instruction.
- Providing independent work before guided practice.
- Teaching vocabulary without context.
- Teaching grammar in isolation.
- Treating grammar as correction only, not meaning support.
- Treating revision as proofreading only.
- Making lessons too brief for independent mastery.
- Skipping prerequisite steps.
- Using vague directions such as "analyze" or "explain" without defining the expected thinking.
- Using prompts that require background knowledge not taught or provided.
- Using texts without adequate reading support.
- Overloading lessons with too many new skills.
- Treating support benchmarks as optional.
- Treating accelerated learners as students who need less clarity.
- Treating remedial learners as students who need lower rigor.
- Making Unit 5 the first serious exposure to language, vocabulary, or revision.
- Making Unit 6 the first serious exposure to oral or digital communication.
- Creating decorative visuals that do not teach.
- Using generic worksheets that do not connect to the target skill.
- Treating completion as mastery.

## 11. Final MLA English Instructional Design Standards

The following standards are mandatory for future MLA English curriculum development.

### Standard 1: Independent Mastery

Every English lesson must be designed so a student can learn the target skill independently through the curriculum materials.

### Standard 2: Explicit Instruction

Every new reading, writing, research, language, vocabulary, revision, or communication skill must be explicitly taught before students are asked to apply it.

### Standard 3: Modeled Thinking

Lessons must model the thinking process behind expert reading, writing, research, revision, and communication.

### Standard 4: Worked and Annotated Examples

Every major skill must include worked examples and annotated examples that show what mastery looks like and why.

### Standard 5: Guided Before Independent

Students must receive guided practice before independent application.

### Standard 6: Scaffolded Complexity

Complex skills must be broken into smaller teachable components and sequenced from simple to complex.

### Standard 7: Spiral Support

Paraphrase, evidence, academic vocabulary, conventions, revision, discussion, source awareness, and audience awareness must spiral across English courses.

### Standard 8: Dedicated and Distributed Language Instruction

Language, vocabulary, style, and revision must be taught throughout the course and also receive dedicated focus where the approved course blueprint requires it.

### Standard 9: Writing as Process

Writing instruction must teach planning, drafting, evidence use, organization, style, revision, and editing. Students must not be assigned writing without instruction in the writing process.

### Standard 10: Research as Process

Research instruction must teach question development, source evaluation, reliability, validity, credibility, bias, note-taking, integration, and synthesis.

### Standard 11: Reading Analysis as Process

Reading analysis must teach students how to move from comprehension to observation, inference, evidence, explanation, and interpretation.

### Standard 12: Mastery Over Completion

Course design must verify mastery, support revision and resubmission, and help students correct misunderstandings.

### Standard 13: Learner Range

Lessons must support remedial, standard, and accelerated learners without lowering rigor or creating unnecessary barriers.

### Standard 14: Authentic Application

Grammar, vocabulary, style, and revision must be connected to authentic reading, writing, speaking, research, and presentation tasks.

### Standard 15: Accreditation-Level Clarity

Instruction must be clear enough for external review: the intended skill, instructional path, scaffolds, examples, practice, and mastery expectations must be visible in the lesson design.
