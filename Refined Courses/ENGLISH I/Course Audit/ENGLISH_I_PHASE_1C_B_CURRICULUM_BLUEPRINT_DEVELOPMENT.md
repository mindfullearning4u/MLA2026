# English I Phase 1C-B Curriculum Blueprint Development

## Scope Control

This report is curriculum blueprint analysis only.

The approved MLA structure is treated as fixed:

- 6 Units
- 8 Lessons per Unit
- Lesson 8 = Putting It All Together
- Lesson 8 contains Unit Assessment
- Lessons 1-7 contain Lesson Quizzes
- Unit Pretest
- Guided Practice
- Notebook Evidence
- Checkpoint Submission

This report does not recommend structural changes.

This report does not create lessons, assessments, benchmark mappings, crosswalks, lesson sequences, HTML files, quiz items, or assessment items.

The recommendations below place benchmark clusters at the unit level only. They are intended to drive a future crosswalk, not replace it.

## Working Basis

This Phase 1C-B blueprint uses:

- The completed Phase 1B architecture report.
- The completed Phase 1C-A Benchmark Analysis and Clustering report.
- The local English I Florida B.E.S.T. benchmark tracker.
- The Phase 1C-A benchmark clusters: foundational reading and academic response, literary analysis and craft, informational text and rhetoric, argument analysis and argument writing, narrative writing and literary craft application, research and expository communication, language/vocabulary/revision, and oral/digital communication.

No individual benchmark-to-unit crosswalk is created in this report.

## Executive Summary

The recommended English I blueprint should preserve the approved six-unit MLA structure while organizing instruction around a clear progression from foundational reading and writing habits to independent synthesis and communication.

The strongest unit sequence is:

| Unit | Blueprint Focus | Primary Cluster Placement |
|---|---|---|
| Unit 1 | Foundations of Close Reading and Evidence-Based Writing | Foundational Reading and Academic Response |
| Unit 2 | Literary Analysis and Narrative Craft | Literary Analysis and Craft; Narrative Writing and Literary Craft Application |
| Unit 3 | Informational Text, Rhetoric, and Argument | Informational Text and Rhetoric; Argument Analysis and Argument Writing |
| Unit 4 | Research and Expository Writing | Research and Expository Communication |
| Unit 5 | Language, Vocabulary, Style, and Revision | Language, Vocabulary, and Revision |
| Unit 6 | Synthesis, Presentation, and Digital Communication | Oral and Digital Communication, with synthesis of prior clusters |

This blueprint balances Florida B.E.S.T. requirements with SAT, ACT, college readiness, and workforce communication expectations. The largest benchmark density occurs in Units 2 and 3 because Grade 9 has substantial literary and informational reading requirements. Unit 5 is intentionally language- and vocabulary-heavy, but it should also reinforce reading and writing so it does not become disconnected grammar practice. Unit 6 should function as the capstone application unit, not as a place to introduce large new skill loads.

## Unit Placement Recommendations

### Unit 1: Foundations of Close Reading and Evidence-Based Writing

Recommended clusters:

- Foundational Reading and Academic Response

Supporting subskills from the cluster:

- Paraphrase and accurate comprehension.
- Academic vocabulary use.
- Standard English foundations.
- Evidence-ready written and spoken response.

Why these clusters belong here:

Unit 1 should establish the habits students need before they can analyze literature, rhetoric, argument, sources, or digital communication. Students must be able to read grade-level text accurately, paraphrase content, identify relevant evidence, use basic academic vocabulary, and write in standard English before the course increases analytical complexity.

Required prerequisite skills:

- Basic reading comprehension.
- Sentence-level writing control.
- Ability to identify stated details.
- Basic familiarity with school-based academic vocabulary.

Progression logic:

Unit 1 creates the common foundation for all later units. Literary analysis in Unit 2 depends on accurate paraphrase and evidence use. Informational and argumentative analysis in Unit 3 depends on central idea recognition and precise language. Research in Unit 4 depends on the ability to summarize, paraphrase, and explain source information.

Placement cautions:

- Do not overload Unit 1 with advanced literary or rhetorical analysis.
- Do not turn Unit 1 into a general orientation unit.
- Keep all foundations tied to real reading and writing performance.

### Unit 2: Literary Analysis and Narrative Craft

Recommended clusters:

- Literary Analysis and Craft
- Narrative Writing and Literary Craft Application

Supporting subskills from the clusters:

- Literary elements and layered meaning.
- Universal theme development.
- Character, structure, and epic poetry.
- Narrator perspective, irony, and satire.
- Figurative language and mood.
- Adaptation of mythical, classical, or religious texts.
- Narrative techniques, point of view, and transitions.

Why these clusters belong here:

Literary analysis is the first major interpretive demand after foundational reading. Students are ready to move from "What does the text say?" to "How does the author create meaning?" This unit should concentrate the Literary Analysis and Craft cluster because its benchmarks share analysis of craft, theme, structure, perspective, figurative language, and literary tradition. The Narrative Writing and Literary Craft Application cluster belongs here as a writing application of literary craft.

Required prerequisite skills:

- Accurate paraphrase.
- Evidence selection.
- Basic claim-evidence-explanation response.
- Academic vocabulary for reading and analysis.
- Standard English sentence control.

Progression logic:

The literary clusters should come before informational rhetoric because students can first practice analysis in narrative and poetic contexts where theme, character, and perspective are concrete entry points. Narrative writing belongs here because it helps students understand author craft from the writer's side without becoming a full creative-writing course.

Placement cautions:

- Do not isolate narrative writing from literary craft.
- Do not treat epic/classical material as a separate literature survey.
- Avoid making this unit the only place where literary reading appears; literary skills should recur later for synthesis and style analysis.

### Unit 3: Informational Text, Rhetoric, and Argument

Recommended clusters:

- Informational Text and Rhetoric
- Argument Analysis and Argument Writing

Supporting subskills from the clusters:

- Informational text structure and features.
- Central idea and support.
- Author purpose.
- Rhetorical appeals and figurative language in nonfiction.
- Rhetorical explanation.
- Opposing arguments.
- Claims, evidence, validity, and counterclaims.
- Argument writing.

Why these clusters belong here:

Informational analysis and argument require a stronger analytical foundation than Unit 1 provides. Placing these clusters after literary analysis lets students transfer evidence, structure, purpose, and word-choice analysis into nonfiction and argument. This unit is also central to SAT Reading and Writing, ACT Reading, ACT English, college reading, and civic/workplace communication.

Required prerequisite skills:

- Accurate comprehension and paraphrase.
- Evidence-based response.
- Basic analysis of author choices.
- Awareness of tone and word choice.
- Sentence and paragraph control.

Progression logic:

Students move from literary craft to nonfiction craft, then from nonfiction craft to argumentative reasoning. Central idea and text structure should precede rhetoric and argument because students need to understand how informational texts are organized before they can evaluate rhetorical purpose or claim validity.

Placement cautions:

- Do not start with full argument writing before students can evaluate arguments.
- Do not treat rhetorical appeals as vocabulary only; they must connect to purpose, audience, and effect.
- Keep opposing-argument analysis tied to evidence quality and validity.

### Unit 4: Research and Expository Writing

Recommended clusters:

- Research and Expository Communication

Supporting subskills from the cluster:

- Research question development.
- Source reliability and validity.
- Scope refinement.
- Source-based explanation.
- Multi-source expository writing.
- Collaborative publication-ready writing.

Why these clusters belong here:

Research belongs after informational and argument analysis because students need to evaluate central ideas, evidence, claims, and credibility before conducting meaningful research. Expository writing belongs with research because students must explain and analyze information from multiple sources using logical organization.

Required prerequisite skills:

- Paraphrasing.
- Central idea analysis.
- Evidence evaluation.
- Source credibility awareness.
- Organized paragraph and multi-paragraph writing.
- Academic vocabulary and conventions.

Progression logic:

Unit 4 converts reading and argument skills into source-based writing. Students first learn to ask and refine questions, then evaluate sources, then synthesize evidence, then explain findings in organized expository writing. Collaborative publication fits here because source-based writing benefits from revision, audience awareness, and digital tools.

Placement cautions:

- Do not introduce research before students understand evidence quality.
- Do not let research become a mechanical source hunt.
- Do not create full assessment tasks in this blueprint phase.

### Unit 5: Language, Vocabulary, Style, and Revision

Recommended clusters:

- Language, Vocabulary, and Revision

Supporting subskills from the cluster:

- Standard English grammar, usage, and conventions.
- Sentence variety, phrases, clauses, and flow.
- Revision for clarity and cohesion.
- Feedback-based revision.
- Academic vocabulary in speaking and writing.
- Context clues and word relationships.
- Figurative language, connotation, and denotation.
- Etymology and derivations.

Why these clusters belong here:

Language and vocabulary occur throughout the course, but they need concentrated attention before the final synthesis and communication unit. Unit 5 should improve the quality of student writing across narrative, literary analysis, argument, expository writing, and research. It should also deepen vocabulary acquisition strategies that support independent reading and precise communication.

Required prerequisite skills:

- Prior writing in multiple modes.
- Basic convention control.
- Experience using textual evidence.
- Familiarity with academic vocabulary.
- Exposure to literary and informational word choice.

Progression logic:

By Unit 5, students have produced and revised several types of writing. That gives language and revision instruction real material to improve. Vocabulary can also move beyond introductory academic terms into morphology, etymology, connotation, denotation, and rhetorical precision.

Placement cautions:

- Do not make Unit 5 disconnected grammar drill.
- Do not delay all language instruction until Unit 5; conventions must spiral from Unit 1.
- Do not treat vocabulary as memorized word lists only.

### Unit 6: Synthesis, Presentation, and Digital Communication

Recommended clusters:

- Oral and Digital Communication

Supporting synthesis from prior clusters:

- Cross-text synthesis.
- Coherent perspective.
- Credible evidence in communication.
- Audience-aware writing.
- Online collaborative communication.
- Reading, writing, research, language, and vocabulary skills developed earlier in the course.

Why these clusters belong here:

Unit 6 should be the culminating application of the course. Students use reading, writing, research, language, vocabulary, and communication skills to present a coherent perspective with credible evidence. This placement supports college and career readiness because students must communicate ideas clearly in oral, digital, and collaborative contexts.

Required prerequisite skills:

- Text comprehension and paraphrase.
- Literary and informational analysis.
- Evidence evaluation.
- Argument and expository writing.
- Research and source use.
- Revision and language control.
- Academic vocabulary.

Progression logic:

Synthesis and presentation should come after students have built the necessary analytical and written communication skills. The final unit should integrate rather than expand the benchmark load. Its success depends on repeated spiraling of evidence, source credibility, clarity, cohesion, vocabulary, and conventions.

Placement cautions:

- Do not make Unit 6 a new-skills unit.
- Do not introduce a major new writing mode here.
- Keep the emphasis on synthesis, communication, audience, and evidence.

## Skill Progression Blueprint

### Reading Progression

| Skill Area | Early Course | Middle Course | Late Course |
|---|---|---|---|
| Literary Analysis | Comprehend and paraphrase literary texts; identify key details and basic elements. | Analyze literary elements, universal theme, narrator perspective, irony, satire, mood, epic structure, and adaptation. | Reuse literary analysis in synthesis, style analysis, discussion, and presentation. |
| Informational Analysis | Identify main ideas, supporting details, and basic text organization. | Analyze central idea development, text structures, features, purpose, support, and argument development. | Synthesize informational texts with research and presentation demands. |
| Evidence Analysis | Select relevant evidence and explain what it shows. | Evaluate support, credibility, validity, reasoning, and counterclaims. | Use evidence across sources to support a coherent perspective. |
| Rhetorical Analysis | Notice author word choice and purpose at a basic level. | Explain rhetoric, appeals, figurative language, audience, purpose, and effect. | Apply rhetorical awareness to presentations, digital communication, and revision. |

### Writing Progression

| Skill Area | Early Course | Middle Course | Late Course |
|---|---|---|---|
| Text-Based Responses | Write short responses with claim, evidence, and explanation. | Expand responses into literary and informational analysis. | Use text-based response skills in synthesis and presentation support. |
| Literary Analysis Writing | Explain how evidence supports interpretation. | Analyze theme, craft, perspective, mood, and adaptation. | Integrate literary insights into broader synthesis when appropriate. |
| Informative Writing | Explain information clearly from a text. | Organize expository writing from multiple sources. | Use informative writing to support presentation and digital communication. |
| Argument Writing | Identify claims, evidence, and reasoning. | Write arguments with credible evidence, reasoning, counterclaims, transitions, and tone. | Apply argument logic to synthesis and oral/digital perspective. |
| Research Writing | Paraphrase and summarize source information accurately. | Develop research questions, evaluate sources, synthesize information, and explain findings. | Use research evidence to support coherent communication. |

### Language Progression

| Skill Area | Early Course | Middle Course | Late Course |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grammar | Establish sentence control and standard English usage. | Apply grammar to analytical, argumentative, and expository writing. | Edit and polish writing for presentation and publication quality. |
| Usage | Use formal academic language in short responses. | Maintain appropriate tone, precise wording, and logical relationships. | Adapt usage to audience, purpose, oral presentation, and digital publication. |
| Conventions | Apply punctuation, capitalization, spelling, and sentence boundaries. | Use punctuation and sentence structure to clarify complex ideas and source integration. | Proofread and revise for final communication quality. |

### Vocabulary Progression

| Skill Area | Early Course | Middle Course | Late Course |
|---|---|---|---|
| Academic Vocabulary | Use core academic terms for reading, writing, evidence, and analysis. | Use literary, rhetorical, argument, and research vocabulary accurately. | Use audience-appropriate academic and domain vocabulary in presentations. |
| Context Clues | Infer meaning from nearby textual clues. | Interpret connotation, denotation, figurative meaning, and rhetorical word choice. | Apply context strategies independently across complex texts and sources. |
| Word Relationships | Identify basic relationships among words and ideas. | Use synonyms, antonyms, analogies, semantic relationships, and reference materials. | Choose precise words for clarity, tone, and audience. |
| Etymology | Recognize common roots and affixes where helpful. | Apply etymology and derivation to determine meaning. | Use morphology and etymology independently for unfamiliar academic words. |

### Communication Progression

| Skill Area | Early Course | Middle Course | Late Course |
|---|---|---|---|
| Discussion | Participate in text-based discussion using evidence. | Discuss literary, informational, rhetorical, and research ideas with greater precision. | Present and defend a coherent perspective using credible evidence. |
| Presentation | Explain a short text-based idea orally. | Organize information and evidence for audience understanding. | Deliver an organized oral presentation with coherent focus and clear perspective. |
| Digital Communication | Use digital tools for writing and basic collaboration. | Use collaborative platforms for source-based writing and revision. | Create digital presentations with coherent ideas and audience awareness. |

## Unit Balance Analysis

### Benchmark Balance

The recommended blueprint produces a deliberate benchmark-density curve:

| Unit | Expected Benchmark Density | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Unit 1 | Low-medium | Foundation skills are essential but should not absorb advanced benchmark clusters. |
| Unit 2 | High | Literary analysis contains several major Grade 9 reading expectations plus narrative writing support. |
| Unit 3 | High | Informational analysis, rhetoric, argument evaluation, and argument writing form the densest readiness area. |
| Unit 4 | Medium-high | Research and expository writing require multi-step source work but should not compete with all argument benchmarks. |
| Unit 5 | Medium | Language, vocabulary, and revision are concentrated here but must also spiral all year. |
| Unit 6 | Medium | Communication and synthesis apply prior skills rather than introducing many new clusters. |

### Skill Balance

Strengths:

- Reading foundations precede complex analysis.
- Literary and informational analysis receive separate high-focus units.
- Argument is placed after students have enough nonfiction-analysis skill.
- Research follows evidence and credibility analysis.
- Language and vocabulary receive both spiral support and focused refinement.
- Communication appears as the final application of the course.

Potential gaps:

- If Unit 1 is too light on evidence writing, later units will struggle.
- If Unit 2 does not spiral paraphrase and evidence, literary analysis may become unsupported interpretation.
- If Unit 3 overemphasizes full argument writing before argument reading, students may write weak claims.
- If Unit 4 lacks clear source-evaluation practice, research writing may become summary only.
- If Unit 5 is isolated from prior student writing, revision will not transfer.
- If Unit 6 does not reuse research and evidence, presentation may become performance rather than academic communication.

### Reading Balance

Recommended balance:

- Unit 1: Short literary and informational texts for shared foundations.
- Unit 2: Literary concentration, including short stories, poetry, drama excerpts, epic/classical texts, and adaptation pairings.
- Unit 3: Informational, rhetorical, argumentative, and speech-based texts.
- Unit 4: Source sets, informational texts, digital sources, and reference-style materials.
- Unit 5: Short literary and informational texts selected for style, diction, grammar, and vocabulary.
- Unit 6: Mixed source clusters for synthesis.

Reading balance conclusion:

The blueprint avoids a literature-only course and supports a balanced Grade 9 reading profile. The main risk is underusing literary texts after Unit 2 or underusing nonfiction before Unit 3. Both should be handled through spiraling, not by changing the unit structure.

### Writing Balance

Recommended writing balance:

- Unit 1: Evidence-based short responses.
- Unit 2: Literary analysis writing and narrative writing.
- Unit 3: Argument writing.
- Unit 4: Expository and research writing.
- Unit 5: Revision across writing modes.
- Unit 6: Synthesis and presentation-support writing.

Writing balance conclusion:

The blueprint gives each major writing mode a logical instructional home without assigning specific lesson tasks. Revision is placed after students have generated enough writing to revise meaningfully.

### Overloaded Units

Potentially overloaded:

- Unit 2, because it contains multiple literary benchmarks plus narrative writing.
- Unit 3, because it contains informational analysis, rhetoric, argument evaluation, and argument writing.
- Unit 4, if research, expository writing, and collaborative publication are all treated as separate full units within the unit.

Mitigation:

- Keep Unit 2 organized around literary craft and theme rather than separate genre mini-units.
- In Unit 3, sequence from central idea and structure to rhetoric, then argument evaluation, then argument writing.
- In Unit 4, treat research and expository writing as one connected source-based communication process.

### Weak Units

Potentially weak:

- Unit 1, if it becomes too general.
- Unit 5, if language and vocabulary are disconnected from authentic reading and writing.
- Unit 6, if it only repeats prior work without a clear synthesis purpose.

Mitigation:

- Unit 1 must require real grade-level reading and evidence-based writing.
- Unit 5 must revise authentic student writing and analyze real texts for style and vocabulary.
- Unit 6 must require coherent perspective, credible evidence, and audience-aware oral/digital communication.

### Redundancies

Expected and appropriate spirals:

- Paraphrase should recur across every unit.
- Academic vocabulary should recur across every unit.
- Standard English conventions should recur across every unit.
- Evidence selection and explanation should recur across every unit.
- Source credibility should recur in Units 3, 4, and 6.

Problematic redundancies to avoid:

- Re-teaching claim-evidence-explanation as if new in every unit.
- Repeating isolated grammar lessons without application.
- Repeating generic vocabulary lists instead of increasing word-analysis complexity.
- Repeating presentations without increasing organization, evidence, and audience control.

## SAT/ACT Alignment Review

### SAT Reading Alignment

Strengths:

- Unit 1 supports Information and Ideas through comprehension, paraphrase, and evidence.
- Unit 2 supports Craft and Structure through literary elements, perspective, mood, and word choice.
- Unit 3 strongly supports nonfiction Craft and Structure through rhetoric, purpose, and argument.
- Unit 4 supports information synthesis through source work.
- Unit 6 supports cross-text synthesis and evidence-based communication.

Gaps to monitor:

- Include regular paired-text work before Unit 6.
- Ensure students practice interpreting evidence across both literary and informational contexts.
- Include vocabulary-in-context throughout, not only in Unit 5.

### SAT Writing Alignment

Strengths:

- Unit 1 supports sentence control and clarity.
- Unit 3 supports organization, transitions, rhetorical goals, claims, and evidence.
- Unit 4 supports source-based expression of ideas.
- Unit 5 strongly supports Standard English Conventions, precision, cohesion, and style.
- Unit 6 supports audience-aware expression and digital communication.

Gaps to monitor:

- Revision should appear before Unit 5 in smaller cycles.
- Grammar should be applied in authentic writing, not delayed.
- Transitions and logical relationships need consistent attention in Units 1-4.

### ACT Reading Alignment

Strengths:

- Unit 1 supports key ideas and details.
- Unit 2 supports literary craft and structure.
- Unit 3 supports informational craft, argument, author purpose, and evidence.
- Unit 6 supports integration of knowledge and ideas.

Gaps to monitor:

- Include timed or efficient-reading practice only when assessment design is authorized.
- Maintain genre variety so students read prose fiction, humanities, social science, and science-style informational texts over time.

### ACT English Alignment

Strengths:

- Unit 3 supports production of writing through organization, purpose, and argument.
- Unit 4 supports organization and clarity in expository writing.
- Unit 5 supports conventions, usage, punctuation, sentence structure, precision, and cohesion.
- Unit 6 supports audience and purpose in communication.

Gaps to monitor:

- ACT English expects revision decisions at sentence and paragraph levels, so revision should spiral before Unit 5.
- Students need repeated practice with concision, placement, transitions, and sentence relationships.

## College & Career Readiness Review

### College Reading

The blueprint supports college reading by requiring students to:

- Read grade-level complex literary and informational texts.
- Analyze central ideas, themes, structures, claims, and rhetoric.
- Evaluate evidence and validity.
- Synthesize across texts and sources.
- Use academic vocabulary accurately.

Readiness gap to monitor:

Students should encounter sufficiently complex nonfiction and literary nonfiction. Overreliance on short literary texts would weaken college reading readiness.

### College Writing

The blueprint supports college writing by requiring students to:

- Write text-based analytical responses.
- Develop literary analysis.
- Write argument with reasoning, evidence, and counterclaims.
- Write expository texts from multiple sources.
- Revise for clarity, cohesion, tone, and conventions.

Readiness gap to monitor:

Students need multi-paragraph development before research writing. If Unit 1 and Unit 2 stay only at short response level, Unit 4 may be too steep.

### Research

The blueprint supports research readiness by requiring students to:

- Ask and refine research questions.
- Evaluate reliable and valid sources.
- Synthesize information.
- Use source evidence in explanatory communication.
- Prepare for citation and publication expectations.

Readiness gap to monitor:

Research must include source evaluation and synthesis, not only gathering facts.

### Workplace Communication

The blueprint supports workforce communication by requiring students to:

- Communicate with audience and purpose in mind.
- Use clear standard English.
- Present information orally.
- Use digital presentation tools.
- Collaborate through online writing platforms.
- Support claims and perspectives with credible evidence.

Readiness gap to monitor:

Oral and digital communication should be practiced before Unit 6 in small ways so the final unit is not the first exposure.

| Unit 3 | Informational Text, Rhetoric, and Argument | Informational Text and Rhetoric; Argument Analysis and Argument Writing | Informational texts, speeches, essays, arguments | Argument writing | Transitions, parallel structure, formal tone | Rhetorical and argument vocabulary | Discussion of claims and evidence |

### Blueprint Principles

- Unit 1 establishes foundations.
- Unit 2 develops literary interpretation.
- Unit 3 develops nonfiction, rhetoric, and argument.
- Unit 4 develops research and source-based explanation.
- Unit 5 refines language, vocabulary, and writing quality.
- Unit 6 applies and synthesizes the full course.

### Non-Negotiable Design Guardrails

- Do not change the approved 6-unit, 8-lesson MLA structure.
- Do not assign individual benchmarks to lessons in this phase.
- Do not create lesson objectives in this phase.
- Do not create assessments or assessment items in this phase.
- Do not create a benchmark crosswalk in this phase.
- Do not let any unit become disconnected from text-based reading and writing.

## Recommendations for Phase 2: English I Crosswalk Development

Phase 2 should convert this blueprint into a formal crosswalk framework.

Recommended Phase 2 outputs:

- Complete benchmark inventory using official Florida B.E.S.T. benchmark language.
- Cluster-to-unit crosswalk.
- Primary and supporting benchmark placement.
- Spiral reinforcement notes.
- Benchmark evidence expectations.
- SAT/ACT readiness tags.
- College and career readiness tags.
- Quality-control review for overload, gaps, and redundancies.

Phase 2 should still avoid lesson creation unless separately authorized.

Before Phase 2 begins, confirm:

- The six unit titles.
- The cluster placement by unit.
- Whether narrative writing remains paired with literary analysis.
- Whether research and expository writing remain paired.
- Whether Unit 5 is approved as the dedicated language, vocabulary, style, and revision unit.
- Whether Unit 6 is approved as the synthesis, presentation, and digital communication unit.
