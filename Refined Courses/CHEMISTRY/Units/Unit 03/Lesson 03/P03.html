Covalent Bonding and Molecular Compounds

Quick review: A neutral atom has the same number of protons and electrons. Valence electrons are the outer electrons that take part in bonding. In an ionic bond, electrons are transferred. In a covalent bond, atoms share one or more pairs of electrons.

1. Why nonmetal atoms share electrons

Two nonmetal nuclei both attract electrons strongly. Neither atom easily gives its electrons away. When their valence orbitals overlap, both nuclei attract the same pair of electrons. That shared attraction holds the atoms together.

3. Read a molecular formula

A subscript tells how many atoms of that element are in one molecule. No subscript means 1 atom. In H2O, the 2 belongs only to H: two H atoms and one O atom.

Water: H2O
H—O—H

O forms two single bonds. Each H forms one.

Carbon dioxide: CO2
O=C=O

C forms two double bonds, one to each O.

4. A repeatable method

  1. Count every valence electron contributed by every atom.
  2. Choose a central atom; hydrogen is never central.
  3. Connect atoms with single bonds first.
  4. Complete the outside atoms' octets (hydrogen needs only 2).
  5. Place leftover electrons on the central atom.
  6. If the central atom lacks an octet, convert a neighboring lone pair into a double or triple bond.
  7. Count again to confirm the drawing uses exactly the available electrons.
Important: A molecular formula tells the number of atoms. A Lewis structure also shows how valence electrons are arranged and which atoms are connected.
