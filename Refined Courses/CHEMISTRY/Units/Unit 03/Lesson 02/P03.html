How Ionic Compounds Form

This is the teaching page. Read and study; do not complete student work here.

1. Electrons create ions

An atom is neutral when protons equal electrons. Losing electrons leaves more protons than electrons and forms a positive cation. Gaining electrons creates more electrons than protons and forms a negative anion.

Particle Protons Electrons Charge calculation
Li+ 3 2 3 - 2 = +1
F- 9 10 9 - 10 = -1
O2- 8 10 8 - 10 = -2

2. Opposite charges attract

Electron transfer produces ions. Electrostatic attraction holds oppositely charged ions together; this attraction is the ionic bond. Like charges repel, so a stable arrangement places positive and negative ions next to one another.

3. Use the periodic table to predict common ion charges

The periodic table usually does not print Ca2+ or F- inside each element box. Instead, use the element's group (vertical column) to predict how many valence electrons a main-group atom gains or loses.

Visual charge map for common main-group ions
Periodic-table group 1 2 13 15 16 17 18
Common charge +1 +2
Ca		 +3 -3 -2 -1
F		 0
What atoms usually do Lose electrons → positive cations Gain electrons → negative anions Already stable

Scope note: this pattern is for common main-group ions. Transition metals can have more than one charge and will be taught separately.

Derive Ca2+ step by step:
  1. Find Ca (calcium) on the periodic table.
  2. Calcium is in Group 2, so it has two valence electrons.
  3. It is easier for calcium to lose two electrons than to gain six.
  4. A neutral calcium atom has equal protons and electrons. After losing two electrons, it has two more protons than electrons.
  5. Therefore its charge is +2, written Ca2+.
Derive F- step by step:
  1. Find F (fluorine) on the periodic table.
  2. Fluorine is in Group 17, so it has seven valence electrons.
  3. Fluorine gains one electron to reach a stable set of eight valence electrons.
  4. It then has one more electron than proton, so its charge is -1, written F-.

4. Use ion charges to derive CaF2

  1. Write the ions with charges: Ca2+ and F-.
  2. One Ca2+ contributes +2. One F- contributes only -1, so one fluoride is not enough: +2 + (-1) = +1.
  3. Add a second F-: +2 + (-1) + (-1) = 0.
  4. The least neutral ratio is 1 calcium ion to 2 fluoride ions.
  5. Use that ratio as subscripts: Ca1F2. Do not write the subscript 1, so the formula is CaF2.

5. Ionic formulas must be neutral

The subscripts show the least whole-number ratio that makes total charge zero. For Mg2+ and Cl-, one Mg2+ requires two Cl-: (+2) + 2(-1) = 0, so the formula is MgCl2.

6. How to draw a repeating ionic lattice model

A lattice is a repeating arrangement, not one isolated molecule. The checkpoint uses CaF2. One formula unit contains one Ca2+ for every two F- ions. Two repeated formula units therefore require two Ca2+ and four F- ions.

Visual model — two repeating CaF2 units
F- — Ca2+ — F-
    |    
F- — Ca2+ — F-

Color key: red circles are Ca2+ cations; blue circles are F- anions. The lines show nearest-neighbor attraction, not covalent bonds.

Draw it step by step:
  1. Write and circle one Ca2+ near the center.
  2. Place one circled F- on each side so opposite charges are neighbors: F- — Ca2+ — F-.
  3. Repeat the pattern below it with a second Ca2+ and two more F- ions.
  4. Label every ion with its symbol and charge. Do not draw neutral Ca or F atoms.
  5. Count the model: 2 Ca2+ gives +4 and 4 F- gives -4. The total is zero.
  6. Show repetition by continuing the alternating arrangement or adding small arrows at the edges.

Checkpoint connection: when asked to draw the lattice, copy this construction method, but produce your own neat labeled model on the checkpoint answer sheet.
