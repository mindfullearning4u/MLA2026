Worked Example 1

Problem: A student claims, 'The unknown liquid is probably water.' The evidence is: clear liquid, boiling point 100 deg C, density 1.00 g/mL.

  1. Step 1: Identify the claim: The liquid is probably water.
  2. Separate weak evidence from strong evidence: Clear appearance is weak because many liquids are clear.
  3. Use measured evidence: Boiling point and density are stronger because they can be compared to known water properties.
  4. Write reasoning: Water has a boiling point near 100 deg C at standard pressure and density near 1.00 g/mL, so these measurements support the claim.
  5. Interpretation: The claim is supported, but 'probably' is appropriate because a full identification may need more tests.

Worked Example 2

Problem: A website says a supplement is safe because it is natural. What evidence is missing?

  1. Identify the claim: the supplement is safe.
  2. Notice the support: 'natural' is a label, not data.
  3. Ask what Chemistry evidence is needed: substance identity, concentration, dose, exposure, and reliable safety data.
  4. Conclusion: the claim is not well supported by the evidence given.

Worked Example 3

Problem: Three trials measure a sample mass as 10.2 g, 10.1 g, and 10.2 g. Does this support a claim that the mass is about 10.2 g?

  1. Read the data: the three values are close together.
  2. Connect to precision: close repeated measurements show precision.
  3. Choose a reasonable claim: the mass is about 10.2 g.
  4. Reasoning: repeated values near 10.2 g support that estimate.
Common Mistake: Assuming a scientific-sounding claim is reliable even when no measurement, data, method, or source evidence is provided.
Incorrect: The cleaner is chemical free, so it must be safer than other cleaners.
Correct: The claim 'chemical free' is not reliable because all matter is made of chemicals. A stronger safety conclusion would need the cleaner's ingredients, concentration, exposure information, and safety data.

Teachable Explanation

Each worked example follows the same evidence path: claim, evidence, reasoning, conclusion. That order keeps the answer from becoming a guess.
