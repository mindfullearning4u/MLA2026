Continue the Teaching

A common Chemistry error is treating every observation as proof. Observations are important, but they are not automatically conclusions. If bubbles appear, the evidence says bubbles appeared. To claim a gas formed, you need reasoning: bubbles may indicate gas production if the procedure and context rule out simple boiling or trapped air.

Source reliability also matters. A source is stronger when it gives data, methods, and specific substance information. A source is weaker when it uses vague words such as safe, natural, pure, or chemical free without evidence.

Reasoning Steps for This Lesson

  1. Write the claim in one sentence.
  2. List the evidence exactly as given, including units if present.
  3. Label each evidence item as observation, measurement, repeated data, or source information.
  4. Choose the strongest evidence, not the most dramatic wording.
  5. Explain how the evidence connects to the claim.
Common Mistake: Assuming a scientific-sounding claim is reliable even when no measurement, data, method, or source evidence is provided.
Incorrect: The cleaner is chemical free, so it must be safer than other cleaners.
Correct: The claim 'chemical free' is not reliable because all matter is made of chemicals. A stronger safety conclusion would need the cleaner's ingredients, concentration, exposure information, and safety data.

Teachable Explanation

The correct example does not reject the product automatically. It rejects the unsupported reasoning. Chemistry decisions need specific evidence, not vague labels.
