Worked Example 1

Problem: Rewrite this step: 'Add some water to the beaker.'

  1. Step 1: Identify the missing amount: 'some' is not measurable.
  2. Identify the tool needed: liquid volume should be measured with a graduated cylinder.
  3. Choose a clear amount and unit: 20.0 mL.
  4. Write the improved step: Use a graduated cylinder to measure 20.0 mL of water, then pour it into a clean beaker.
  5. Why this works: another student can repeat the same action with the same amount.
  6. Interpretation: Explain what the chemistry evidence shows and why it supports the conclusion.

Worked Example 2

Problem: A procedure says, 'Heat the unknown powder until it smokes.' What is wrong?

  1. Identify the hazard: unknown substance plus heat plus smoke.
  2. Identify missing controls: no PPE, ventilation, material identity, or assignment.
  3. Safety decision: do not perform the step as written.
  4. Better alternative: use provided observation data or a teacher-assigned demonstration record.

Worked Example 3

Problem: Which tool should measure 15.0 g of salt in a written scenario?

  1. Read the unit: grams measure mass.
  2. Choose the tool for mass: balance.
  3. Reject volume tools: a graduated cylinder measures mL, not g.
  4. Answer: use a balance and record the mass in grams.
Common Mistake: Thinking a procedure is acceptable because the general idea is clear, even when amounts, tools, units, order, or safety controls are missing.
Incorrect: Heat the liquid until it changes, then write down what happened.
Correct: Do not heat an unknown liquid without assignment. A safe data-based alternative is to analyze a provided temperature table or assigned demonstration data.

Teachable Explanation

The examples show that a good procedure is not just a list of actions. It is a controlled plan with correct tools, units, order, and safety decisions.
