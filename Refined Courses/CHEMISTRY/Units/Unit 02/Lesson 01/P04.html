Three Worked Evidence-to-Model Examples

Study all three worked examples. You do not submit work from this page. Each example follows the same reasoning pattern: prediction, observation, inference, and limitation.

Example 1 - Cathode-ray evidence

  1. Prediction: If atoms contain no charged internal particles, an electric field should not bend a beam produced inside the tube.
  2. Observation: The cathode ray bends toward the positive plate and away from the negative plate.
  3. Inference: The beam contains negatively charged particles. Because the same particles appear with different electrode materials, electrons are present in all atoms.
  4. Model revision: Dalton's indivisible solid sphere cannot explain electrons; Thomson's model adds internal negative particles.
  5. Limitation: This evidence shows electrons exist but does not locate a nucleus.

Model explanation: “The direction of bending supports negatively charged electrons because opposite charges attract. The evidence changes the atom from indivisible to a structure containing smaller particles.”

Example 2 - Rutherford scattering

Strongly deflected/backscattered2A tiny dense nucleus produces rare large deflections.
  1. Prediction: Thomson's spread-out positive charge predicts no rare, very large deflections.
  2. Observation: Two particles backscatter while 982 pass through.
  3. Inference: Most space is empty, but positive charge and most mass occupy a tiny nucleus.
  4. Limitation: Scattering data do not show exact electron positions.

Model explanation: “The nuclear model explains both the many straight paths and the rare strong deflections. A spread-out positive charge cannot explain the backscattering.”

Example 3 - Line-spectrum evidence

A heated hydrogen sample emits four visible lines rather than every color: approximately 410 nm, 434 nm, 486 nm, and 656 nm.

  1. Prediction: If electrons could possess any energy, emitted light would form a continuous spectrum.
  2. Observation: Only specific wavelengths appear.
  3. Inference: Electrons can occupy specific allowed energy levels; each line represents one allowed energy change.
  4. Model revision: Bohr's model adds quantized energy levels to the nuclear atom.
  5. Limitation: The simple Bohr model works best for hydrogen and does not fully describe multi-electron atoms.

Model explanation: “The separate spectral lines support quantized energy because each wavelength corresponds to a specific energy difference. A continuous set of electron energies would not produce separate lines.”

Reusable reasoning pattern: State the older model's prediction, identify the observation that does or does not match, make the narrowest supported inference, and name one limitation.
