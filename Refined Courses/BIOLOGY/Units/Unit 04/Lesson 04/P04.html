Worked Example 1: Complete a cross

Problem: Parents Aa and Aa. What genotypes are possible?

  1. Step 1: Put A and a for one parent across the top. Why: each parent contributes one allele.
  2. Step 2: Put A and a for the other parent on the side. Why: every box combines one allele from each.
  3. Step 3: Fill boxes: AA, Aa, Aa, aa. Why: these are all combinations.

Interpretation: The genotype ratio is 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa.

Worked Example 2: Find phenotype probability

Problem: Using Aa x Aa, what percent show dominant phenotype?

  1. Step 1: Use completed boxes. Why: phenotype comes from genotype.
  2. Step 2: Count AA and Aa as dominant. Why: one dominant allele shows the dominant trait.
  3. Step 3: Three of four boxes are dominant phenotype. Why: 3/4 equals 75%.

Interpretation: Dominant phenotype probability is 75% in this model.

Worked Example 3: Explain recessive phenotype

Problem: Why does aa show the recessive trait?

  1. Step 1: Identify both alleles. Why: genotype has two lowercase alleles.
  2. Step 2: Notice no dominant allele is present. Why: dominant allele would mask recessive in this simple model.
  3. Step 3: State recessive phenotype appears. Why: recessive trait shows when genotype is homozygous recessive.

Interpretation: The recessive phenotype requires two recessive alleles in this model.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: "A Punnett square tells exactly which child will have which trait."

Correct: "A Punnett square predicts probabilities for possible offspring genotypes and phenotypes."

Teachable explanation: Inheritance predictions are probability models, not guarantees for one individual.
