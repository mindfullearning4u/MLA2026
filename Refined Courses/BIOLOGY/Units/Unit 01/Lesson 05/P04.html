Worked Example 1: Rate Source Reliability

Problem: A blog claims a plant supplement doubles bean plant growth. The blog sells the supplement and shows no data. Is the source reliable for the claim?

  1. Step 1: Identify the claim. The claim is that the supplement doubles growth. Why: You must know what statement the source is trying to prove.
  2. Step 2: Check the source purpose. This matters because selling the product can create bias. Why: A source may still be useful, but possible bias means the evidence must be checked carefully.
  3. Step 3: Look for evidence. No data are shown, so the claim is unsupported. Why: Scientific reliability depends on relevant evidence, not confidence or advertising.

Interpretation: The source is weak for this claim because it has possible bias and no measurable evidence.

Worked Example 2: Choose the Better Explanation

Problem: Which explanation is better supported by the table?

WeekNitrateAlgae Coverage
12 mg/L10%
212 mg/L45%
  1. Step 1: Find the pattern. Nitrate and algae both increased. Why: A supported explanation must match the trend in the data.
  2. Step 2: Match explanations to evidence. A nutrient explanation matches the nitrate change. Why: Nitrate is a measured nutrient, so it is directly relevant to the claim.
  3. Step 3: Avoid claiming proof beyond the data. The table supports a likely explanation but does not prove every cause. Why: Accurate scientific arguments do not overstate what the evidence can show.

Interpretation: Increased nutrients are the better supported explanation because the measured nitrate increase matches the algae increase.

Worked Example 3: Write a CER Argument

Problem: Write a CER response for this claim: "The source with measured pond data is more useful than the opinion paragraph."

  1. Step 1, Claim: The measured data source is more useful for evaluating the pond algae claim. Why: The claim directly answers which source is stronger.
  2. Step 2, Evidence: It reports nitrate values and algae coverage over time, while the opinion paragraph gives no measurements. Why: Evidence must include checkable details, not just a conclusion.
  3. Step 3, Reasoning: Scientific arguments need evidence that can support or challenge a claim. Measurements allow comparison. Why: Reasoning explains why measured data make the source more reliable.

Interpretation: The answer is strong because it compares source quality and explains why evidence matters.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: "The best source is the one that agrees with my claim."

Correct: "The best source is the one with relevant, reliable evidence, even if it challenges my first claim."

Teachable explanation: Scientific argument is not about protecting an opinion. It is about following the best evidence.
