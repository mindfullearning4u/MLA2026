Worked Example 1: Identify Evidence

Problem: A student claims, "Plants grow faster when they receive more light." Which information is the strongest evidence for the claim?

  1. Step 1: Find the claim. The claim is about light and plant growth. Why: You must know exactly what idea the evidence is supposed to support or challenge.
  2. Step 2: Look for measured data. Strong evidence should compare plant growth under different light conditions. Why: Measurements are stronger than opinions because they can be checked and compared.
  3. Step 3: Choose evidence that matches the claim. A table showing plant heights after different amounts of light directly supports or challenges the claim. Why: Evidence must connect to the same variables named in the claim.
Light per Day Average Plant Height After 10 Days
2 hours6 cm
8 hours14 cm

Answer: The data table is evidence because it includes measured plant growth under different light conditions.

Interpretation: This evidence is useful because it compares the exact factor in the claim, light, with the exact result in the claim, plant growth.

Worked Example 2: Decide Whether a Question Is Testable

Problem: Is this a scientific question? "Do mealworms move faster at room temperature or in a cooler environment?"

  1. Step 1: Identify what can be measured. Movement speed can be measured by distance over time. Why: A scientific question needs an observable or measurable outcome.
  2. Step 2: Identify what can be changed. Temperature condition can be changed in a controlled investigation or represented with provided data. Why: A testable question compares conditions instead of asking for a personal opinion.
  3. Step 3: Decide if evidence can answer it. Yes. Data from each temperature condition could answer the question. Why: If evidence can answer the question, the question can be investigated scientifically.

Answer: Yes, the question is testable because movement speed and temperature condition can be measured.

Interpretation: The question is scientific even before the answer is known because it can be investigated with evidence.

Worked Example 3: Build a CER Response

Problem: Use the table to write a claim, evidence, and reasoning statement.

CupDaily LightAverage Height
A8 hours14 cm
B2 hours6 cm
  1. Step 1: Claim. More daily light increased seedling growth in this investigation. Why: The claim gives the direct answer to the investigation question.
  2. Step 2: Evidence. Cup A received 8 hours of light and averaged 14 cm. Cup B received 2 hours of light and averaged 6 cm. Why: These numbers are the measured support for the claim.
  3. Step 3: Reasoning. Plants use light during photosynthesis, so different light exposure can affect growth when other conditions are controlled. Why: Reasoning explains the biology idea that connects the data to the claim.

Interpretation: A strong CER response does not just list numbers. It explains why the numbers matter.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: "The plant grew because plants are alive."

This is too vague. It does not use the data, and it does not explain the relationship between light and growth.

Correct: "The plant with more light grew taller; the table shows 14 cm compared with 6 cm, and light supports photosynthesis."

Teachable explanation: The correct statement uses the measured evidence and connects it to a biology process.
