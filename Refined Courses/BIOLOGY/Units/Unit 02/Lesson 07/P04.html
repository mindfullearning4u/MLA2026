Worked Example 1: Analyze transmission

Problem: A diagram shows droplets moving from an infected person to a nearby person. What does it model?

  1. Step 1: Identify the movement: droplets travel between people. Why: transmission is movement of pathogens.
  2. Step 2: Identify the possible agent: pathogen in droplets. Why: pathogens can spread through respiratory droplets.
  3. Step 3: Classify the model as transmission. Why: the diagram shows a pathway of spread.

Interpretation: Transmission diagrams show how spread can happen.

Worked Example 2: Evaluate a health claim

Problem: A post says a product prevents all infections but gives no data. How reliable is it?

  1. Step 1: Identify the claim: prevents all infections. Why: the claim is broad.
  2. Step 2: Check evidence: no data are provided. Why: claims need support.
  3. Step 3: Rate reliability as weak. Why: broad claims without evidence are not strong science.

Interpretation: Reliable health claims require evidence.

Worked Example 3: Connect immunity

Problem: A model shows immune cells recognizing a pathogen marker and responding faster after prior exposure. What concept is shown?

  1. Step 1: Identify recognition. Why: immune response depends on recognizing invaders.
  2. Step 2: Identify faster response after exposure. Why: prior exposure can prepare immune defenses.
  3. Step 3: Connect to immunity. Why: the model shows a targeted response.

Interpretation: Immune models explain defense using recognition and response.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: "A health claim is true if it sounds urgent."

Correct: "A health claim is stronger when it uses reliable evidence, a clear biological mechanism, or relevant data."

Teachable explanation: Urgency is not evidence. Biology uses data, models, and source reliability.
