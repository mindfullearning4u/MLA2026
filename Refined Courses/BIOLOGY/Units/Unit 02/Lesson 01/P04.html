Worked Example 1: Classify a cell with no nucleus

Problem: A diagram shows DNA in the cytoplasm and no nucleus. Classify the cell.

  1. Step 1: Look for a nucleus. No nucleus is shown. Why: nucleus presence is the first major comparison.
  2. Step 2: Check whether the DNA is inside a membrane-bound nucleus. It is not. Why: free DNA supports prokaryotic classification.
  3. Step 3: Classify the cell as prokaryotic. Why: prokaryotic cells lack a nucleus.

Interpretation: The absence of a nucleus is the strongest evidence.

Worked Example 2: Identify a plant cell

Problem: A diagram has a nucleus, cell wall, chloroplasts, and a large vacuole. What type of cell is it?

  1. Step 1: Confirm it is eukaryotic because it has a nucleus. Why: only eukaryotic cells have a nucleus.
  2. Step 2: Use plant-only evidence: cell wall and chloroplasts. Why: those structures separate plant cells from animal cells.
  3. Step 3: Classify it as a plant cell. Why: multiple plant features agree.

Interpretation: Several matching features make the classification stronger.

Worked Example 3: Compare plant and animal cells

Problem: Both diagrams show a nucleus and mitochondria, but only one shows chloroplasts. Which is plant?

  1. Step 1: Find shared structures first. Both have nucleus and mitochondria. Why: shared structures do not distinguish them.
  2. Step 2: Find the difference. One has chloroplasts. Why: chloroplasts perform photosynthesis in plant cells.
  3. Step 3: Select the chloroplast cell as plant. Why: the distinguishing feature matches the plant cell model.

Interpretation: Comparison questions require finding both similarities and differences.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: "This cell is a plant cell because it is shaped like a box."

Correct: "This cell is a plant cell because it has a cell wall and chloroplasts."

Teachable explanation: Use labeled structures as evidence. Shape can support a guess, but structures make the classification scientifically stronger.
