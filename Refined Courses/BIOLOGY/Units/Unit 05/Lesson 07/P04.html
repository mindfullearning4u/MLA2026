Worked Example 1: Identify bipedal evidence

Problem: A pelvis and foot fossil show upright walking adaptations. What trend is supported?

  1. Step 1: Identify the structures. Why: pelvis and foot are locomotion evidence.
  2. Step 2: Connect to upright walking. Why: those traits support bipedalism.
  3. Step 3: State bipedal trend. Why: evidence supports walking pattern.

Interpretation: Skeletal evidence can support bipedalism.

Worked Example 2: Use cranial data

Problem: A chart shows average cranial capacity increasing across several later hominid groups. What is the careful conclusion?

  1. Step 1: Read the pattern. Why: trend comes from data.
  2. Step 2: Use cautious wording. Why: averages do not describe every individual.
  3. Step 3: Connect to trend, not superiority. Why: biology avoids value ranking.

Interpretation: The chart supports a cranial capacity trend.

Worked Example 3: Avoid overclaiming

Problem: A fossil is older than another fossil. Does that prove it is a direct ancestor?

  1. Step 1: Identify age evidence. Why: age is relevant.
  2. Step 2: Recognize missing relationship evidence. Why: age alone is not enough.
  3. Step 3: State more evidence is needed. Why: ancestry requires multiple evidence lines.

Interpretation: Age alone does not prove direct ancestry.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: "Every older hominid fossil is a direct ancestor of every newer hominid."

Correct: "Fossils can show trends and relationships, but direct ancestry requires careful evidence and is not assumed from age alone."

Teachable explanation: Trend data supports patterns; it does not automatically prove direct ancestor-descendant relationships.
