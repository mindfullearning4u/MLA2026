Worked Example 1: Identify evidence

Problem: A simulation produces amino acids under early Earth-like conditions. What does it support?

  1. Step 1: Identify the result. Why: amino acids were produced.
  2. Step 2: Connect to organic molecules. Why: amino acids are building blocks.
  3. Step 3: Limit the claim. Why: this does not prove every step to living cells.

Interpretation: The model supports possible organic molecule formation.

Worked Example 2: Recognize a limit

Problem: A model forms vesicle-like structures but no complete cells. What is the limit?

  1. Step 1: Identify what formed. Why: vesicles are the evidence.
  2. Step 2: Identify what did not form. Why: limits matter.
  3. Step 3: State the model supports only part of the explanation. Why: evidence must not be overstated.

Interpretation: Evidence limits keep explanations accurate.

Worked Example 3: Stay scientific

Problem: Why should the answer use evidence tables instead of personal opinion?

  1. Step 1: Identify the task as science. Why: standards require scientific explanation.
  2. Step 2: Use testable evidence. Why: science relies on evidence.
  3. Step 3: Avoid substituting debate for benchmark content. Why: the lesson assesses evidence-based models.

Interpretation: Scientific explanations must be evidence-based.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: "A scientific model must answer every origin question completely or it is useless."

Correct: "A scientific model can support part of an explanation while still having limits."

Teachable explanation: Science often builds explanations from partial, testable evidence and revises them as evidence improves.
