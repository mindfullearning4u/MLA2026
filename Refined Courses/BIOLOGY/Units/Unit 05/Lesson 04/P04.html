Worked Example 1: Find closest relatives

Problem: On a cladogram, species A and B share the most recent branch point. What does that mean?

  1. Step 1: Find the branch point. Why: it shows common ancestry.
  2. Step 2: Compare branch points with other species. Why: recent shared branch means closer relationship.
  3. Step 3: State A and B are closest relatives in this model. Why: they share the most recent common ancestor.

Interpretation: Closest relationship comes from most recent common ancestry.

Worked Example 2: Use a derived trait

Problem: A trait appears before species C and D branch apart. Who has the trait?

  1. Step 1: Locate the trait mark. Why: traits apply after their position.
  2. Step 2: Follow branches after the mark. Why: descendants inherit the trait in the model.
  3. Step 3: Name C and D if both are past the mark. Why: both branches include the trait.

Interpretation: Derived trait marks help interpret groups.

Worked Example 3: Explain revision

Problem: Why might classification change after DNA analysis?

  1. Step 1: Identify new evidence. Why: classification depends on evidence.
  2. Step 2: Compare DNA to older appearance-based grouping. Why: appearance can mislead.
  3. Step 3: Revise if DNA better supports different relationships. Why: science updates explanations with stronger evidence.

Interpretation: Classification systems can change with new evidence.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: "The organism farthest right on a cladogram is the most evolved."

Correct: "A cladogram shows relationships and common ancestry, not a ranking from less evolved to more evolved."

Teachable explanation: All living lineages have been evolving; position on a diagram is not a value ranking.
