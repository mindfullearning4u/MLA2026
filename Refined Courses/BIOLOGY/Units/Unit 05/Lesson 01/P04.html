Worked Example 1: Use fossil evidence

Problem: A fossil layer shows older horse relatives with smaller body size and different toes. What does this support?

  1. Step 1: Identify the evidence source. Why: fossils show past life.
  2. Step 2: Compare older and newer forms. Why: change through layers matters.
  3. Step 3: Connect to population change over time. Why: evolution is change across generations.

Interpretation: Fossils can support change over time.

Worked Example 2: Use molecular evidence

Problem: Two species have very similar DNA sequences. What can this suggest?

  1. Step 1: Read the molecular comparison. Why: DNA similarity is evidence.
  2. Step 2: Connect similarity to relatedness. Why: closer relatives often share more sequence similarity.
  3. Step 3: Avoid overclaiming. Why: DNA is one evidence line.

Interpretation: Molecular similarity can support shared ancestry.

Worked Example 3: Combine evidence

Problem: Fossils, homologous limbs, and DNA data all support relatedness. Why is that strong?

  1. Step 1: Name each evidence type. Why: multiple sources are being compared.
  2. Step 2: Check whether they support the same claim. Why: agreement strengthens reasoning.
  3. Step 3: State the supported claim. Why: evidence must connect to a conclusion.

Interpretation: Converging evidence strengthens scientific explanations.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: "One picture of a fossil proves every detail of evolution."

Correct: "Several independent evidence lines can support a stronger explanation of evolutionary change."

Teachable explanation: Scientific claims are stronger when multiple evidence sources point toward the same explanation.
