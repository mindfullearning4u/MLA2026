Worked Example 1: Identify secondary succession

Problem: A forest fire removes trees, but soil remains and grasses grow the next year. What process is shown?

  1. Step 1: Identify disturbance. Why: fire changed the ecosystem.
  2. Step 2: Check for soil. Why: soil remaining indicates secondary succession.
  3. Step 3: Name early colonizers. Why: grasses often appear early.

Interpretation: Soil remaining after disturbance supports secondary succession.

Worked Example 2: Read a climate graph

Problem: A graph shows average temperature increasing over 40 years. What type of evidence is this?

  1. Step 1: Read the time scale. Why: 40 years is long-term.
  2. Step 2: Identify trend. Why: sustained increase matters.
  3. Step 3: Connect to climate. Why: long-term patterns support climate-related conclusions.

Interpretation: Long-term data can support climate change analysis.

Worked Example 3: Use seasonal data

Problem: A pond has more algae in summer than winter each year. What should you consider first?

  1. Step 1: Identify repeated yearly pattern. Why: repetition suggests seasonality.
  2. Step 2: Connect sunlight/temperature to algae. Why: conditions affect growth.
  3. Step 3: Avoid overclaiming. Why: seasonal pattern alone is not a full climate claim.

Interpretation: Seasonal variation can explain repeated annual changes.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: "Any ecosystem change is automatically climate change."

Correct: "The type of ecosystem change depends on the evidence, time scale, and starting conditions."

Teachable explanation: Students must distinguish seasonal variation, disturbance recovery, succession, and long-term climate trends.
