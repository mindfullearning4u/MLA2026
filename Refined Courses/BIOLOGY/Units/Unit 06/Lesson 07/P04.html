P04 Worked Example

Worked Example 1: Use water data

Problem: A river table shows dissolved oxygen falling below fish survival needs. What policy goal is supported?

  1. Step 1: Read the indicator. Why: dissolved oxygen affects aquatic animals.
  2. Step 2: Compare to survival needs. Why: evidence shows risk.
  3. Step 3: Recommend reducing the cause if identified. Why: policy should address the problem.

Interpretation: Monitoring data can support water-quality policy.

Worked Example 2: Use baseline data

Problem: A site had 80% wetland cover in 2000 and 45% in 2025. Why is the baseline useful?

  1. Step 1: Identify starting value. Why: baseline allows comparison.
  2. Step 2: Compute direction of change. Why: wetland cover decreased.
  3. Step 3: Connect to policy. Why: land-use decisions need trend evidence.

Interpretation: Baseline data helps measure environmental change.

Worked Example 3: Name missing evidence

Problem: A policy claim says pollution is increasing, but only one data point is provided. What is missing?

  1. Step 1: Identify the claim. Why: increasing means trend.
  2. Step 2: Check evidence. Why: one point cannot show trend.
  3. Step 3: Ask for repeated measurements. Why: monitoring requires comparison over time.

Interpretation: Trend claims need repeated monitoring data.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: "Environmental policy should be chosen before looking at data."

Correct: "Environmental policy should be supported by monitoring data, trend evidence, costs, benefits, and limits."

Teachable explanation: Policy decisions are stronger when the evidence explains the problem and supports the response.
