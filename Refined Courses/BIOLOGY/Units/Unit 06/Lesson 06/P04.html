  • SC.912.L.17.11: Costs and benefits of renewable and nonrenewable resources.
  • SC.912.L.17.20: Individual impacts, lifestyle, and sustainability.

Worked Example 1: Compare resources

Problem: A table shows coal has high energy output and high emissions, while solar has low emissions but variable output. What is the tradeoff?

  1. Step 1: Identify benefits. Why: energy output and reliability matter.
  2. Step 2: Identify costs. Why: emissions and variability matter.
  3. Step 3: State balanced comparison. Why: sustainability uses tradeoffs.

Interpretation: Resource choices involve costs and benefits.

Worked Example 2: Evaluate an action

Problem: A household reduces electricity use by 20%. What sustainability impact is supported?

  1. Step 1: Read the percent change. Why: data shows reduced use.
  2. Step 2: Connect to resource demand. Why: lower use can reduce demand.
  3. Step 3: Avoid claiming it solves all problems. Why: one action has limited scope.

Interpretation: Conservation can reduce resource demand.

Worked Example 3: Use evidence

Problem: Why should a policy recommendation include data?

  1. Step 1: Identify decision claim. Why: recommendation needs support.
  2. Step 2: Use cost-benefit evidence. Why: impacts can be compared.
  3. Step 3: Include limitation. Why: no decision is impact-free.

Interpretation: Evidence makes sustainability recommendations defensible.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: "Renewable resources have no environmental costs."

Correct: "Renewable resources may reduce some impacts but still require evidence-based cost-benefit analysis."

Teachable explanation: Sustainability reasoning weighs benefits, costs, and possible mitigation.
