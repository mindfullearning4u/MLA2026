Note-Taking, Paraphrase, and Source Synthesis

Direct Teaching

Researchers do more than collect facts. They select useful evidence, record it accurately, restate it in their own words, and connect ideas from more than one source.

Step 1: Start with the research question

Write the question at the top of your notes. Every note must help answer it. If your question is How can schools reduce cafeteria waste?, a detail about tray waste is relevant; a detail about school mascots is not.

Step 2: Record a traceable note

For every note, record the source title or author, the page or paragraph when available, and whether the wording is a direct quotation, paraphrase, or summary. This prevents accidental plagiarism and makes later citation easier.

Step 3: Choose quote, paraphrase, or summary

Quote

Use exact words when the wording is distinctive, authoritative, or necessary to analyze. Copy exactly and use quotation marks.

Paraphrase

Restate a focused idea completely in your own wording and sentence structure while preserving its meaning.

Summary

Condense the main idea of a larger section. Include only the most important points.

Step 4: Build a true paraphrase

  1. Read the source until you understand it.
  2. Look away from the source and state the idea from memory.
  3. Change both the wording and sentence structure.
  4. Compare your version with the original for accuracy.
  5. Add a source signal or citation.

Step 5: Synthesize sources

Group notes by idea—not by source. Ask how the sources agree, differ, qualify one another, or reveal a cause-and-effect relationship. A synthesis sentence names that relationship.

Common Mistakes

  • Patchwriting: changing only a few words from the original.
  • Distortion: leaving out a limitation that changes the meaning.
  • Source stacking: listing one source after another without explaining their relationship.
  • Orphan evidence: inserting evidence without analysis or a source signal.
