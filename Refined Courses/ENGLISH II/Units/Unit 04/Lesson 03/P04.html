Worked Examples: From Source Notes to Synthesis

Modeling goal: Watch how a researcher selects relevant notes, paraphrases accurately, and explains relationships among sources.

Source Set

Source A — District Waste Audit: During a four-week lunch pre-order pilot, unwanted meal trays declined by 28 percent. Staff reported that the first week required frequent reminders because many students forgot to submit selections.

Source B — Student Council Survey: Sixty-four percent of surveyed students said they would use a pre-order system regularly. Students who opposed it most often cited uncertainty about choosing meals early.

Source C — Cafeteria Manager Interview: The manager explained that predictable orders help staff prepare the right quantities, but the system works only when students receive clear menus and reminders.

Worked Example 1: Select useful notes

  1. Research question: How can a pre-order system reduce cafeteria waste?
  2. Relevant note: “Unwanted trays declined 28% during the pilot” (Source A).
  3. Limitation note: Students needed reminders during week one (Source A).
  4. Reasoning: Both notes matter because a complete answer must show the result and the condition needed for success.

Worked Example 2: Paraphrase without patchwriting

Weak: During the four-week pilot, unwanted trays decreased by 28 percent. This copies the original structure and most of its words.
Strong: The district’s month-long trial produced noticeably less discarded food, with unused meals falling by more than one quarter (Source A).
Why it works: The wording and structure are new, the number remains accurate, and the source is credited.

Worked Example 3: Synthesize sources

Draft: Evidence across the three sources suggests that advance ordering can reduce waste when schools also make participation easy. The audit shows a measurable decline in unused meals, while student responses and the manager’s experience explain why clear menus and reminders are necessary (Sources A–C).

Step-by-step explanation:

  1. The first sentence states a combined conclusion.
  2. The second sentence assigns a different role to each source.
  3. The word while signals a relationship rather than merely listing sources.
  4. The explanation preserves the program’s success and its limitation.
Common mistake: “Source A says… Source B says… Source C says…” is a list, not synthesis. Explain how the ideas work together.
