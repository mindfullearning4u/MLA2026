P03 — Lesson: Evaluating Sources

A source is useful only when it fits the research question and provides trustworthy evidence. Reliability, credibility, and validity overlap, but they are not identical.

The Source-Evaluation Process

  1. Check relevance. Does the source directly address the research question?
  2. Check authority and credibility. Who created it? What qualifications, experience, or institutional responsibility support the author?
  3. Check accuracy and evidence. Are claims supported by data, documented examples, citations, or methods that can be checked?
  4. Check currency. Is the date appropriate for the topic? Current statistics may matter for changing conditions; older primary sources may be essential for historical questions.
  5. Check purpose and bias. Was the source created to inform, sell, entertain, or advocate? What language, evidence, or perspectives are emphasized or omitted?
  6. Check validity. Does the evidence actually support the conclusion for which it is being used?
  7. Corroborate. Compare important claims with independent sources.

Important Distinctions

  • A credible author can still make a claim that is not valid for your question.
  • A biased source is not automatically useless; it may reveal a perspective, but its claims require careful verification.
  • An accurate source can be irrelevant. A current bus schedule may be correct but cannot prove that later buses improve job access.
  • A source can be old and still valuable when it is a primary source or when the information has not changed.

Reading Language for Bias

Loaded words such as obviously, disastrous, everyone knows, or perfect solution can signal an attempt to shape reaction. Also check for omitted evidence, one-sided examples, exaggerated certainty, and financial or political interests.

Common Mistakes

  • Choosing the first source that agrees with your opinion.
  • Judging credibility only by professional-looking design.
  • Confusing popularity with expertise.
  • Using a statistic without checking who collected it, how, or when.
  • Rejecting every source with a viewpoint instead of evaluating its evidence and purpose.
