Source Integration, Purposeful Transitions, Tone, and Voice

Direct Teaching

Integrated evidence becomes part of your explanation. It is introduced, credited, connected to surrounding ideas, and followed by analysis.

Step 1: Establish the point before the evidence

Begin with a claim or topic sentence so readers know why the evidence matters.

Step 2: Choose an integration method

  • Signal phrase: names the author or source before a quote or paraphrase.
  • Embedded quotation: blends a short exact phrase into your own sentence.
  • Paraphrase: restates a source idea in original wording and structure.
  • Synthesis: places evidence from multiple sources in conversation.

Step 3: Use the evidence sandwich

  1. Introduce the source and context.
  2. Present the quotation, paraphrase, or statistic accurately.
  3. Explain how the evidence supports the point.

Step 4: Select transitions by meaning

Use similarly for agreement, however for contrast or limitation, for example for illustration, therefore for a supported result, and in addition for a related point. Do not choose transitions merely for variety.

Step 5: Control tone and voice

Maintain a voice appropriate to purpose and audience. Use precise verbs, measured claims, consistent point of view, and respectful language. Avoid slang, unsupported certainty, and sudden shifts from formal explanation to personal reaction.

Common Mistakes

  • Dropping a quotation into a paragraph without context.
  • Using therefore when the evidence does not prove causation.
  • Repeating the evidence instead of analyzing it.
  • Letting the source’s wording overpower the writer’s own voice.
  • Changing tone or point of view within a paragraph.
