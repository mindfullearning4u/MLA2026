Worked Examples: Integrating Evidence Smoothly

Source A: A district pilot found that later library hours increased student visits by 37% on project nights.

Source B: A student survey reported that jobs and athletics often prevented students from arriving before 5:00 p.m.

Worked Example 1: Evidence Sandwich

Point: Later hours can improve access for busy students.

Model: A district pilot found that evening student visits rose by 37% on project nights (Source A). This increase suggests that extended schedules can remove a time barrier for students completing research.

Steps: The signal phrase establishes credibility, the statistic supplies evidence, and the second sentence explains its significance.

Worked Example 2: Purposeful Transition

Weak: Visits increased. Therefore, students had jobs.

Strong: Visits increased during extended hours. In addition, survey responses show that jobs and athletics previously limited access.

Why: The sources add related findings; one does not cause the other, so in addition is accurate and therefore is not.

Worked Example 3: Blend a Short Quotation and Maintain Voice

Source wording: Students described the old schedule as “nearly impossible” after evening practices.

Model: For students attending evening practices, the earlier closing time was “nearly impossible” to meet, illustrating how scheduling limited access (Source B).

  1. Introduce the affected group.
  2. Embed only the distinctive phrase.
  3. Credit the source.
  4. Explain the phrase in the writer’s own academic voice.
Common mistake: “Source A says…” followed by a quotation and no explanation leaves the reader to infer the connection.
