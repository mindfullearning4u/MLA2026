Notebook Task Part 2: Revising for Publication Readiness

Now you will apply the checklist to a draft communication.

Draft to Review

The lunch form helped waste. A report said trays changed. Students should do it. We might use a chart.

Step-by-Step Revision

  1. The first sentence is too vague because it says "helped waste" without naming the exact finding.
  2. The second sentence mentions a report but does not explain what changed or why it matters.
  3. The third sentence gives an action step, but it needs a reason students can understand.
  4. The fourth sentence mentions a chart, but publication-ready writing should explain what the chart will show.
  5. A stronger revision connects source evidence, explanation, audience action, and visual support.

Publication-Ready Revision

A district pilot report found fewer unwanted trays after staff prepared meals from student pre-order selections. This finding matters because each completed form helps the cafeteria prepare meals students are more likely to choose. A simple before-and-after chart can help students see the change quickly.

Common Mistake

Incorrect Example:

The form thing was good, and the report said stuff, so students should just do it.

Correct Example:

The pilot report found fewer unwanted trays when staff used student pre-order selections, so students should complete the form to help the cafeteria prepare meals students are more likely to choose.

Teachable Explanation: The incorrect example is vague, informal, and unsupported. The correct example names the source finding, explains why it matters, and gives the student audience a clear action.
