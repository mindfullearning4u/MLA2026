Worked Examples: Designing for Audience and Purpose

Project Scenario

A student team must publish research about reducing cafeteria waste for students, families, and school leaders.

Worked Example 1: Adapt the Same Finding

Finding: A tray-preorder pilot reduced discarded meals by 28%.

  • Student poster: “Choose before lunch—our pilot cut unwanted meals by 28%.” Use a bold chart and brief action steps.
  • Family newsletter: Explain how preordering works, why reminders matter, and how families can help.
  • Leadership slide: Present the measurement, method, limitation, and implementation cost.

Reasoning: The evidence remains accurate, but detail, tone, and format change with audience needs.

Worked Example 2: Choose Purposeful Media

Weak choice: A decorative photograph of food with no caption.

Strong choice: A two-bar chart comparing discarded trays before and during the pilot, labeled with dates and source.

  1. The chart visualizes change quickly.
  2. The caption explains what was measured.
  3. Alt text communicates the comparison to screen-reader users.

Worked Example 3: Repair a Collaborative Draft

Problem: One slide uses slang, another has a dense paragraph, colors lack contrast, and a statistic has no source.

Revision sequence:

  1. The research lead verifies and credits the statistic.
  2. The editor establishes one measured, student-friendly voice.
  3. The designer converts the paragraph into a headline, three bullets, and white space.
  4. The technical lead improves contrast and adds alt text.
  5. The full team checks purpose, accuracy, accessibility, and navigation.
Common mistake: Dividing slides among team members without a shared style guide often produces a fragmented final publication.
