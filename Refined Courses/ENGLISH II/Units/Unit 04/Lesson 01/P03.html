P03 — Lesson: Building a Focused Research Question

A research question is the plan for an investigation. A strong question tells you what evidence to seek, keeps the project manageable, and invites analysis rather than a one-word answer.

The Five-Step Refinement Process

  1. Start with a broad topic. Name the general subject without trying to solve everything at once.
  2. Choose an inquiry focus. Select one relationship, cause, effect, comparison, or possible solution.
  3. Set the scope. Limit the population, place, time period, or part of the issue.
  4. Make the question open-ended and neutral. Use wording such as how, why, to what extent, or what effects. Remove language that assumes the answer.
  5. Test feasibility and relevance. Ask whether reliable sources can answer the question within the available time and whether every part connects to the research purpose.

How Scope Changes a Question

Topic only: School transportation.

Focused relationship: Transportation and after-school participation.

Scoped question: How does access to a late activity bus affect participation in after-school programs among students at one suburban high school?

The final version identifies the service, the effect, the population, and the location. A researcher can now identify useful evidence.

Main Question and Subquestions

A complex main question becomes manageable when it is divided into subquestions. For the activity-bus question, useful subquestions might ask which students lack transportation, which programs meet after normal bus service, how participation changes when transportation is available, and what the service costs.

Each subquestion supplies one part of the evidence needed to answer the main question.

Common Mistakes and Corrections

  • Too broad: “How does transportation affect society?” Narrow the population, effect, and setting.
  • Too narrow: “What time does bus 14 arrive Tuesday?” Expand the question so it requires analysis.
  • Biased: “Why is a late bus obviously necessary?” Replace the assumption with neutral wording.
  • Not feasible: “How has every transportation system changed throughout history?” Limit the time period and location.
  • Not relevant: Evidence about unrelated school programs should not be included just because it is interesting.
