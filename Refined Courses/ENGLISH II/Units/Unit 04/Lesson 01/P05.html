Research Brief: Reducing Cafeteria Food Waste

Riverview High throws away an estimated 310 pounds of food during a typical five-day school week. A four-week cafeteria audit found that unopened milk, whole fruit, packaged side dishes, and uneaten hot meals made up most of the waste. Waste was greatest on days when a new entrée was served and on days when lunch periods were shortened for assemblies.

The nutrition director is considering three responses. A share table would allow students to place unopened food in a supervised area for other students to take. A pre-order system would let students select an entrée each morning so cafeteria staff could prepare more accurate quantities. A composting program would divert some remaining food from the landfill, although it would require collection bins, staff training, and a community partner.

Student council members also proposed a short education campaign explaining portion choices and food waste. In a survey of 240 students, 58 percent said they sometimes discard food because they do not have enough time to eat, 34 percent said they take items they do not want, and 27 percent said they would use a share table. The categories overlap because students could select more than one response.

The principal wants a research project that can be completed in six weeks and that results in a practical recommendation. Available evidence includes the cafeteria audit, the student survey, interviews with cafeteria staff, records from two nearby schools, and published guidance from state nutrition and environmental agencies. The project cannot test all four solutions at once.

Questions

  1. State the broad topic of the research brief.
  2. Identify four possible inquiry focuses.
  3. Explain why studying all four solutions would create an unmanageable scope.
  4. Choose one inquiry focus and explain why it is relevant to the principal’s purpose.
  5. Write one research question that is too broad.
  6. Explain specifically why that question is too broad.
  7. Write one question that is too narrow or merely factual.
  8. Explain why that question would not support meaningful analysis.
  9. Write one biased question about your chosen focus.
  10. Revise the biased wording so the question becomes neutral and open-ended.
  11. Refine the question by naming a population, location, time frame, effect, comparison, or solution.
  12. Evaluate whether the refined question is feasible in six weeks using the available evidence.
  13. Write three subquestions that would help answer the refined main question.
  14. Match each subquestion with at least one available source from the research brief.
  15. Present your final research question and write a developed paragraph explaining its inquiry focus, scope, relevance, neutrality, feasibility, and research purpose.

Success Check

Your final question must require evidence-based analysis, remain neutral, fit the six-week limit, and point toward sources that are actually available.
