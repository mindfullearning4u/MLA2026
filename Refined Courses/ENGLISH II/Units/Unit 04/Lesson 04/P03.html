Expository Organization and Voice From Research Findings

Direct Teaching

Expository writing explains a focused idea with logically arranged findings, accurate evidence, and a clear academic voice.

Step 1: Turn findings into a controlling idea

Review your notes and identify the relationship that matters most: cause and effect, problem and solution, comparison, sequence, or classification. State that relationship in a precise controlling idea.

Step 2: Choose the structure that matches the purpose

  • Cause and effect: explains why something happens or what it produces.
  • Problem and solution: defines a challenge, evaluates responses, and explains conditions for success.
  • Compare and contrast: organizes meaningful similarities and differences by point.
  • Sequence: explains stages or a process in a logical order.

Step 3: Group evidence into purposeful paragraphs

Each body paragraph needs one subtopic, relevant evidence, and explanation. Do not organize one paragraph per source; organize by ideas so sources can work together.

Step 4: Build paragraph cohesion

  1. Begin with a topic sentence connected to the controlling idea.
  2. Introduce and credit evidence.
  3. Explain what the evidence shows.
  4. Use transitions that name relationships: because, as a result, similarly, in contrast, however, for example.
  5. End by connecting the paragraph back to the purpose.

Step 5: Maintain an effective academic voice

Use precise nouns and verbs, measured claims, varied sentences, and an objective tone. Replace exaggeration such as “always fixes” with qualified language such as “may improve when.” Explain technical terms an audience may not know.

Common Mistakes

  • Listing sources instead of organizing ideas.
  • Dropping evidence into a paragraph without analysis.
  • Using a transition that does not match the relationship.
  • Switching between informal, emotional, and academic voice.
  • Claiming more than the evidence proves.
