Keep the River Open

Context: An adapted historical-style civic speech urges residents to fund restoration of a polluted river.

For generations, this river carried our crops, powered our mills, and gave children a place to learn the shape of their own town. Today, warning signs stand where fishing docks once stood. Shall we allow a source of life to become a boundary no family may cross?

The evidence is plain. Tests at five sites found contamination above safe levels. Clinics recorded rising skin irritation after summer contact, and merchants near the closed landing reported fewer visitors. Pollution is not an invisible inconvenience; it is a measurable cost to health, memory, and livelihood.

I have worked beside engineers and river crews for twenty years. They agree that damaged drainage pipes and untreated runoff are the chief causes. Repairing pipes, planting buffer grasses, and enforcing discharge rules will not solve the problem overnight, but each step has succeeded in comparable towns.

Some say the restoration plan costs too much. But what is the cost of another closed season? What is the cost of teaching children that neglect is cheaper than responsibility? We pay either for restoration now or for deeper damage later.

Let us reopen the river—not for one business, not for one neighborhood, not for one generation. Let us restore it for the farmer, the child, the worker, and every resident who deserves clean water and a living connection to this place.

Questions

  1. Identify speaker, audience, context, and purpose.
  2. State the central claim.
  3. How does the first sentence use shared history?
  4. Analyze the rhetorical question ending paragraph one.
  5. Identify three examples of logos in paragraph two.
  6. How does the phrase “health, memory, and livelihood” broaden the appeal?
  7. Explain how paragraph three establishes ethos.
  8. Why does the speaker acknowledge that repairs will not work overnight?
  9. Identify the counterargument.
  10. Analyze the two repeated rhetorical questions in paragraph four.
  11. Explain the contrast between restoration now and deeper damage later.
  12. What effect does repeated “not for one” create?
  13. How does the final list appeal to the audience?
  14. Identify the dominant tone and two diction choices that create it.
  15. Analyze how ethos, logos, pathos, repetition, contrast, and rhetorical questions work together to accomplish the purpose.
