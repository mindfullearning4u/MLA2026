P03 — Lesson: Rhetorical Choices and Audience Effect

Direct Teaching

Rhetorical analysis explains how a deliberate language choice helps a speaker accomplish a purpose with a particular audience. Naming ethos, pathos, logos, repetition, or a rhetorical question is only the beginning.

A Five-Step Analysis

  1. Identify speaker, audience, context, and purpose.
  2. Locate the exact rhetorical choice.
  3. Explain the choice’s literal meaning and connotations.
  4. Describe its likely effect on this audience.
  5. Connect that effect to the speaker’s purpose.

Example 1 — Repetition and Parallelism

Passage: “We will rebuild the school. We will reopen the clinic. We will restore the bridge that joins our neighborhoods.”

Analysis: Repeated We will creates rhythm and certainty, while parallel clauses make three goals feel connected. The inclusive pronoun unites listeners and supports the purpose of inspiring collective action.

Example 2 — Logos Joined with Ethos

Passage: “During my twelve years treating families at this clinic, I have seen preventable illness decline whenever clean-water inspections increase. Last year, inspected districts reported one-third fewer cases.”

Analysis: Professional experience establishes ethos; the reported decline supplies logos. Together they make the recommendation credible and evidence-based.

Example 3 — Rhetorical Question and Pathos

Passage: “What promise do we make to children if the library doors close before they can reach them? No student should lose opportunity because a bus arrives late.”

Analysis: The question invites listeners to judge a moral responsibility rather than answer aloud. Reference to children and lost opportunity creates concern, helping persuade voters to extend library hours.

Common Mistakes

  • Naming a device without quoting or paraphrasing the choice.
  • Claiming every emotional detail is pathos without explaining the emotion and purpose.
  • Calling a statement logos merely because it sounds reasonable; identify evidence or reasoning.
  • Describing effect vaguely as “gets attention.” Name what the audience may think, feel, trust, or do.
