Light for Every Street

Historical context: In this adapted historical-style speech, a civic leader argues for expanding electric street lighting.

We are told that streetlights are a convenience, useful chiefly to merchants who keep their shops open late. But light on a public street serves more than trade. It allows a nurse to return safely after an evening shift, a student to walk home from study, and a family to travel without surrendering the night to fear.

Our brightest streets now gather around the wealthiest blocks, while workers beyond the center travel roads left in darkness. A public improvement that reaches only those already favored cannot be called fully public. If every household contributes to the city, every neighborhood deserves reasonable protection from it.

Lighting the outer streets will require cost and careful planning. Yet the expense of darkness also appears—in injuries, restricted movement, and opportunities refused because the journey home is unsafe. We must compare not spending with saving, but one cost with another.

Let us therefore extend the lines in stages, beginning with routes used by schools, factories, and clinics. In doing so, we will not merely install lamps. We will declare that safety, movement, and participation belong throughout the city.

Questions

  1. Identify the topic.
  2. State the central idea precisely.
  3. How does the first paragraph broaden the meaning of street lighting?
  4. What does the contrast between wealthy and outer neighborhoods add?
  5. Explain how the sentence about public contribution develops the central idea.
  6. What counterargument does the speaker acknowledge?
  7. How does the speaker answer that counterargument?
  8. Explain how the proposed order of expansion supports the speaker’s purpose.
  9. How does the final sentence refine the central idea?
  10. Write a developed paragraph tracing the central idea through three details and explaining how historical context shapes the appeal.
