P04 Worked Example

Study how each example separates topic, central idea, and supporting details.

Worked Example 1

Step-by-step explanation: Step 1: identify the specific lesson skill being modeled. Step 2: examine the words, evidence, sentence, or passage detail that matters most. Step 3: explain why the final response or revision demonstrates the skill accurately.

Passage: A speaker argues that citizens must attend town meetings because laws affect roads, schools, and taxes. The speaker says silence gives decisions to others.

Step 1: Topic: civic participation.

Step 2: Central idea: Citizens should participate in local government because public decisions affect daily life.

Step 3: Details about roads, schools, taxes, and silence develop the idea by showing consequences of participation and nonparticipation.

Worked Example 2

Step-by-step explanation: Step 1: identify what changed from the first example. Step 2: connect the example to the lesson focus. Step 3: explain how the response uses clearer reasoning, stronger evidence, or more precise language.

Passage: An essay explains that public parks give families places to gather, workers places to rest, and children places to play. It ends by saying parks are shared promises that a city belongs to all its people.

Step 1: Topic: public parks.

Step 2: Central idea: Public parks strengthen community life because they serve many groups of people.

Step 3: The examples of families, workers, and children develop the central idea by showing different people benefiting from the same public space.

Worked Example 3

Step-by-step explanation: Step 1: combine the lesson skill with evidence or revision choices. Step 2: check that the explanation stays focused on the assigned standard. Step 3: confirm that the final response is clear, accurate, and written in standard English.

Passage: A historical speech says that a bridge does not only cross a river; it joins markets, families, schools, and neighborhoods that had been separated.

Step 1: Topic: a bridge.

Step 2: Central idea: Public improvements can connect communities and create shared opportunity.

Step 3: The list of markets, families, schools, and neighborhoods develops the idea by showing that the bridge has social and economic importance.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: The central idea is the passage talks about a bridge.

Correct: The central idea is that the bridge connects people and creates shared opportunity, not just transportation.

Why this matters: A topic names the subject. A central idea explains what the writer says about that subject.

Teachable Explanation: A strong answer does more than choose the correct wording. It names the specific issue, corrects it, and explains why the correction fits the lesson skill, audience, evidence, or meaning.
