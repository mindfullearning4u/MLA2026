P03 — Lesson: Building Claims, Evidence, and Reasoning

A formal argument succeeds when every part performs a different job: the claim answers the issue, evidence supplies verifiable support, and reasoning explains why that support proves the claim.

Step-by-Step Method

  1. Read the prompt and identify the exact decision or issue.
  2. Write a precise, arguable claim that answers the prompt.
  3. Select evidence that is credible, relevant, and sufficient.
  4. Explain the logical connection between each fact and the claim.
  5. Use formal tone and precise academic vocabulary.
  6. Check that the conclusion follows from the evidence without exaggeration.

Example 1 — School Start Time

Claim: The high school should begin thirty minutes later.

Evidence: During a semester pilot, first-period absences fell 18 percent and on-time arrivals increased 12 percent.

Reasoning: The attendance changes directly support the claim because a later start helped more students arrive ready for instruction.

Example 2 — Community Garden

Weak claim: A garden would be nice.

Revised claim: The town should convert the vacant Oak Street lot into a community garden because the project would provide fresh produce and supervised neighborhood space.

Why stronger: The revision takes a specific position and previews two reasons that evidence can support.

Example 3 — Public Wi-Fi

Evidence: Library computers are fully occupied after school on four of five weekdays.

Weak reasoning: Wi-Fi is obviously better.

Revised reasoning: Reliable public Wi-Fi would expand access beyond the limited computer stations, allowing more students to complete online assignments.

Common Mistakes

  • Using personal preference as evidence.
  • Listing a fact without explaining its significance.
  • Using evidence that concerns a different issue.
  • Claiming that limited evidence “proves” an absolute conclusion.
