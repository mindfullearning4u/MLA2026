Cooling a City, Block by Block

Section 1 — A Growing Problem

On summer afternoons, some city blocks become much hotter than nearby neighborhoods. Dark roofs and pavement absorb sunlight, while areas with fewer trees receive little shade. Tall buildings may also trap warm air between them. During a heat wave, these conditions can keep temperatures elevated even after sunset. Older adults, young children, outdoor workers, and people without reliable air conditioning face the greatest risk.

Section 2 — How Heat Builds

First, asphalt and concrete collect solar energy throughout the day. Next, these surfaces release stored heat slowly after the sun goes down. At the same time, vehicles and air-conditioning systems add more heat to the street. Because nighttime temperatures remain high, residents’ bodies have less opportunity to recover. Repeated hot nights can therefore turn several uncomfortable days into a serious public-health emergency.

Section 3 — Two Cooling Strategies

Tree planting and reflective roofs both reduce heat, but they work differently. Trees shade sidewalks and cool the air as water evaporates from their leaves. Reflective roofs, by contrast, send more sunlight away from buildings and reduce indoor temperatures. Trees may take years to provide broad shade and require water and maintenance. Reflective coatings work immediately on suitable roofs, but they do not shade pedestrians. City planners often combine both approaches.

Section 4 — A Neighborhood Solution

One district mapped its hottest bus stops and walking routes. The map revealed a problem: residents traveling to clinics and grocery stores crossed long stretches without shade. In response, the city created a “cool corridor.” Workers planted drought-tolerant trees, installed pale pavement near crossings, added covered benches, and placed drinking-water stations along the route. A year later, temperature sensors recorded cooler surfaces, and more residents used the corridor during afternoon hours.
