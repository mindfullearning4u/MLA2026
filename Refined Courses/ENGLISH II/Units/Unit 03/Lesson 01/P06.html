Small Forests, Fast Results?

A traditional city park may spread across many acres, but a “microforest” can grow on land as small as a few tennis courts. Supporters plant many native tree and shrub species close together. The dense planting imitates layers found in an older forest: low shrubs, medium trees, and taller canopy trees.

How the Method Works

First, planners study the soil and select native species. Next, workers loosen compacted ground and add organic material. Saplings are then planted closely so they compete for light and grow upward quickly. Finally, volunteers water and weed the site during its first years. Once the young forest becomes established, it generally needs less care.

Benefits and Limits

Microforests and conventional parks both provide green space, yet they serve different purposes. A conventional park offers open lawns, sports areas, and wide paths. A microforest provides dense habitat, shade, and soil improvement in a smaller area. However, dense planting leaves little room for recreation, and young trees still require careful maintenance. For that reason, planners treat microforests as one tool rather than a replacement for every park.

A Schoolyard Problem

At Eastview School, an unused corner collected storm water and became muddy after heavy rain. The area was too small for a playing field. The school partnered with neighborhood volunteers to plant a microforest there. Roots helped water soak into the soil, shade reduced surface heat, and science classes began monitoring birds and insects. The project turned an overlooked drainage problem into an outdoor learning space.
