P03 — Lesson: How Text Structure Shapes Meaning

Direct Teaching

Informational writers do more than place facts on a page. They choose an organizational pattern that controls which relationships readers notice first. Strong analysis names the structure, identifies its evidence, and explains its impact on understanding.

A Five-Step Analysis Process

  1. Identify the topic and central idea. Ask what the author wants the reader to understand.
  2. Track relationships among ideas. Look for time, causes, differences, problems, solutions, or descriptive categories.
  3. Notice signal words and text features. Words such as because, unlike, first, as a result, headings, dates, charts, and captions provide clues.
  4. Name the dominant structure. A passage may contain several patterns, but choose the one organizing most of the important information.
  5. Explain the impact. State what the structure helps the reader compare, follow, understand, or remember.

Example 1 — Chronological Order

Passage: In 1916, Jeannette Rankin campaigned for Congress. The following year, she became the first woman elected to the U.S. House of Representatives. Decades later, more women entered national office.

Analysis: Dates organize events chronologically.
