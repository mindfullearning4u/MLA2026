A Memorial That Teaches

Context: An adapted historical-style dedication speech addresses families, veterans, and city residents.

We place this stone beside the harbor not to glorify battle, but to preserve the names of neighbors who accepted danger when the nation called. Their service belonged to soldiers, yet its cost belonged also to families who waited and communities that carried on.

A monument can become silent decoration unless each generation asks what its names require of the living. Let this place teach that courage is joined to responsibility, that disagreement need not destroy fellowship, and that peace must be protected through patient citizenship.

Bring students here. Tell them not only when the ships departed, but how ordinary people answered uncertainty with service. Let memory become instruction, and let gratitude become a commitment to the public good.

Questions

  1. Identify the audiences and occasion.
  2. State the central idea.
  3. State the precise primary purpose.
  4. Why does the speaker contrast glory with preservation?
  5. How does the reference to families broaden the purpose?
  6. What warning appears in paragraph two?
  7. How do the parallel phrases about courage, disagreement, and peace affect tone?
  8. Why does the speaker command the audience to bring students?
  9. Identify a secondary purpose with evidence.
  10. Analyze how context, diction, tone, and calls to action work together to accomplish the purpose.
