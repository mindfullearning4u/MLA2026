Time to Live as Citizens

Historical context: This adapted historical-style labor speech addresses factory owners, workers, and city voters during a campaign for a shorter workday.

We gather not because honest labor is a burden, but because labor should sustain life rather than consume it. A worker who enters the mill before sunrise and leaves after dark may earn a wage, yet loses the hours in which a parent knows a child, a neighbor serves a community, and a citizen studies the questions placed before the ballot.

Some employers warn that reducing the workday will silence machines and empty markets. Their concern deserves an answer. Exhausted workers make costly errors, suffer injuries, and leave positions that must be filled and taught again. Reasonable hours can protect both human strength and steady production.

Look beyond the factory gate. When workers have time to read, attend meetings, care for families, and purchase goods in local shops, the entire city benefits. Rest is not idleness when it restores the body for work and gives the mind room for responsibility.

We therefore ask lawmakers to establish an eight-hour standard. We ask owners to measure prosperity not only by the length of a shift but by the health and skill of the people whose hands create that prosperity. And we ask citizens to recognize that free government requires time enough to practice citizenship.

This reform will not end every hardship, but it will declare a principle: industry exists within society, not above it. The strength of a city is measured not merely by what its factories produce, but by whether its people have the opportunity to live, learn, and participate.

Questions

  1. Identify the speaker, audiences, and historical situation.
  2. State the central idea.
  3. State the speaker’s precise primary purpose.
  4. How does the opening contrast wages with lost time?
  5. What values does the list of family, neighbor, and citizen roles appeal to?
  6. What counterargument appears in paragraph two?
  7. Why does the speaker say the concern “deserves an answer”?
  8. How do errors, injuries, and turnover support the purpose?
  9. How does paragraph three broaden the issue beyond workers?
  10. Describe the tone and identify two diction choices that create it.
  11. Why does the speaker repeat “We ask”?
  12. How does the call to three groups support the purpose?
  13. Explain “within society, not above it.”
  14. Identify a secondary purpose and support it with evidence.
  15. Analyze how audience, counterargument, diction, tone, and call to action work together to accomplish the purpose.
