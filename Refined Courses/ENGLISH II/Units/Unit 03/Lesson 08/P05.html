Source Packet: A Phone-Free School Day?

Westfield High is considering a policy requiring students to place phones in classroom storage during instruction. Supporters point to a ten-week pilot in which classroom interruptions fell 37 percent and teachers reported longer periods of sustained discussion. Assignment completion rose 8 percent in the pilot classes, although overall grades changed by less than one percentage point.

Principal Elena Ruiz argues that the policy would protect instructional time while allowing phone access during lunch and emergencies. Her statement begins with classroom data, explains the proposed rules, and answers concerns about family communication. She uses a calm tone and emphasizes that the goal is focused learning rather than punishment.

Student council representative Marcus Lee opposes a schoolwide requirement. He accepts that distraction is real but argues that teachers should choose rules suited to each course. He cites a survey in which 61 percent of students said phones sometimes support research, translation, calendars, or accessibility tools. He also notes that the pilot included only twelve classes and lasted ten weeks.

Both speakers agree that emergencies, accessibility needs, and teacher-approved academic use require exceptions. Ruiz assumes that consistent rules will be easier to follow; Lee assumes that flexibility will better serve varied classrooms. The board must decide whether to adopt the policy, extend the pilot, or leave decisions to teachers.

Questions

  1. Identify the dominant text structure and explain its effect.
  2. State the central idea and identify three supporting details.
  3. Explain Principal Ruiz’s purpose and intended audience.
  4. Analyze two rhetorical choices Ruiz uses.
  5. Explain Marcus Lee’s purpose and intended audience.
  6. Analyze two rhetorical choices Lee uses.
  7. State each speaker’s claim neutrally.
  8. Identify three pieces of shared information.
  9. Explain how each speaker interprets the pilot differently.
  10. Evaluate the validity of each speaker’s main assumption.
  11. Identify the strongest evidence and strongest limitation for each side.
  12. Write a precise claim choosing one of the board’s three options.
  13. Organize three supporting facts and explain the order.
  14. State and rebut the strongest counterclaim objectively.
  15. Write a formal argument of at least three developed paragraphs integrating text analysis, credible evidence, reasoning, counterclaim, rebuttal, logical organization, and objective tone.
