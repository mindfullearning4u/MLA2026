P03 — Lesson: Unit 3 Synthesis

Synthesis means using several reading and writing skills together. In this lesson, you will analyze how an informational text is built and then use that analysis to evaluate and construct an argument.

Six-Part Synthesis Process

  1. Identify text structure and explain how it shapes information.
  2. Determine the central idea and trace supporting details.
  3. Analyze author purpose, audience, and rhetorical choices.
  4. Evaluate claims, shared evidence, assumptions, and validity.
  5. Develop a precise claim supported by credible evidence and reasoning.
  6. Address a fair counterclaim using logical organization and objective tone.

Example 1 — Reading Structure and Central Idea

A report first describes rising summer temperatures, then explains increased energy use and proposes shade-tree programs. The problem-solution structure emphasizes the central idea that targeted community planning can reduce heat-related impacts.

Example 2 — Purpose and Rhetoric

An author addressing voters uses local statistics, testimony from emergency workers, and measured language. These choices build credibility and appeal to both logic and community responsibility.

Example 3 — From Evaluation to Argument

Two authors use the same transit data but reach different conclusions. A strong writer identifies each assumption, judges which reasoning is more valid, then constructs a claim that acknowledges uncertainty and rebuts the strongest opposing point.

Common Mistakes

  • Summarizing without analyzing structure or purpose.
  • Choosing a preferred position without testing evidence.
  • Listing facts without explaining reasoning.
  • Ignoring the strongest counterclaim or using biased tone.
