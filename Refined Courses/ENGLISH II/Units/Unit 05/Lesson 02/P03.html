P03 — Lesson: Etymology, Derivations, and Word Families

Words carry histories. You can determine meaning more accurately by combining a word's origin, its meaningful parts, its related forms, and the sentence context.

A Reliable Five-Step Method

  1. Locate the target word and read the full sentence.
  2. Divide the word into a prefix, root or base, and suffix when possible.
  3. Recall the history or origin of the root. Etymology explains where a word came from; it does not replace context.
  4. Build a word family by listing related forms that share the base meaning.
  5. Test the meaning in context and paraphrase the sentence. Revise if the meaning does not fit.

Example 1: reconstruct

Sentence: After the storm damaged the outdoor stage, volunteers worked together to reconstruct it.

Parts: re- (again) + struct (build) + -ion/-ive family forms. Family: construct, construction, constructive, reconstruct.

Reasoning: The damaged stage must be built again, so reconstruct means “build again.”

Example 2: inaudible

Sentence: The announcement was inaudible above the crowd, so the principal repeated it.

Parts: in- (not) + aud (hear) + -ible (able to be). Family: audio, audience, audible, inaudible.

Reasoning: The announcement could not be heard; inaudible means “not able to be heard.”

Example 3: predictable

Sentence: Because the bus arrived at the same time each day, its schedule was predictable.

Parts: pre- (before) + dict (say) + -able (capable of). Family: dictate, diction, dictionary, predict, prediction.

Reasoning: The repeated schedule allowed students to “say before” when the bus would arrive; predictable means able to be anticipated.

Common Mistakes

  • Using only one clue: A familiar-looking root can mislead you. Confirm with context.
  • Assuming relatives are synonyms: audience and audible share aud, but they have different functions and meanings.
  • Ignoring suffixes: predict, prediction, and predictable have related meanings but different parts of speech.
