P04 Worked Example

Each example shows how context, connotation, and denotation work together.

Worked Example 1: Denotation

Step-by-step explanation: Step 1: identify the specific lesson skill being modeled. Step 2: examine the words, evidence, sentence, or passage detail that matters most. Step 3: explain why the final response or revision demonstrates the skill accurately.

Sentence: The cabin was small.

Step 1: Find the literal meaning of small.

Step 2: Small means limited in size.

Reasoning: This is denotation because it focuses on dictionary meaning.

Worked Example 2: Connotation

Step-by-step explanation: Step 1: identify what changed from the first example. Step 2: connect the example to the lesson focus. Step 3: explain how the response uses clearer reasoning, stronger evidence, or more precise language.

Sentence: The cabin was cramped.

Step 1: Cramped also means small, but it feels uncomfortable.

Step 2: The word creates a negative association.

Reasoning: Cramped has a negative connotation and creates a more critical tone.

Worked Example 3: Tone Effect

Step-by-step explanation: Step 1: combine the lesson skill with evidence or revision choices. Step 2: check that the explanation stays focused on the assigned standard. Step 3: confirm that the final response is clear, accurate, and written in standard English.

Sentence: The cabin was cozy.

Step 1: Cozy can mean small, warm, and comfortable.

Step 2: The word creates a positive feeling.

Reasoning: Cozy gives the sentence a warm and approving tone.

Common Mistake

Incorrect Example:

Cramped and cozy both mean small, so they create the same mood.

Correct Example:

Cramped creates discomfort, while cozy creates warmth and comfort, so the mood changes even though both words can describe a small space.

Teachable Explanation: Denotation tells the basic meaning. Connotation explains the feeling. Tone and mood change when the feeling changes.
