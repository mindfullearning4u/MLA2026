Two Views of Harbor Square

For years, Harbor Square had been a quiet patch of brick between the library and city hall. When officials proposed weekend markets there, the local newspaper printed two responses.

The first writer called the plan a revival. She imagined cheerful stalls, lively music, and neighbors gathering beneath bright awnings. In her view, vendors would energize an overlooked space and transform an empty plaza into a welcoming center of community life. She admitted that traffic might increase, but described the inconvenience as a small adjustment.

The second writer described the proposal as an invasion. He predicted noisy crowds, cluttered sidewalks, and delivery trucks crowding narrow streets. Rather than energizing the square, he argued, the market would disrupt a peaceful civic space. He called the same traffic change a daily burden for residents.

Both writers discussed the same plan and agreed on several facts: vendors would arrive each weekend, music would be permitted, and nearby streets would carry more vehicles. Yet their language guided readers toward different judgments. Revival and invasion both name a major change, but one suggests renewal while the other suggests an unwanted takeover. Small adjustment minimizes difficulty; daily burden emphasizes hardship.

At the next council meeting, a student compared the responses. She observed that neither writer relied on dictionary meaning alone. Each selected words with emotional associations and repeated those associations until a clear attitude emerged. The first response sounded enthusiastic and hopeful. The second sounded alarmed and disapproving. Understanding the difference did not require choosing a side; it required noticing how context, connotation, denotation, and tone worked together.

Questions

  5. Identify three words that help create the first writer's tone.
  6. Name that tone precisely and explain your evidence.
  7. Identify three words that help create the second writer's tone.
  8. Name that tone precisely and explain your evidence.

  11. Rewrite one sentence using neutral language. Explain how the tone changes.
  12. Write a paragraph explaining how context, denotation, and connotation work together in the passage.
