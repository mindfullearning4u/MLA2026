Editing the Community Pool Proposal

The student council drafted a proposal asking the city to extend community-pool hours. The first version said the pool was “good for many people” and that closing at six created “a problem.” During revision, students used dictionaries, a thesaurus, and context to make the language more precise.

They first replaced good with beneficial. A dictionary defined beneficial as producing a helpful result. The adjective fit because the next sentences named specific benefits: swim practice, water-safety lessons, and low-impact exercise. The thesaurus also suggested pleasant and profitable, but neither matched the evidence. The pool might be pleasant, yet the paragraph emphasized usefulness; it did not discuss financial profit.

Next, the group examined problem. Possible replacements included obstacle, inconvenience, crisis, and limitation. Closing early prevented working families from attending lessons, so obstacle captured something blocking a goal. Crisis exaggerated the situation, while inconvenience minimized it. The students selected obstacle and explained their reasoning.

The sentence “Many residents want longer hours” also needed attention. Survey data showed that 68 percent of respondents supported the change. The word many was not false, but a clear majority communicated the evidence more exactly. Students kept support rather than replacing it with demand, because the survey measured preference, not an angry requirement.

Finally, they checked word relationships. Extend and shorten were antonyms. Lap swimming, lessons, and exercise were examples within the broader category of pool uses. Helpful, beneficial, and advantageous were synonyms of different formality and intensity. The finished proposal was not merely more impressive; it was more accurate.
