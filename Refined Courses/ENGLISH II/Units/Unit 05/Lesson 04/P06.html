Choosing Words for a Trail Report

After a weekend cleanup, Lena wrote that the riverside trail was “really messed up.” Her editor asked her to replace the vague phrase with language readers could evaluate. A dictionary and the photographs showed three distinct conditions: branches obstructed the northern path, litter covered the picnic area, and rain had eroded soil near the bridge.

Lena considered calling the entire trail destroyed, but the word meant damaged beyond use or repair. Most of the trail remained open, so the term exaggerated the evidence. She revised the sentence to say that several sections were damaged and then named each type of damage.

For the branches, a thesaurus suggested blocked, concealed, crowded, and obstructed. Concealed meant hidden from view, which was not the problem. Crowded described limited space but did not clearly show that passage was prevented. Obstructed precisely indicated that the fallen limbs interfered with movement.

In the final paragraph, Lena wrote that volunteers “fixed everything fast.” She replaced it with measurable details: twenty volunteers cleared the path in three hours, collected fourteen bags of litter, and installed two erosion barriers. The revision used examples and quantities instead of a broad claim. Reference materials helped, but context and evidence controlled every final choice.

Checkpoint Questions

  1. Identify the vague phrase in Lena's original report.
  2. Why is destroyed inaccurate?
  3. How does damaged better match the evidence?
  4. Compare blocked, concealed, crowded, and obstructed.
  5. Why is obstructed the most precise choice?
  6. What word relationship connects damaged and destroyed?
  7. How do the three trail conditions function as examples within a category?
  8. Why are quantities more precise than “fixed everything fast”?
  9. Explain the proper role of a dictionary and thesaurus in Lena's revision.
  10. Write a paragraph explaining how context, evidence, and reference materials work together to produce precise meaning.
