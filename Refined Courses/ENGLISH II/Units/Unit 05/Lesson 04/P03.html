P03 — Lesson: Word Relationships, Reference Materials, and Precise Meaning

Reference tools generate possibilities; careful readers use context and word relationships to choose the most precise meaning.

Six-Step Method

  1. Read the complete sentence and identify the word's job.
  2. Use a dictionary to compare definitions, pronunciation, part of speech, and usage.
  3. Use a thesaurus to generate related options—not automatic replacements.
  4. Check relationships: synonym, antonym, category/example, cause/effect, or degree of intensity.
  5. Test each candidate in the original sentence.
  6. Select the word whose meaning, tone, and grammar fit most precisely.

Example 1: important

For “an ___ announcement about the exam schedule,” a thesaurus may list meaningful, serious, major, valuable, necessary. Important is precise when the message matters; major may overstate the scale, and necessary announcement is awkward.

Example 2: walk

Stroll, march, wander, limp, and pace all involve walking, but they differ in purpose and manner. “The guard marched to the gate” signals discipline; “wandered” would contradict that context.

Example 3: temperature words

Cool, cold, freezing, and frigid form a scale of intensity. A forecast of 55°F may be cool, but calling it frigid is imprecise. Degree relationships matter.

Common Mistakes

  • Choosing the first thesaurus synonym.
  • Ignoring part of speech or usage labels.
  • Replacing a precise word with a more dramatic but inaccurate one.
  • Assuming related words are interchangeable.
