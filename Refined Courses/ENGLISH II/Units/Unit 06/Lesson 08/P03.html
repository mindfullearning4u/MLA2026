P03 — Lesson: Building a Complete Synthesis Presentation

Direct Teaching
A strong presentation is a connected argument: every choice must serve the audience, purpose, and central message. Use the six-step process below to plan, build, revise, and rehearse a complete oral and digital presentation.

Step-by-Step Process

  1. Define audience and purpose. Write one sentence naming who will listen, what they need to understand, and what you want them to think or do.
  2. State a precise central claim. Make it focused, arguable, and appropriate for the situation.
  3. Select and qualify evidence. Choose credible facts, examples, quotations, or data. Explain the source and any limit that affects interpretation.
  4. Organize the message. Sequence ideas logically: opening and claim, evidence with reasoning, response to a concern, and a purposeful conclusion.
  5. Design for understanding. Use concise slide text, readable visuals, accurate labels, and speaker notes that explain rather than repeat.
  6. Revise and rehearse. Check clarity, tone, transitions, pacing, eye contact, volume, pronunciation, and timing. Revise after feedback.

Three Connected Examples

Example 1 — Alignment: A student asks the school board to expand library hours. The claim, attendance data, and respectful formal tone all fit the audience and purpose. The speaker explains how the data supports the request.
Example 2 — Qualification: A survey shows that 72% of 50 respondents want a study hall. The presenter says, “This small volunteer sample suggests strong interest, but a schoolwide survey would provide stronger evidence.” That qualification builds credibility.
Example 3 — Digital and Oral Roles: A slide displays one labeled graph and a six-word takeaway. The speaker note explains the trend, connects it to the claim, and gives the audience a clear next step.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Adding facts without explaining how they support the claim.
  • Reading crowded slides word for word.
  • Using a tone that does not fit the audience.
  • Ignoring evidence limits or opposing concerns.
  • Ending without a conclusion or audience action.
Final Quality Check: Can the audience identify your claim, follow the reasoning, trust the evidence, read the visuals, and understand what matters at the end?
