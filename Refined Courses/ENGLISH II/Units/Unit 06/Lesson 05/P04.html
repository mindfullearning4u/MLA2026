P04 — Worked Example: Making Digital Evidence Clear and Useful

Model Situation: A student will present to the principal and parent council about improving a dangerous crosswalk near school. Study the source notes, then follow three worked examples showing how to choose, display, and explain evidence.

Model Source Notes

During five school mornings, a student safety team counted 186 pedestrians crossing Oak Street between 7:15 and 7:45. In 27 observations, a vehicle entered the crosswalk before the pedestrian reached the opposite curb. The team did not record vehicle speed or determine whether each driver could see the pedestrian. A city transportation memo states that high-visibility markings and warning signs can improve driver awareness, but the city must conduct an engineering review before changing the intersection.

Worked Example 1 — Select and Qualify the Evidence

  1. Claim: The city should conduct an engineering review of the Oak Street crossing.
  2. Best evidence: 27 observed crosswalk entries during five mornings.
  3. Qualification: The observations identify a repeated concern but do not establish speed, visibility, or driver intent.
  4. Reasoning: The repeated concern justifies expert review without overstating what the student data proves.

Worked Example 2 — Design the Slide

Weak slide: “There were 186 people and 27 problems and signs can help and the city should fix it.”
Revised title: “Five-Morning Observation Shows a Repeated Crossing Concern”
Visual: A simple two-bar display labeled “Pedestrian crossings: 186” and “Vehicle entries before crossing completed: 27.”
Source line: Student Safety Team, five morning observations.

Why it works: The title states the takeaway, the labels make the numbers understandable, and the source line shows where the evidence came from.

Worked Example 3 — Write Complementary Speaker Notes

Speaker note: “Across five mornings, the team observed 27 occasions when a vehicle entered the crosswalk before a pedestrian finished crossing. Because we did not measure speed or visibility, this does not prove why the events occurred. It does show a repeated safety concern that supports our request for the city’s engineering review.”
  1. The note interprets the visual rather than reading it.
  2. It qualifies the evidence honestly.
  3. It connects the data to the claim and requested action.

Digital Evidence Quality Checklist

CheckQuestion
AccuracyAre numbers, quotations, labels, and source credits correct?
ClarityCan the audience identify the main evidence in a few seconds?
RelevanceDoes the evidence directly support the claim?
ExplanationDo speaker notes explain meaning, limits, and significance?
Audience FitAre the design and language appropriate for the audience?
Common mistake: Treating design as decoration. A strong digital choice makes evidence easier to understand and does not exaggerate what the source proves.
