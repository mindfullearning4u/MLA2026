Conventions and Clarity in Short Responses

Direct Teaching

Conventions are the rules that help a reader follow your meaning. A strong short response must express an accurate idea and use complete, correctly punctuated sentences.

Step 1: Find every complete thought

A complete thought has a subject and a verb and can stand alone. Students revised is complete. Because students revised is not; the word because makes the reader wait for the result.

Example: Because the first draft was unclear. → Because the first draft was unclear, Maya added a specific detail.

Step 2: Mark sentence boundaries

If two complete thoughts are joined without correct punctuation, you have a run-on. Separate them with a period, a semicolon, or a comma plus a coordinating conjunction.

  • Run-on: The response states a claim it does not explain the evidence.
  • Correct: The response states a claim, but it does not explain the evidence.
  • Correct: The response states a claim. It does not explain the evidence.

Step 3: Check capitals and punctuation

Capitalize the first word of every sentence and all proper nouns. End statements with periods, questions with question marks, and use commas where they separate introductory phrases or joined clauses.

Step 4: Preserve meaning while revising

Do not “fix” a sentence by changing the author’s idea. Read the source sentence, revise the conventions, and compare the revision with the original meaning.

Common Mistakes

  • Adding a period to a fragment without completing the thought.
  • Joining two sentences with only a comma (a comma splice).
  • Changing a fact while rewriting.
  • Correcting punctuation but overlooking capitalization.

Final Four-Part Check

  1. Is every sentence complete?
  2. Are sentence boundaries correct?
  3. Are capitals and punctuation correct?
  4. Does the revision preserve the original meaning?
