The Bell in the Fog

By the time Mara reached the old North Street station, fog had erased the far end of the platform. The building had been closed longer than she had been alive. Weeds pushed through the bricks, and the timetable behind the ticket window had faded to the color of weak tea. She had come only because her history teacher wanted each student to photograph a forgotten place.

As Mara lifted her camera, a dull metal note sounded above her. The station bell moved once, though the air was still. Beneath it stood Mr. Vale, a retired mechanic, balancing on a ladder. He explained that he had cleaned the bell’s rusted hinge. “A town should remember what once called it together,” he said.

Mara almost replied that a bell could not matter when no trains stopped there. Then Mr. Vale pulled the rope. The clear sound traveled through the fog. A woman walking her dog paused at the fence. A delivery driver lowered his window. From the bakery across the street, two workers stepped outside. For a moment, everyone faced the station.

Mrs. Chen, the baker, told Mara that her parents had arrived at that platform with two suitcases and the address of a cousin. The delivery driver remembered leaving for military service there. Another neighbor recalled waiting under the clock each Friday for her mother, who worked in the city. Their stories did not appear on the faded timetable, yet each began or ended beside the same bricks.

The next afternoon, Mara returned with a recorder instead of a camera. She interviewed older residents and labeled each account with a date and name. She learned that the station’s value did not depend on moving trains. Its importance survived in the memories people carried away from it.

With permission from the town, Mara and her classmates cleaned one room. They displayed copies of photographs beside short quotations from the interviews. Mr. Vale mounted the repaired bell rope near the door, where visitors could see it but not pull it without supervision.

On opening day, fog again covered the tracks. Mara watched visitors point to photographs, correct one another’s dates, and add names to a blank map of the neighborhood. When the bell rang at noon, the crowd became quiet. The sound did not announce a train. It announced that the town had decided its forgotten stories were worth hearing.

Part A — Close Reading

  1. Describe the setting and opening situation.
  2. State the central idea in one complete sentence.
  3. Identify two essential details that develop the central idea and explain why each matters.
  4. What does Mara initially believe about the station? Cite or paraphrase evidence.
  5. How does the fog contribute to the mood?

Part B — Literary Meaning and Evidence

  1. Why does Mr. Vale repair the bell?
  2. What does the bell symbolize by the end of the passage? Explain with evidence.
  3. What do the residents’ reactions reveal about the station?
  4. How do Mara’s interviews change private memories into a community record?
  5. Explain how the classroom display gives the station a new purpose.

Part C — Accurate Paraphrasing

  1. Paraphrase the paragraph beginning “The next afternoon.”
  2. Explain two changes you made to wording or sentence structure.
  3. State the main idea of the paragraph beginning “On opening day.”
  4. Paraphrase the final two sentences without changing their meaning.
  5. Explain why your paraphrase is accurate and not merely copied.

Part D — Synthesis

  1. Write a three- to four-sentence objective summary of the entire passage.
  2. Explain how Mara changes from the beginning to the end.
  3. Choose the strongest piece of evidence for the central idea and explain the connection.
