The Unfinished Mural

Dev had promised the community center a mural before its reopening. He planned a dramatic image of a phoenix because it would look impressive in photographs. Mrs. Alvarez, who had organized programs there for decades, asked him to include the old sycamore tree that once shaded neighborhood meetings. Dev refused. “People want something bold,” he said. “They do not want a history lesson.” His younger cousin Tessa disagreed, arguing that art should help people recognize themselves.

Two days before the reopening, Dev found a box of photographs beneath a drop cloth. One showed children reading under the sycamore; another showed residents distributing food there after a storm. He recognized Mrs. Alvarez as a teenager in both pictures. That afternoon she arrived carrying paint but did not repeat her argument. Dev studied his unfinished phoenix, then sketched branches rising through its wings. He invited Tessa to add small books and food baskets among the leaves.

At the reopening, visitors pointed out relatives and memories hidden in the mural. Dev realized the image was still bold, but its strength came from belonging to more people than himself. When a reporter praised his design, he answered, “It became our design when I finally listened.”
