Directions

Read “The Lantern on Cedar Street.” Answer all 18 questions in complete sentences. Use specific evidence and explain how each quotation or detail supports your thinking.

The Lantern on Cedar Street

Amara had spent three years helping her grandmother prepare the neighborhood lantern walk. This year, at sixteen, she wanted to lead it herself. She designed a faster route through the new shopping district and printed bright maps before anyone could object. Her grandmother studied the route and said, “The walk is not a race. Cedar Street matters because every lantern there carries a family’s story.” Amara heard only hesitation. The old route was uneven, dim, and slow; the new one looked polished enough to attract visitors.

At the planning meeting, Mr. Bell objected too. His family’s bakery had stood on Cedar Street for seventy years, and the walk traditionally paused there while he read his grandfather’s immigration letter. Amara replied that long speeches discouraged younger participants. Her friend Luca supported the new route because the shopping district offered better lighting and space for photographs. The room divided between preserving tradition and creating something current. Amara ended the meeting by announcing that the printed maps made the decision final.

On the evening of the walk, heavy rain flooded the shopping district’s underpass. Amara’s route was suddenly unsafe. She stared at the crowd gathering beneath umbrellas, ashamed that she had ignored every warning. Her grandmother did not take control. Instead, she handed Amara the old route map and asked, “What does a leader do when the plan fails?” Amara wanted to cancel, but she noticed Mr. Bell shielding a small wooden lantern marked with his grandfather’s name. She asked him to lead the group toward Cedar Street while she and Luca checked the sidewalks.

The walk moved slowly. At the bakery, Mr. Bell shortened his reading and invited two children to hold the letter beneath the awning. Luca photographed the lanterns reflected in rainwater, and the images looked more powerful than the shopping district signs would have. When they reached the final block, Amara thanked everyone for adapting. “I thought making the walk mine meant replacing what came before,” she admitted. “Now I think leading means understanding what people need and helping their stories move forward.” The next morning, she began a new plan that preserved the Cedar Street stops while improving lighting and accessibility.

Questions

  1. What does Amara believe leadership means at the beginning?
  2. What details reveal her grandmother’s perspective?
  3. Why does Mr. Bell oppose the new route?
  4. Why does Luca support it?
  5. What values are in conflict during the meeting?
  6. How does Amara’s announcement reveal her initial level of maturity?
  7. What event becomes the turning point?
  8. Why is the grandmother’s question important?
  9. What does the wooden lantern symbolize?
  10. How does Amara respond differently after the plan fails?
  11. Quote one detail that shows her growth.
  12. Explain how Mr. Bell also adapts.
  13. How does Luca’s photograph complicate the old-versus-new conflict?
  14. State a theme about leadership.
  15. Explain how the setting develops that theme.
  16. How does dialogue reveal conflicting perspectives?
  17. Compare Amara’s first and final definitions of leadership.
  18. Write a well-developed paragraph explaining how the author represents Amara’s coming-of-age experience through conflict, consequence, and choice.
