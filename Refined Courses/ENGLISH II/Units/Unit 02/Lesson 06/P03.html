P03 — Lesson: Analyzing Literary Adaptations

Direct Teaching

  1. Identify the source’s central character, conflict, setting, symbols, and theme.
  2. Identify what the adaptation preserves.
  3. Identify what it changes: setting, character role, outcome, point of view, or cultural context.
  4. Compare how both versions develop the shared theme.
  5. Infer why the adapter made each change for a new audience or purpose.
  6. Evaluate the effect: what becomes clearer, more urgent, less complex, or newly relevant?
  7. Support every comparison with evidence from both texts.

Quick Model

A source hero steals guarded fire so people can survive; a modern adaptation has a student release restricted research so classmates can learn. The adaptation preserves forbidden knowledge and sacrifice but changes physical fire into information, emphasizing modern questions about access and responsibility.

Common Mistakes

Do not merely list similarities, assume every change weakens the source, or discuss only one version. Explain the adapter’s likely purpose and the effect of the change.

Additional Diverse Examples: Adaptation Across Texts

Example 1 — Myth Reframed

A modern story retells the Icarus myth through a student who ignores safety rules while testing a drone. The adaptation preserves the warning about ambition but shifts the conflict to technology and responsibility.

Example 2 — Classical Allusion

A character calls a difficult decision “my crossroads.” The allusion can connect the choice to older stories about fate, temptation, and identity without copying a full plot.

Example 3 — Religious Image Transformed

A novel uses a forty-day journey to represent testing and renewal. Analysis should explain how the pattern functions in the new story, not merely identify its source.

Comparison Routine

  1. Identify the source pattern.
  2. State what the adaptation preserves.
  3. State what it changes.
  4. Explain why the new context matters.
  5. Compare the themes.
  6. Use evidence from both texts.
