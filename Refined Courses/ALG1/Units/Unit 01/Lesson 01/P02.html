Notebook Title

Lesson 1: Algebra Readiness Diagnostic and Mathematical Thinking

Why Readiness Comes First

Algebra 1 builds from earlier math skills. Before learning new ideas, you need to know which skills are strong and which skills need review.

A readiness diagnostic is not a punishment. It is a tool that helps you and your Teacher of Record understand what support you need.

In this lesson, the goal is to think carefully, show work clearly, and notice patterns in your own problem solving.

Mathematical Thinking Habits

Strong math students do more than get answers. They use habits that make their thinking visible.

  • Read carefully: Identify what the problem is asking before starting.
  • Show work: Write enough steps so your reasoning can be followed.
  • Use labels: Include units, variables, or words when they help explain meaning.
  • Check reasonableness: Ask whether the answer makes sense in the problem.
  • Correct mistakes: Use errors as information about what to review.

Vocabulary

  • Diagnostic: A task used to identify strengths and gaps before instruction continues.
  • Reasoning: The thinking used to support an answer.
  • Strategy: A planned method for solving a problem.
  • Reasonable answer: An answer that makes sense based on the numbers and context.
  • Error analysis: The process of finding, explaining, and correcting a mistake.

Example 1: Read the Problem First

Problem: A student has 18 completed assignments and 6 missing assignments. How many assignments are there in all?

Think: The question asks for the total number of assignments.

18 + 6 = 24

Answer: There are 24 assignments in all.

Reasonableness Check: The answer should be greater than 18 because missing assignments are being added to completed assignments.

Example 2: Organize the Work

Problem: Evaluate 4 + 3 × 5.

Multiplication is completed before addition.

4 + 3 × 5

4 + 15 = 19

Answer: 19

Reasoning: Clear steps help show that the order of operations was followed.

Common Mistake to Avoid

Do not rush into a problem without reading what is being asked.

Problem: A notebook costs $3. A student buys 4 notebooks. How much does the student spend?

Incorrect:

3 + 4 = 7 dollars

Correct:

3 × 4 = 12 dollars

Why: The situation has 4 groups of 3 dollars, so multiplication matches the context.
