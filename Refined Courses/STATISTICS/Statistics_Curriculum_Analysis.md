# Statistics Curriculum Analysis

## Purpose

This document analyzes the strongest MLA Statistics course architecture before standards development. It establishes the recommended course purpose, target student population, instructional domains, six-unit architecture, readiness alignment, and relationship to existing MLA mathematics courses.

This is an analysis document only. It does not create MLA standards, benchmarks, lessons, assessments, or unit mappings.

---

## What MLA Statistics Should Be

MLA Statistics should be a mastery-based high school mathematics course focused on statistical thinking, data literacy, research design, probability, inference, and data-based decision making.

The course should help students move beyond simply calculating measures of center or reading graphs. Students should learn how data are collected, how displays and summaries can reveal or distort information, how probability supports uncertainty, how samples support inference, and how statistical claims should be evaluated in context.

MLA Statistics should not be a narrow test-preparation course, a spreadsheet-only data course, or a duplicate of Math for College Readiness. It should be a full statistics course that prepares students for introductory college statistics, social science research, healthcare data interpretation, business analytics, workforce reporting, and informed civic decision making.

---

## Primary Purpose of the Course

The primary purpose of MLA Statistics should be:

**To prepare students to collect, analyze, interpret, communicate, and critique data in real-world and academic contexts using statistical reasoning, probability, inference, research literacy, and ethical data-based decision making.**

The course should develop students who can:

- Ask statistical questions
- Plan data collection
- Identify variables and sources of bias
- Represent and summarize data
- Interpret variability
- Use probability to reason about uncertainty
- Draw conclusions from samples
- Evaluate claims and limitations
- Communicate findings responsibly

---

## Target Student Population

MLA Statistics is appropriate for:

- Students who have completed Algebra I and Geometry
- Students who have completed or are taking Algebra II
- Students preparing for introductory College Statistics
- Students entering psychology, education, nursing, healthcare, business, criminal justice, social sciences, public service, or technical programs
- Students who need stronger data interpretation, research literacy, and evidence-based reasoning
- Students who may not be pursuing Calculus but still need rigorous college and career quantitative preparation

The course can serve grade 11 or 12 students, especially those whose future pathway requires data interpretation and evidence-based decision making rather than Precalculus or Calculus preparation.

---

## How Statistics Should Differ From Other MLA Math Courses

### Algebra II

Algebra II emphasizes advanced algebraic structure, functions, symbolic manipulation, polynomial/rational/radical/exponential/logarithmic relationships, systems, and modeling.

Statistics should use algebra only as a support tool. Its central focus should be data, variability, probability, inference, research design, and interpretation of evidence.

### Precalculus

Precalculus prepares students for Calculus and STEM pathways through advanced functions, trigonometry, conics, vectors, polar/parametric relationships, sequences, series, and limit readiness.

Statistics should not function as a Precalculus substitute. It should prepare students for college statistics, research literacy, and data-driven fields rather than Calculus.

### Math for College Readiness

Math for College Readiness is a hybrid readiness course covering quantitative reasoning, applied algebra, data literacy, finance, probability, measurement, and workforce readiness.

Statistics should go deeper into data and uncertainty. It should include more formal statistical questions, sampling methods, experimental design, probability models, inference, confidence intervals, hypothesis testing, and statistical communication.

### Algebra I and Geometry

Algebra I and Geometry provide foundational reasoning, functions, graphing, measurement, and modeling. Statistics should build on those foundations but shift the course identity from solving equations or proving relationships to investigating data and evaluating claims.

---

## Skills Students Should Possess Upon Completion

By the end of MLA Statistics, students should be able to:

- Distinguish statistical questions from non-statistical questions
- Identify populations, samples, variables, parameters, and statistics
- Design or critique surveys, observational studies, and experiments
- Identify bias, confounding, randomization, control, and limitations
- Create and interpret data displays for categorical and quantitative data
- Summarize data using center, spread, shape, outliers, and context
- Analyze relationships between two variables using tables, scatterplots, association, correlation, residuals, and regression models where appropriate
- Use probability rules, conditional probability, independence, and simulations to reason about uncertainty
- Understand sampling distributions conceptually
- Interpret confidence intervals and margins of error
- Interpret hypothesis tests, p-values, statistical significance, and practical significance at an introductory level
- Evaluate statistical claims from media, research summaries, workplace reports, and civic contexts
- Communicate statistical conclusions clearly, including assumptions and limitations

## Model Evaluation

## Model 1: Traditional Statistics

### Focus

- Descriptive statistics
- Probability
- Inference
- Research methods

### Strengths

- Strong alignment to introductory college statistics
- Clear progression from data to probability to inference
- Supports research literacy and statistical communication
- Provides a strong academic identity distinct from Algebra II and Precalculus

### Limitations

- May underemphasize modern data visualization and applied analytics
- Can become formula-driven if not taught through context and interpretation
- May not fully address workforce data-literacy needs
- Advanced learners may need richer real-world datasets and technology-supported investigations

### Best Use

This model is strongest when the main goal is college statistics preparation and research-methods readiness.

---

## Model 2: Applied Data Science

### Focus

- Data literacy
- Visualization
- Statistical analysis
- Decision making
- Real-world data

### Strengths

- Strong alignment to workforce data literacy
- Emphasizes visualization, technology, interpretation, and applied decision making
- Supports business, healthcare, public service, and technical pathways
- Encourages authentic data investigations

### Limitations

- May underemphasize formal probability and inference
- Can drift into tool use without enough statistical reasoning
- May not fully prepare students for introductory college statistics expectations
- Can be difficult to audit against traditional high school statistics benchmarks if not carefully structured

### Best Use

This model is strongest when the main goal is workforce analytics and applied data interpretation.

---

## Model 3: Hybrid Statistics Model

### Focus

- Descriptive statistics
- Probability
- Inference
- Research literacy
- Data visualization
- Decision making
- Applied analytics

### Strengths

- Best balances college statistics readiness and workforce data literacy
- Preserves the formal structure needed for probability and inference
- Includes research design and responsible interpretation of claims
- Supports AP Statistics themes for awareness without becoming AP Statistics
- Supports SAT/ACT readiness through data interpretation, probability, modeling, and quantitative reasoning
- Provides a distinct identity from Math for College Readiness by going deeper into statistical investigation and inference

### Limitations

- Requires careful sequencing so inference is not introduced before students understand variability and sampling
- Requires consistent emphasis on interpretation rather than formulas alone
- Requires technology use without allowing technology to replace reasoning

### Best Use

This model is strongest for MLA because it supports college statistics readiness, research literacy, workforce data literacy, and applied decision making without narrowing the course to test prep or tool training.

---

## Recommended Course Model

**Recommended Model: Hybrid Statistics Model**

MLA Statistics should use the Hybrid Statistics Model. This model provides the strongest balance of academic rigor, college readiness, workforce relevance, research literacy, and data-based decision making.

The course should be more formal and statistically focused than Math for College Readiness, but less specialized than AP Statistics. AP Statistics themes should inform the course architecture, but MLA Statistics should remain a mastery-based high school statistics course designed for broad college and career readiness.

---

## Recommended Major Instructional Domains

The strongest MLA Statistics instructional domains are:

### Statistical Thinking and Questioning

Students learn to identify statistical questions, understand variability, distinguish data types, and connect context to appropriate methods.

### Data Collection and Research Design

Students analyze populations, samples, surveys, observational studies, experiments, randomization, bias, confounding, ethics, and limitations.

### Data Visualization and Descriptive Statistics

Students create, interpret, and critique displays; summarize distributions; compare groups; and communicate center, spread, shape, and outliers.

### Bivariate Data and Modeling

Students analyze relationships between variables using two-way tables, scatterplots, association, correlation, regression, residuals, and contextual interpretation.

### Probability and Simulation

Students use probability, conditional probability, independence, expected value, and simulation to reason about uncertainty.

### Statistical Inference

Students develop introductory understanding of sampling distributions, confidence intervals, margins of error, hypothesis tests, p-values, statistical significance, practical significance, and limitations.

### Data-Based Decision Making and Communication

Students evaluate claims, communicate conclusions, explain assumptions, identify limitations, and make evidence-based decisions in academic, workforce, civic, and personal contexts.

## Recommended Six-Unit Structure

## Unit 1: Statistical Thinking and Data Foundations

**Purpose:** Introduce statistical questions, variability, data types, variables, populations, samples, parameters, statistics, and the role of context in statistical reasoning.

**Why It Belongs First:** Students need to understand what makes a question statistical before they collect, display, summarize, or infer from data.

**Connections to Future Units:** Supports data collection, descriptive statistics, bivariate analysis, probability, inference, and data-based communication.

---

## Unit 2: Data Collection and Research Design

**Purpose:** Develop understanding of sampling, surveys, experiments, observational studies, randomization, control, bias, confounding, ethics, and limits of conclusions.

**Why It Belongs Here:** Students should understand where data come from before using data to make claims. This prevents students from treating all datasets as equally trustworthy.

**Connections to Future Units:** Supports inference, claim evaluation, research literacy, and responsible interpretation of statistical reports.

---

## Unit 3: Data Visualization and Descriptive Statistics

**Purpose:** Develop skills for displaying, summarizing, comparing, and interpreting categorical and quantitative data using center, spread, shape, outliers, and context.

**Why It Belongs Here:** Once students understand how data are generated, they can describe what the data show and whether summaries are appropriate.

**Connections to Future Units:** Supports bivariate analysis, probability, inference, media/research claim evaluation, and workforce data reporting.

---

## Unit 4: Bivariate Data, Association, and Models

**Purpose:** Analyze relationships between two variables using two-way tables, scatterplots, association, correlation, linear models, residuals, and contextual interpretation.

**Why It Belongs Here:** Students should understand single-variable distributions before comparing variables and modeling relationships.

**Connections to Future Units:** Supports probability, conditional reasoning, inference about relationships, business analytics, healthcare trends, and research interpretation.

---

## Unit 5: Probability, Simulation, and Uncertainty

**Purpose:** Develop probability reasoning, conditional probability, independence, expected value, probability models, and simulation as tools for understanding uncertainty.

**Why It Belongs Here:** Probability should come after students have worked with real data and association, so uncertainty is connected to actual decision making rather than isolated rules.

**Connections to Future Units:** Supports sampling distributions, confidence intervals, hypothesis testing, risk evaluation, and statistical inference.

---

## Unit 6: Statistical Inference, Claims, and Decisions

**Purpose:** Introduce inference through sampling variability, confidence intervals, margins of error, hypothesis testing, p-values, statistical significance, practical significance, claim evaluation, and communication of conclusions.

**Why It Belongs Last:** Inference requires the full foundation of statistical questions, data collection, descriptive statistics, bivariate reasoning, probability, and sampling variability.

**Connections to Future Learning:** Prepares students for College Statistics, research-based college coursework, healthcare interpretation, social science analysis, workforce reporting, and evidence-based decision making.

## Readiness Analysis

### College Statistics

The recommended structure strongly supports College Statistics by developing statistical questions, data collection, descriptive statistics, probability, inference, and interpretation of conclusions.

### Psychology

Students gain survey design, experimental design, bias detection, correlation versus causation, and introductory inference skills needed to interpret psychological research.

### Education

Students learn to interpret assessment data, compare groups, understand sampling, evaluate claims, and communicate evidence-based conclusions.

### Nursing and Healthcare

The course supports interpretation of patient data, risk, probability, study design, treatment comparisons, statistical significance, and practical significance.

### Criminal Justice

Students learn to interpret crime data, identify bias, evaluate trends, understand risk, and critique statistical claims in public safety contexts.

### Business

Students develop skills in data visualization, trend interpretation, association, forecasting, sampling, decision making, and communicating findings.

### Social Sciences

Students learn research design, survey interpretation, sampling, bias, association, causation, inference, and responsible claim evaluation.

### Workforce Data Literacy

The course supports spreadsheet interpretation, dashboard literacy, workplace reports, quality control, risk evaluation, data visualization, and evidence-based decision making.

## Relationship to Other MLA Courses

### Algebra I

Algebra I develops foundational symbolic and functional reasoning. Statistics uses those skills to interpret data, model relationships, and communicate conclusions.

### Geometry

Geometry develops visual reasoning, measurement, and proof. Statistics develops evidence-based reasoning from data rather than geometric relationships.

### Algebra II

Algebra II develops advanced function and equation reasoning. Statistics uses algebraic tools sparingly and focuses on variability, data, probability, inference, and interpretation.

### Precalculus

Precalculus prepares students for Calculus and STEM pathways through advanced functions and analytical reasoning. Statistics prepares students for College Statistics, research literacy, data-based fields, and applied decision making.

### Math for College Readiness

Math for College Readiness is a broad hybrid readiness course. Statistics is narrower and deeper: it centers on statistical investigation, research design, probability, inference, and data-based communication.

---

## Recommended Course Purpose Statement

Statistics is a mastery-based high school mathematics course that develops students' ability to collect, analyze, interpret, and communicate data using statistical reasoning, probability, research design, inference, and evidence-based decision making.

Students explore data through authentic contexts, visual displays, summary measures, bivariate relationships, probability models, simulations, sampling, confidence intervals, hypothesis testing, and evaluation of statistical claims.

The course prepares students for College Statistics, research-based coursework, workforce data literacy, and data-informed decision making in academic, career, civic, and personal contexts.

## Final Recommendation

### 1. Course Model

MLA should adopt the **Hybrid Statistics Model**.

### 2. Major Domains

Recommended domains:

- Statistical Thinking and Questioning
- Data Collection and Research Design
- Data Visualization and Descriptive Statistics
- Bivariate Data and Modeling
- Probability and Simulation
- Statistical Inference
- Data-Based Decision Making and Communication

### 3. Six-Unit Architecture

Recommended unit sequence:

1. Statistical Thinking and Data Foundations
2. Data Collection and Research Design
3. Data Visualization and Descriptive Statistics
4. Bivariate Data, Association, and Models
5. Probability, Simulation, and Uncertainty
6. Statistical Inference, Claims, and Decisions

### 4. Rationale

This architecture is strongest for MLA because it:

- Establishes a clear course identity distinct from Algebra II, Precalculus, and Math for College Readiness
- Supports Florida high school Data Analysis and Probability expectations
- Prepares students for introductory College Statistics
- Builds research literacy for psychology, education, healthcare, social sciences, business, and criminal justice
- Supports SAT/ACT readiness through data analysis, probability, modeling, and interpretation
- Supports workforce readiness through data visualization, communication, evidence evaluation, and decision making
- Provides a logical progression from statistical questions to data collection, description, relationships, probability, inference, and communication

### Final Determination

The strongest MLA Statistics course architecture is a **Hybrid Statistics Model** organized around statistical thinking, research design, data visualization, descriptive statistics, bivariate analysis, probability, inference, and data-based decision making.
