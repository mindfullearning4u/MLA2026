# MLA Precalculus Unit Mapping Audit

## Executive Summary

This audit reviews `mla_precalculus_unit_mapping_v1.md` for instructional sequencing only. Standards coverage, benchmark coverage, and crosswalk accuracy are treated as approved and are not evaluated here.

The mapping is instructionally sound overall. It correctly treats Precalculus as a bridge course rather than a remediation course, begins with function analysis, separates trigonometric foundations from trigonometric function modeling, and avoids creating a false standards-bearing Calculus unit. The decision to make limits a support-only readiness concept is appropriate.

The sequence should be revised in a few areas to reduce cognitive load and strengthen readiness progression:

- Unit 1 is dense and should make function transformations more explicit before advanced function families and inverses.
- Unit 3 should begin with graphing and transformations before formal periodic modeling.
- Advanced trigonometric formulas and equation strategies should be paced more carefully across Units 2 and 3.
- Unit 5 is the most cognitively demanding unit and should be revised to reduce overload in vector operations, polar graphing, and parametric modeling.
- Prerequisite callouts should be strengthened for algebraic manipulation, graphing technology, completing the square, unit circle fluency, vector components, and recursive notation.

Final determination: **PASS WITH REVISIONS**

---

## Strengths

- The overall six-unit structure follows a defensible Precalculus learning progression: functions, trigonometry, advanced coordinate representations, vectors, sequences, series, and calculus readiness.
- The mapping avoids organizing instruction by standards-document order.
- The split between trigonometric foundations and trigonometric functions is instructionally appropriate.
- Calculus readiness is included without turning Unit 6 into an unofficial Calculus course.
- Lesson 8 in each unit is reserved for synthesis and mastery demonstration, which supports MLA's mastery framework.
- Advanced learner extensions are present in every unit and include SAT, ACT, modeling, technology, and justification opportunities.
- The course supports College Algebra, Trigonometry, Calculus, and STEM readiness.

## Findings

### Unit 1: Advanced Function Analysis and Modeling

The unit begins in the right place: advanced function analysis is the correct foundation for Precalculus. The progression from function review to polynomial, radical/rational, exponential/logarithmic, composition, inverses, and modeling is broadly logical.

Primary concern: Unit 1 is overloaded. It contains nearly every major non-trigonometric function family plus composition, inverses, systems, and piecewise modeling. For strong students, this is efficient. For struggling students, it may feel like a rapid survey of Algebra II before they have stabilized Precalculus-level expectations.

Specific issue: function transformations are referenced as prerequisite knowledge but do not appear as a clearly visible lesson focus. Transformations are essential before polynomial, rational, exponential/logarithmic, trigonometric, and polar function analysis.

Inverses are placed appropriately after composition. Systems and piecewise modeling are not misplaced, but they may be too much as the final new-content lesson in an already dense opening unit.

### Unit 2: Trigonometric Foundations and Identities

The start of Unit 2 is strong. Radian measure, unit circle definitions, exact values, and unit circle symmetry are correctly placed before identities and equations.

Primary concern: advanced identity formulas may be introduced too early for many students. Pythagorean identities are appropriate in Unit 2, but sum, difference, double-angle, and half-angle formulas require more algebraic maturity and may be more successful after students have graphed trigonometric functions and seen why identities matter in equations and models.

Triangle trigonometry is placed reasonably, though it may be more intuitive before advanced identities because Law of Sines, Law of Cosines, and area formulas build from prior Geometry knowledge and provide applied context.

### Unit 3: Trigonometric Functions and Applications

The unit purpose is correct, but the first two lessons should likely be reordered. The current sequence begins with periodic phenomena and trigonometric models before graphing trigonometric functions. For many students, modeling before formal graph structure creates unnecessary abstraction.

Graphing should come first, followed by transformations and key features, then periodic modeling and applications. Trigonometric equation strategies are placed at a reasonable point, but they should explicitly depend on graphing, exact values, identities, and inverse reasoning.

The unit has several support-only lessons, which is instructionally acceptable, but the support-only design should be used to reduce load rather than create a second synthesis-heavy unit before Lesson 8.

### Unit 4: Conics and Complex Plane Relationships

The conics sequence is logical: foundations, circles, parabolas, ellipses, and hyperbolas. Circles first is appropriate, and parabolas before ellipses/hyperbolas builds from students' prior quadratic knowledge.

Primary concern: each conic lesson asks students to derive, graph, solve, and interpret in a single lesson. That is a high cognitive load, especially for ellipses and hyperbolas. The sequence itself is sound, but lesson-level pacing should include strong scaffolds.

Complex numbers after conics are not mathematically dependent on conics, but the placement is acceptable because complex polar/trigonometric form prepares students for Unit 5 polar and vector work. If revisions are made, complex-plane work could be moved immediately before vectors and polar coordinates, but doing so would make Unit 5 heavier.

### Unit 5: Vectors, Polar Coordinates, and Parametric Relationships

This is the highest-risk unit for cognitive overload. The conceptual progression is mathematically sound: vectors, vector operations, applications, polar coordinates, polar graphs, parametric curves, and modeling. However, the unit contains several advanced representation systems in quick succession.

Lesson 2 is overloaded because it includes scalar multiplication, vector addition, subtraction, and resultants. Lesson 3 is also demanding because dot products, projections, velocity, and applications are all introduced together. Lessons 4 and 5 introduce polar conversions and polar graphing quickly, and Lessons 6 and 7 add parametric curves and modeling.

This unit will work for advanced students, but struggling students may need explicit technology routines, representation tables, and repeated coordinate conversion practice.

### Unit 6: Sequences, Series, and Calculus Readiness

The sequence is logical and appropriately paced: arithmetic sequences, geometric sequences, arithmetic series, geometric series, recursive/explicit models, difference quotients, informal limits, and synthesis.

Calculus readiness is appropriately placed late in the course. The mapping correctly uses difference quotients as the standards-backed bridge and keeps limits as support-only readiness. This avoids false benchmark ownership and avoids turning Precalculus into Calculus.

The only pacing concern is that sigma notation appears in advanced extensions and prerequisite language but is not visible as a lesson-level support skill. If sigma notation is expected in series work, it should be explicitly named in prerequisite supports or lesson notes.

## Prerequisite Skill Recommendations

Add or strengthen prerequisite callouts in the mapping:

- Unit 1: function transformations, parent functions, interval notation, end behavior, asymptotes, technology graphing, and domain restrictions.
- Unit 1 Lesson 5: operations with domains, especially restrictions created by rational and radical expressions.
- Unit 1 Lesson 6: one-to-one behavior, horizontal line test, inverse notation, and restricted domains.
- Unit 2: special right triangles, reference angles, radian arc length meaning, reciprocal trig relationships, and algebraic manipulation.
- Unit 2 Lesson 6: factoring, common denominators, verifying identities, and formula substitution.
- Unit 3: amplitude, period, frequency, phase shift, midline, parameter interpretation, and inverse trig calculator conventions.
- Unit 4: completing the square, distance formula, midpoint formula, standard conic forms, and graphing from parameters.
- Unit 4 Lesson 7: polar coordinates as a preview skill before complex trigonometric form.
- Unit 5: vector components, magnitude/direction, coordinate conversion routines, graphing technology, table generation, and parameter interpretation.
- Unit 6: explicit and recursive notation, sigma notation, common difference/common ratio, average rate of change, and simplifying rational expressions in difference quotients.

## Advanced Learner Recommendations

Advanced learners are not likely to be under-challenged if the listed extensions are implemented. The mapping includes strong extension categories, but several should be made more explicit:

- Unit 1: require optional justification of inverse restrictions and composition domains.
- Unit 2: add optional identity proof tasks and derivations of sum/difference formulas.
- Unit 3: add sinusoidal regression and real data modeling extensions.
- Unit 4: add derivation of conic equations from geometric definitions.
- Unit 5: add vector proof tasks, dot product angle derivations, polar graph investigations, and parametric motion projects.
- Unit 6: add informal convergence explorations, difference quotient derivations, and limit behavior investigations using graphing technology.

These should remain optional and should not change mastery expectations.

## College Algebra Readiness Review

The mapping strongly supports College Algebra readiness through advanced function analysis, polynomial/radical/rational/exponential/logarithmic models, systems, piecewise functions, sequences, series, and function operations.

Primary strengths:

- Function notation and graph interpretation are placed at the beginning.
- Polynomial, radical, rational, exponential, and logarithmic models are included early.
- Composition and inverses are appropriately included before advanced applications.
- Sequences and series reinforce algebraic pattern reasoning.

Potential improvement:

- Strengthen explicit callouts for transformations, domain restrictions, asymptotes, and interval notation.

## Calculus Readiness Review

The mapping prepares students for Calculus appropriately without creating false benchmark ownership.

Primary strengths:

- Function behavior is emphasized throughout the course.
- Difference quotients appear late, after function fluency has been developed.
- Informal limits are placed as support-only readiness.
- Trigonometric functions, polar coordinates, parametric equations, vectors, and sequences/series are included before course completion.

Potential improvement:

- Add more explicit references to continuity, average rate of change, local behavior, end behavior, and graphical limit intuition in Unit 6 Lesson 7.
- Keep limits conceptual and visual; do not turn the lesson into formal epsilon-delta or full Calculus limit evaluation.

Readiness placement: appropriate.

False benchmark ownership: avoided.

## STEM Readiness Review

The sequence supports STEM pathways well. The strongest STEM preparation appears in:

- Unit 3: periodic functions and trigonometric modeling
- Unit 4: conics and complex-plane representations
- Unit 5: vectors, polar coordinates, parametric relationships, and motion modeling
- Unit 6: rates of change, sequence/series reasoning, and calculus readiness

Potential improvement:

- Unit 5 should include explicit technology routines and representation comparison tasks so students can move among rectangular, polar, vector, and parametric forms without confusion.
- STEM applications should be recurring but optional when they exceed mastery requirements.

## Sequencing Analysis

### Lessons Placed Too Early

- Unit 3 Lesson 1, Periodic Phenomena and Trigonometric Models, appears too early before formal graphing of trigonometric functions.
- Unit 2 Lesson 6, Sum/Difference/Double-Angle/Half-Angle Formulas, may be too early for struggling students before graphing and equation contexts provide purpose.

### Lessons Placed Too Late

- Function transformations are too implicit and should appear earlier as an explicit support in Unit 1.
- Sigma notation support appears too late or only implicitly if students are expected to use series notation in Unit 6.

### Overloaded Lessons

- Unit 1 Lesson 2: polynomial theorems, graphing, and modeling may be heavy in one lesson.
- Unit 1 Lesson 3: radical and rational function models combine two demanding families.
- Unit 4 Lessons 4 and 5: ellipses and hyperbolas each require significant feature coordination.
- Unit 5 Lesson 2: vector operations and resultants are too much without internal scaffolding.
- Unit 5 Lesson 3: dot products, projections, velocity, and applications are demanding together.
- Unit 5 Lesson 7: parametric and polar modeling is broad and should be carefully constrained.

### Overloaded Units

- Unit 1 is dense because it consolidates many advanced function families and modeling forms.
- Unit 5 is the highest-risk unit because it introduces several advanced representation systems.

### Struggling Learner Impact

Struggling students may encounter avoidable cognitive load in Units 1, 2, 3, and 5 unless the mapping is revised with stronger prerequisite callouts and internal scaffolds.

Most important supports:

- Explicit graphing technology routines
- Function transformation review
- Domain and range review
- Unit circle fluency checks
- Completing-the-square support
- Vector component routines
- Coordinate conversion routines
- Series notation support

### Advanced Learner Impact

Advanced students will not be under-challenged if the optional extensions are implemented. The mapping includes enough depth for accelerated learners, especially in function investigations, identity proofs, conic derivations, vector applications, polar/parametric modeling, and calculus readiness.

---

## Final Determination

**PASS WITH REVISIONS**

The Precalculus Unit Mapping is fundamentally sound and follows strong instructional logic. It should not be rejected. Recommended revisions are primarily lesson-order refinements, pacing improvements, prerequisite callout additions, and cognitive-load reductions. The most important changes are to make function transformations explicit, reorder early Unit 3 so graphing precedes full modeling, pace advanced trigonometric identities more carefully, and scaffold Unit 5 more intentionally.
