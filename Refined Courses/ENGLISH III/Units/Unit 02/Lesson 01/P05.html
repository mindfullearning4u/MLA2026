Independent Work: “What the Building Remembered”

Directions

Read the complete passage. Answer all 18 questions in your notebook using complete sentences and specific evidence. Submit these responses with your vocabulary as Notebook Evidence.

Passage

After the city converted Harbor Station into a museum, Niko was hired to record the sounds of the building before renovation ended. The architects wanted polished exhibits: the click of a ticket punch, the whistle of a restored engine, a cheerful announcement recreated by actors. Niko recorded those sounds, but he also captured what the plans called noise—the radiator knocking behind the waiting room, gulls arguing over the roof, and wind pressing a loose timetable against the wall.

His grandmother Irena had arrived at Harbor Station forty years earlier with one suitcase and a card bearing an unfamiliar address. Whenever Niko asked about that day, she described almost nothing except the waiting room clock. “It lost seven minutes every hour,” she said. “We trusted it anyway.” In the museum, the repaired clock kept perfect time. Its new mechanism made no sound.

On opening morning, visitors moved quickly between bright displays. The official soundtrack played every fifteen minutes. Children copied the conductor’s pose; adults photographed the engine. Near the old waiting room, however, several former passengers lingered beside Niko’s small listening station. When they heard the faulty radiator and the timetable tapping the wall, they began offering corrections and additions: a porter sang while sweeping; the bakery cart arrived before sunrise; the clock was not always seven minutes slow.

The director worried that the unscripted memories would confuse visitors. Niko proposed adding a second channel labeled “What the Building Remembered Differently.” The director frowned at the phrase but approved a trial. Soon the channel contained dozens of voices that contradicted one another in minor ways while agreeing on one thing: the station had been less orderly, and more alive, than the exhibit suggested.

Weeks later, Irena visited. She listened to the flawless whistle, then to the wall’s tapping rhythm. At the clock, she did not check the hands. She placed her palm against the silent case and laughed. “Still pretending,” she said. Niko added her laugh to the second channel, between the radiator and the wind.

Part A — Elements and Literal Meaning

  1. Summarize the literal conflict surrounding the museum’s soundtrack.
  2. How does the station setting influence Niko’s work?
  3. Identify two details that characterize Niko and explain what each reveals.
  4. Describe Irena’s attitude toward the repaired clock.
  5. Explain how the director functions as a contrast to Niko.

Part B — Layers of Meaning

  1. What does the silent, accurate clock symbolize?
  2. Analyze the meaning of Irena’s statement, “We trusted it anyway.”
  3. How does the title of the second channel create personification?
  4. Why are the contradictory memories important rather than merely confusing?
  5. Explain how the passage contrasts order with life.

Part C — Style and Evidence

  1. Analyze the effect of listing unofficial sounds in paragraph 1.
  2. How does the author’s diction change when describing official and unofficial exhibits?
  3. Explain the irony in the repaired clock’s perfection.
  4. How does the final placement of Irena’s laugh affect the ending?
  5. Write one claim about how sound imagery develops meaning and support it with two details.

Part D — Synthesis

  1. State a central idea in one complete sentence.
  2. Explain how at least three literary elements work together to develop that idea.
  3. Evaluate whether the second channel strengthens or weakens the museum. Defend your judgment with evidence.
