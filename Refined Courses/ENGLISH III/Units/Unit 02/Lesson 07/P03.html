Direct Teaching: Reading Across Narrators and Time

Perspective Is Evidence, Not the Whole Truth

Each narrator provides information and reveals a way of interpreting it. Differences may result from position, memory, motive, knowledge, language, or time. An unreliable narrator is not necessarily dishonest; an account can be sincere and incomplete.

Step-by-Step Process

  1. Create a neutral timeline of events.
  2. Label who narrates each section and when.
  3. Record what each narrator knows directly, infers, or learns from others.
  4. Mark repeated facts and contradictions.
  5. Analyze diction, omissions, and self-presentation.
  6. Ask why the author delays or repeats information.
  7. Determine what readers know that a narrator does not.
  8. Synthesize the strongest interpretation while naming remaining uncertainty.

Mini-Example

A captain’s log says the crew abandoned a damaged boat during a storm. A passenger’s letter says the sea was calm when the captain gave the order. Years later, the captain admits the hull had been secretly condemned.

The contradiction first suggests dishonesty, but delayed information complicates judgment: the captain concealed evidence yet may have prevented danger. Structure makes readers revise both moral and factual conclusions.

Common Mistakes

  • Choosing a favorite narrator instead of weighing evidence.
  • Treating contradiction as proof that one account is entirely false.
  • Reading sections in page order without reconstructing chronology.
  • Ignoring why information is delayed.
  • Claiming certainty where the text preserves ambiguity.
