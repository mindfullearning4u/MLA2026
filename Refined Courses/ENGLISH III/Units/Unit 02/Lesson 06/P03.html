Direct Teaching: Comparing Authors and Their Reasoning

A Meaningful Comparison

Two authors being alive at the same time is not enough. A strong comparison identifies a shared public concern, relevant context, and a significant difference in how each author frames the problem or reaches a conclusion.

Step-by-Step Process

  1. Establish each text’s speaker, genre, audience, and purpose.
  2. State the related topic neutrally.
  3. Identify each author’s central claim or implied position.
  4. Map evidence, assumptions, and reasoning.
  5. Notice concessions, counterarguments, and qualifications.
  6. Analyze how genre shapes available evidence and style.
  7. Compare conclusions and values.
  8. Evaluate which approach is more convincing for a stated audience and criterion.

Mini-Comparison

Essayist: argues that public parks deserve funding because health data show community benefits. Short-story writer: depicts a child who finds safety in a neglected park.

Both address public space, but the essay uses empirical cause-and-effect reasoning while the story invites moral reasoning through character experience. A comparison should explain the different forms of evidence rather than judge the story for lacking statistics.

Common Mistakes

  • Comparing biography instead of texts.
  • Assuming related topics produce identical claims.
  • Listing devices without mapping reasoning.
  • Judging genres by the wrong criteria.
  • Claiming one author “wins” without defining an audience or standard.
