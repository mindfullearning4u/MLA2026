Worked Example: Two Writers on Public Memory

Text A — Editorial

Writer A argues that a city should preserve an old market hall. She cites declining maintenance costs after structural repairs, interviews local business owners, and studies showing that walkable historic districts attract visitors. She concedes that not every old building can be saved, then proposes criteria based on safety, use, and documented community value.

Text B — Personal Essay

Writer B recounts returning to the market hall after twenty years. He remembers translating prices for his parents and learning neighbors’ names from handwritten signs. He acknowledges that nostalgia can make buildings seem better than they were, but argues that shared places allow private memories to become public history.

Map the Reasoning

  • A: evidence about cost, commerce, and planning leads to a qualified preservation policy.
  • B: personal experience and reflection lead to a claim about communal identity.
  • Shared assumption: public value includes more than immediate sale price.
  • Different evidence: civic data versus lived experience.

Model Comparative Claim

Both contemporaneous writers defend the market as a public resource, but Writer A reasons toward a transferable policy through data and explicit criteria, while Writer B uses reflective narrative to show why memory itself belongs in definitions of public value.

Evaluation

For a budget committee, A may be more immediately persuasive because her criteria answer cost objections. For residents deciding what the market means, B’s experiential reasoning may reveal values that financial evidence cannot measure. The evaluation names audience and criterion.
