P02 Notebook Task Part 1

Notebook Title: Literary Elements in Analysis

Vocabulary

Literary element: A part of a literary text, such as symbol, imagery, characterization, structure, tone, or style, that helps create meaning.

Figurative language: Language that means more than its literal meaning, such as metaphor, simile, or personification.

Allegory: A story, image, or situation that works on a literal level and a symbolic level.

Elaboration: The deeper explanation that shows why evidence matters.

Passage

The city garden had one tree left after the storm. Its trunk leaned over the path, but each branch held new leaves. Mira called it a survivor, though the gardener said the roots were still splitting underground.

Source Note

In literature, trees often symbolize endurance, growth, or hidden damage because their visible branches and unseen roots can suggest different layers of experience.

Notebook Directions

Step 1: Identify the main literary element in the passage.

Step 2: Write one claim about what that element suggests.

Step 3: Choose one detail from the passage that supports the claim.

Step 4: Explain how the source note can support your interpretation without replacing your own analysis.

Step-by-Step Teaching Support

Lesson focus: Literary elements in source-supported analysis

Mapped standard focus: MLA.ENG3.W.02

Use this sequence before you complete the notebook task. It makes the Grade 11 thinking process explicit so you do not have to guess what the skill requires.

  1. Begin with the interpretive question or claim. In English III, research should help explain literature, not simply collect facts about an author or time period.
  2. Separate primary evidence from secondary-source support. Primary evidence comes from the literary text; secondary support comes from commentary, context, or scholarly explanation.
  3. Synthesize instead of stacking quotations. Explain how the source idea changes, deepens, or supports your interpretation of the text.
  4. Check that every source detail connects back to the literary claim. If the connection is not clear, add reasoning or choose stronger evidence.
