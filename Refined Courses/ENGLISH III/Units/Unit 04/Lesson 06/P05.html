The Last Broadcast from Alder Hill

At 5:42 on the morning the Alder Hill radio station was scheduled to close, Mara Venn unlocked the studio and found three envelopes beneath the door. The first contained a utility bill stamped FINAL NOTICE. The second held a photograph of the station in 1958, when its windows were new and a crowd had gathered to hear election returns through loudspeakers on the sidewalk. The third envelope had no return address. Inside was a single sentence: Please read the names before the signal disappears.

Mara set the note beside the microphone. For twelve years, she had hosted the station’s early program, announcing road closures, school delays, lost dogs, council votes, and the weather that rolled down from the ridge. The work had once seemed provisional—a job she would keep until something more consequential appeared. Yet the repetition had accumulated into a record of the town. She knew which bridge iced first, which farms lost power in an east wind, and which listeners called whenever silence lasted longer than ten seconds.

The station’s owner had explained the closure in brisk financial terms. Advertising had migrated online; the transmitter needed repairs; the audience measurements were “insufficiently persuasive.” Mara had repeated those phrases during yesterday’s broadcast because they sounded objective. This morning, alone in the control room, they sounded evasive. Numbers could describe the station’s decline, she thought, without measuring what would vanish with it.

At six, she began normally. Her voice was measured and companionable. She reported fog on County Road Nine, a delayed bus route, and a council meeting about the reservoir. Then she mentioned the anonymous request. “I don’t know which names you mean,” she said, “but perhaps you do.” The telephone lit almost immediately.

A retired nurse called first. During the flood of 1977, she said, the station had broadcast the names of families evacuated from the river district. A mechanic remembered a winter when volunteers used the station to match stranded drivers with spare rooms. A former student described listening secretly beneath his blanket while a late-night host read poems submitted by teenagers. Each caller supplied another fragment. Mara wrote names and dates on the backs of expired weather reports until the desk resembled an untidy archive.

Her tone changed without her noticing. The polished cadence of the morning host loosened; pauses grew longer, and her questions became less ceremonial. She stopped summarizing callers and allowed their hesitations to remain on air. Once, after an elderly man forgot the name of the neighbor who had rescued his cattle, Mara waited through twenty-two seconds of silence. In broadcasting, twenty-two seconds is an abyss. That morning, it became an invitation. The man finally remembered: Lenora Pike. Within minutes, Lenora’s daughter called from another state.

At eight, the owner entered the studio and pointed at the clock. Mara nodded but continued. She read the names she had gathered—not as a grand memorial, she insisted, but as evidence of ordinary people answering one another. Her disclaimer was characteristic. Mara distrusted spectacle, especially when she was responsible for producing it. Still, the list altered the room. Even the owner removed his coat and sat down.

The transmitter was supposed to go silent at nine. At 8:57, a storm damaged a relay tower beyond the ridge, cutting mobile service to three communities. The county emergency office called the station because its older signal still reached battery-powered radios. The owner stared at Mara. She did not celebrate the irony. She moved the utility bill aside, opened the emergency script, and began reading shelter locations.

By noon, technicians had restored mobile service. The station remained on air through the afternoon under a temporary county agreement. Later, newspapers would describe the event as proof that older systems still mattered. Mara considered that conclusion too convenient. A machine was not valuable merely because it was old, and nostalgia could not repair a transmitter. What mattered, she decided, was whether a system preserved the human habits required to use it: attention, memory, and the willingness to answer when another voice called.

Weeks later, a regional cooperative purchased the station. Mara kept the anonymous note in the studio, although no one admitted sending it. She sometimes suspected the owner; sometimes the retired nurse; sometimes herself, in the imaginative way people invent causes after outcomes are known. On the station’s first cooperative broadcast, she read no heroic list. She announced a missing terrier, a broken traffic light, and rain advancing from the west. Her voice was steady again, but not quite the same.

Part A — Organization and Development

  1. Describe the opening situation and explain why the three envelopes are presented before Mara’s work history.
  2. How does the passage shift from private reflection to public testimony?
  3. Identify one use of chronological organization and explain its effect.
  4. Why does the author delay the storm emergency until late in the passage?
  5. Explain how the final paragraph echoes—and changes—the beginning.

Part B — Tone, Voice, and Reliability

  1. Describe Mara’s voice at the beginning using two precise adjectives and evidence.
  2. Identify the tonal shift during the callers’ stories. What causes it?
  3. What does Mara’s “characteristic” disclaimer reveal about her?
  4. Is Mara a fully reliable interpreter of the station’s value? Explain one strength and one limitation in her perspective.
  5. Analyze the effect of the sentence “In broadcasting, twenty-two seconds is an abyss.”

Part C — Vocabulary and Close Analysis

  1. Use context to define provisional. Identify the clues that shaped your inference; then verify the definition using a reputable online dictionary or encyclopedia and name your source.
  2. What does evasive mean as used in paragraph three, and why is it more precise than “unclear”?
  3. Explain how the contrast between “abyss” and “invitation” develops meaning.
  4. Analyze how the author’s choice to include mundane announcements contributes to the passage’s central idea.

Part D — Synthesis

  1. State the central idea in one complete sentence.
  2. Select two details from different parts of the passage and explain how their placement develops that idea.
  3. Write a well-developed paragraph analyzing how organization, tone, and voice work together to complicate nostalgia.
  4. Evaluate the final sentence. Explain what has changed in Mara’s voice even though it is again “steady.”
