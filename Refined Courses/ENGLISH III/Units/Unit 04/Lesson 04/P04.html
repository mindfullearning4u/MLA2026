Worked Example: Building an Analytical Paragraph

Passage

Jonas repaired the station clock every spring, though trains had stopped decades earlier. When asked why, he said, “A town should know when it has been waiting too long.” In the final scene, he removes the minute hand but leaves the hour hand moving.

Step 1 — Claim

By preserving the hour hand while removing the minute hand, Jonas transforms the clock from a failed schedule into a public measure of historical waiting.

Step 2 — Evidence and Warrant

The earlier statement links timekeeping with communal delay. The altered clock no longer coordinates trains, so its remaining movement must be read against the town’s unresolved past rather than ordinary punctuality.

Step 3 — Elaboration

Removing only the minute hand matters: Jonas rejects precise scheduling without abandoning time entirely. The clock preserves duration while refusing the illusion that the town is progressing normally.

Step 4 — Counterevidence and Qualification

The repair may also be personal habit. Therefore the claim should not assert that every resident accepts the symbolism; it can state that Jonas offers the town a public object through which waiting may be recognized.

Complete Model

Jonas’s altered clock transforms a failed schedule into a public measure of historical waiting. His statement that a town should know when it has waited too long connects timekeeping to collective delay. Because trains no longer run, the moving hour hand cannot perform its original function; instead, it records duration. Removing the minute hand rejects precise scheduling while preserving the broader passage of time. Although the ritual may remain personal to Jonas, the clock offers the town a visible form for recognizing unresolved history.

Second Quick Model

A poem’s repeated conditional “if” may create hesitation. Strong analysis explains how the syntax postpones commitment and whether the final stanza resolves or preserves that uncertainty.
