Worked Example: Evaluating a Source Set

Research Question

How does the repeated image of an unlit lighthouse in “The Winter Harbor” complicate the narrator’s belief that guidance must come from authority?

Primary Text Evidence

The lighthouse remains dark during three decisions, yet fishermen navigate by “the remembered angle of the cliffs.” In the ending, the narrator lights a small window in the keeper’s abandoned house rather than repairing the official beacon.

Candidate A — Maritime Museum Essay

Content: explains lighthouse authority, maintenance, and community navigation practices; includes artifact photographs and archival references.

Evaluation: authoritative and relevant for historical context. It can clarify the contrast between institutional signals and local knowledge, but it cannot decide the story’s symbolism.

Candidate B — Peer-Reviewed Literary Article

Content: analyzes failed public signals in regional fiction, quotes the story, and addresses alternative readings.

Evaluation: directly relevant secondary scholarship. Check the author’s evidence and whether the article discusses the exact image rather than merely the genre.

Candidate C — Anonymous Study Post

Content: states that the lighthouse “obviously symbolizes hope” and provides no citations.

Evaluation: low authority, thin evidence, and leading interpretation. It may suggest a claim to test, but it should not be used as support.

Candidate D — 1912 Keeper’s Log

Content: firsthand record of outages and improvised navigation at a comparable harbor.

Evaluation: primary historical evidence with valuable detail, but its provenance and connection to the fictional setting must be verified. It supports context, not authorial intent.

Selection and Synthesis

Use the primary text as the analytical center. Use A to explain institutional and local navigation, B to test an interpretive claim, and D cautiously as contextual evidence. Exclude C. A defensible synthesis would show how the story contrasts official guidance with distributed knowledge while acknowledging that the final light preserves, rather than completely rejects, the idea of a beacon.

Second Quick Model

For a poem using courtroom diction, a legal dictionary may define terms, a historical trial transcript may supply context, and scholarship may analyze the poem. Each source answers a different subquestion; none substitutes for the poem.
