Direct Teaching: Choosing Sources by Function

Source Type Is Relational

A novel is a primary source when you analyze its language. A contemporary review is primary evidence of early reception but secondary commentary on the novel. A modern scholarly article is secondary. A literature encyclopedia is tertiary. Classification depends on the research question.

The A-E-C-P-R Test

  1. Authority: Who created it, and what qualifies that person or institution?
  2. Evidence: What documents, texts, methods, or citations support its claims?
  3. Context and currency: When and why was it produced, and does date matter?
  4. Purpose and perspective: Inform, persuade, sell, commemorate, entertain, or advocate?
  5. Relevance: Which part of the research question does it help answer?

Example 1 — Same Source, Different Function

A 1925 newspaper review can show how early readers responded to a novel. It is weak evidence for the author’s private intention unless supported by a letter or manuscript. It may also reflect the newspaper’s cultural assumptions.

Example 2 — Scholarly Disagreement

Scholar A interprets a recurring garden as political resistance; Scholar B treats it as private memory. Do not average the claims. Compare their textual evidence, warrants, theoretical assumptions, and treatment of counterevidence.

Example 3 — Reference and Discovery

An encyclopedia can define a historical movement and supply search terms. It should not become the only support for a nuanced literary claim when stronger scholarship is available.

Common Mistakes

  • Assuming primary means unbiased.
  • Assuming secondary means less valuable.
  • Using popularity as proof of authority.
  • Choosing a source because it mentions the title.
  • Quoting an abstract or search snippet without reading the source.
  • Letting outside commentary replace close reading.

Responsible Search and AI Use

Begin with library databases, museums, universities, government archives, reputable dictionaries, or encyclopedias. AI may suggest search language or evaluation questions, but it is not evidence. Open, read, verify, and record the actual source. Keep every search tied to the literary research question.
