Direct Teaching: Building a Source Conversation

The Synthesis Cycle

  1. State a provisional interpretation from close reading.
  2. Group evidence by concept, not source.
  3. Name each source’s claim, evidence, method, and limits.
  4. Identify relationships: support, complicate, qualify, extend, contradict.
  5. Revise the interpretation in response.
  6. Integrate evidence with signal phrases and accurate paraphrase.
  7. Explain the reasoning after every source use.

Example — Support and Qualification

Text claim: A locked garden represents exclusion.

A historical source supports the association between walled gardens and ownership. A disability-studies article qualifies the claim by showing that gates can also mark negotiated access. The revised interpretation recognizes exclusion while analyzing who controls entry and under what conditions.

Example — Apparent Agreement

Two scholars call a narrator “unreliable,” but one means factually mistaken and the other means ethically evasive. Synthesis must preserve the distinction instead of reporting simple agreement.

Example — Counterevidence

A diary from the period may contradict a scholarly generalization about social expectations. One diary does not disprove a broad pattern, but it can expose variation and require qualification.

Common Mistakes

  • One paragraph per source.
  • Quotation stacking without reasoning.
  • Using “agrees” when sources address different questions.
  • Ignoring counterevidence.
  • Allowing scholarship to overrule clear textual details.
  • Paraphrasing too closely.

Research and AI Boundary

Use actual reputable sources and follow their citation trails. AI may help brainstorm categories or search terms, but verify every claim in the original source and never cite generated text as scholarship.
