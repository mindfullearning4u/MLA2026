Worked Example: A Three-Source Synthesis

Literary Evidence

In “The Flooded Orchard,” Lina preserves one salt-damaged tree while replacing the others. She calls it “the witness that cannot testify,” then grafts a living branch onto its trunk.

Provisional Interpretation

The damaged tree represents memory that remains meaningful even when it cannot provide a complete factual record.

Source A — Environmental History

A museum study explains that communities sometimes retain damaged trees as disaster markers. This supports the memorial function but does not establish the fictional tree’s full meaning.

Source B — Memory Scholarship

A scholarly article argues that material objects can preserve emotional truth while offering incomplete historical evidence. This extends the interpretation and clarifies “cannot testify.”

Source C — Horticultural Guide

A university guide explains that grafting joins living growth to established rootstock. This supports the literal action and enables analysis of continuity, but it cannot prove symbolism.

Relationship Map

  • A supports public memorial context.
  • B complicates the difference between memory and factual testimony.
  • C extends the analysis through the physical logic of grafting.

Revised Synthesis

The tree becomes neither a perfect archive nor a discarded ruin. Disaster-marker practice explains its public memorial role; memory scholarship clarifies its incomplete testimony; grafting science shows continuity through living attachment. Together, the sources support an interpretation in which Lina preserves loss while refusing to make damage the orchard’s final condition.

Alternative Interpretation

The graft may also reveal control: Lina decides which past survives. A fuller analysis should test whether the story presents that authority as care, erasure, or both.
