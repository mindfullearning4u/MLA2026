Lesson: The Literary Investigation Protocol

Stage 1 — Begin with the Text

  1. Read for plot, ideas, and unresolved tensions.
  2. Mark repeated images, structural shifts, unusual diction, silences, and contradictions.
  3. Frame a question that is debatable, narrow enough to investigate, and answerable through evidence.

Stage 2 — Build a Deliberate Evidence Set

  1. Collect textual evidence from different parts of the work.
  2. Identify exactly what outside knowledge is necessary.
  3. Select credible primary and secondary sources suited to that need.
  4. Record each source’s claim, evidence, limitation, and relationship to other sources.

Stage 3 — Synthesize and Test

  1. Group evidence by ideas rather than by source.
  2. Explain whether sources confirm, extend, qualify, or contradict one another.
  3. Return repeatedly to the literary text.
  4. Test a counterinterpretation using the same evidence.
  5. Qualify the final claim where evidence remains incomplete.

Mini-Example 1 — Historical Fiction

A story’s repeated ration-card imagery may suggest deprivation, but historical research on rationing can reveal unequal access and community exchange. The context should clarify why different characters experience the same policy differently, not become a report about the war.

Mini-Example 2 — Science and Poetry

A poem comparing grief to an eclipse may be analyzed symbolically. Basic astronomical knowledge—that an eclipse is alignment and shadow, not disappearance—can extend the interpretation: absence is experienced as darkness even when the obscured body remains present.

Mini-Example 3 — Law and Drama

In a courtroom drama, a character’s refusal to answer may appear guilty. Knowledge about privilege against self-incrimination complicates that inference. The analysis must still examine dialogue, staging, and the reactions of other characters.

Common Failure Patterns

  • Source parade: summarizing sources one after another without relationships.
  • Confirmation search: keeping only evidence that agrees with the first idea.
  • Decorative quotation: inserting a quotation without explaining its role.
  • Context takeover: writing a history or science report instead of literary analysis.
  • False certainty: hiding ambiguity that the evidence cannot resolve.
  • Unverified AI claim: relying on generated information that was not checked against a reputable source.
