The Unfinished Translation

Professor Hale left three translations of the same poem on his desk. The first was literal and nearly unreadable. The second moved gracefully but omitted the poem’s repeated references to weaving. The third preserved the weaving images yet changed the final question into a statement.

Nadia had been hired to prepare one version for publication. The original poem was written in a coastal language now spoken fluently by fewer than two hundred people. Its central word, vara, could mean thread, route, obligation, or the wake left behind a boat. Hale’s notes warned against choosing too quickly.

Nadia began with the literal draft. In the opening stanza, a grandmother “ties the road to the child’s wrist.” A dictionary favored thread, while an ethnographic source described braided cords used to mark family commitments. A maritime glossary used the same word for a visible path across water. Each source clarified a possibility; none decided the poem.

She called Mara Iven, a fluent speaker and boatbuilder. Mara disliked all three drafts. “You keep asking which English word equals vara,” she said. “The poem asks how many meanings can travel together.” She explained that the final question could sound grammatical, doubtful, or gently challenging depending on cadence.

Nadia returned to the poem’s structure. Weaving appeared in the first, fourth, and final stanzas. Boat imagery filled the middle. The last line returned to the grandmother’s hands but ended with rising intonation. The pattern suggested that family obligation and travel were not competing meanings. The poem braided them.

Her published translation retained vara in three places and included a brief translator’s note. Some reviewers praised the choice as honest. Another argued that untranslated words transferred the translator’s difficulty to readers. Nadia accepted the criticism but rejected the assumption that fluency required hiding every resistance.

At a public reading, Mara spoke the original before Nadia read the translation. Listeners who understood neither language heard the recurring sounds and the lift of the final question. The meaning was not complete, Nadia knew. Yet incompleteness was different from failure. A translation could reveal its seams and still carry something across.

Analysis

  1. What problem is established by the three competing translations?
  2. Explain how the multiple meanings of vara create the central interpretive challenge.
  3. How do the dictionary, ethnographic source, maritime glossary, and Mara’s expertise function differently?
  4. Analyze how the poem’s structure helps Nadia synthesize meanings.
  5. Why does Nadia preserve the original word in three places?

Research and Source Synthesis

  1. Which source is primary, and which are secondary or contextual? Explain.
  2. Use context to define ethnographic, then name the kind of reputable reference source that could verify your inference.
  3. Explain one limitation of relying only on the dictionary.
  4. How does the reviewer’s criticism function as a counterinterpretation?

Synthesis and Evaluation

  2. Write a paragraph explaining how close reading, subject knowledge, and multiple sources lead Nadia to a defensible but qualified translation.
  3. Evaluate the final metaphor about revealing seams while carrying something across. How does it synthesize the passage?
