The Archive Beneath the Reservoir

By late August, the reservoir had withdrawn far enough to reveal the roof of the old records office. Each day another line of stone emerged from the mud: first the chimney, then the window arches, then the carved date above the entrance—1906. People drove out to photograph the building that their grandparents had watched disappear when the valley was flooded.

Mina Orr came with a city archive badge and a list of objects she was authorized to recover. The dam authority had given her forty-eight hours before controlled releases raised the water. Her list included council ledgers, property maps, and a steel cabinet believed to contain birth records. It did not include the mural in the western hall because a mural could not be carried up the temporary walkway.

Inside, silt covered the floor in overlapping fans. Water had peeled paint from the walls, leaving islands of color: a blue sleeve, a wheat sheaf, half a locomotive. Mina photographed each fragment and marked its position. Her assistant, Theo, called the images beautiful. Mina called them incomplete.

In the records room, the ledgers had fused into swollen blocks. The steel cabinet stood open and empty. Someone had removed the birth records before the flooding, although no transfer appeared in the surviving inventory. Theo suggested that the missing records had been lost. Mina wrote location unknown. Archivists, she told him, should not turn absence into an event without evidence.

A group gathered behind the safety barrier outside. Among them was Mrs. Varo, whose family had owned a bakery in the valley. She carried a school photograph showing children posed beneath the finished mural. In the black-and-white image, the western wall formed a single scene: factory workers, farmers, railroad porters, and women sorting mail. Mrs. Varo said her mother had identified one of the figures as Mina’s great-grandfather.

Mina compared the photograph with the wall. The visible blue sleeve belonged to a postal uniform. The wheat sheaf lay beside a painted gear, joining farm and factory in a composition more deliberate than the surviving fragments suggested. Yet the photograph also revealed omissions. The Indigenous settlement that had preceded the town was absent, as were the laborers who built the dam that later submerged it.

That evening, a reporter asked Mina whether the mural proved the lost valley had been unusually united. She declined the word proved. Public murals, she explained, often present the identity a community wishes to display. The reporter revised the question: Did the mural lie?

“A selection is not automatically a lie,” Mina said. “But we should ask who selected.”

During the second day, Theo found a narrow drawer wedged behind a collapsed shelf. It held meeting notes from the mural committee. The notes showed disputes over which occupations deserved representation. A railroad union requested two figures; the council reduced them to one. The bakery association offered funds after securing a place for a delivery wagon. A teacher proposed including the earlier settlement, but the committee postponed the issue until a local historian could advise them. No later meeting addressed it.

The discovery made the mural more valuable and less innocent. It was no longer merely an image of unity or an example of exclusion. It was evidence of negotiation: money, civic pride, labor politics, and unfinished historical responsibility arranged beneath a layer of paint.

As the water began rising, Mina faced a decision. Conservation experts had warned that removing the remaining plaster would destroy most of the pigment. Leaving it meant the mural would disappear again. She used her final hours to create a scaled photographic record linking every fragment to the school photograph and committee notes. Mrs. Varo donated her photograph to the archive but asked that the original be returned after scanning.

Weeks later, the city museum opened a digital reconstruction. Visitors could select a painted figure, examine the surviving fragment, read the committee debate, and view records about occupations or communities the mural omitted. The reconstruction did not restore the original scene seamlessly. Missing areas remained gray.

Some visitors complained that the gray spaces made the display look unfinished. Mina considered that its greatest accuracy. The mural had always been unfinished, although fresh paint once concealed the fact. The reservoir had not created every absence. It had merely made some of them visible.

Part A — Interpretive Question and Close Reading

  1. Identify the central interpretive tension established by the emerging building and the limited recovery list.
  2. How does Mina’s distinction between “lost” and “location unknown” establish her analytical values?
  3. Analyze the changing meaning of the mural’s fragments after Mrs. Varo provides the photograph.
  4. What does Mina mean by “A selection is not automatically a lie”?
  5. Explain how the committee notes complicate both the unity interpretation and the exclusion interpretation.

Part B — Research Design and Source Evaluation

  1. Frame a focused interpretive question that could guide an investigation of the mural.
  2. Identify two different source types in the passage and explain how their evidence differs.
  3. What narrow historical knowledge about public murals would help evaluate the reporter’s “proof” claim?
  4. Use a reputable museum, university, or encyclopedia source to verify the passage-specific term conservation as it applies to art or archives. Record the source and connect the definition to Mina’s final decision.
  5. Create a four-entry synthesis matrix in prose: identify one textual detail or source that confirms, extends, complicates, and limits an interpretation.

Part C — Organization, Voice, and Subject Knowledge

  1. Analyze how the two-day deadline organizes the passage and shapes tension.
  2. Describe Mina’s voice using two precise adjectives and evidence.
  3. How does archival subject knowledge deepen the meaning of “absence” in the passage?
  4. Identify one counterinterpretation of the gray spaces and test it against the ending.
  5. Explain why the digital reconstruction preserves visible gaps rather than presenting a seamless restoration.

Part D — Synthesis

  1. State a defensible central interpretation in one precise sentence.
  2. Write a well-developed paragraph synthesizing the mural, photograph, committee notes, and digital reconstruction. Explain the relationships among these sources.
  3. Evaluate the final three sentences. How do they qualify the passage’s ideas about loss, preservation, and historical responsibility?
