The Cartographer’s Window

Every map in Ilyan’s shop placed north at the top, except the unfinished chart above his desk. That map turned the harbor sideways. The lighthouse rested at the lower edge, the market leaned across the center, and the governor’s house appeared so small that its label had to be written beyond the border.

Customers called the chart inaccurate. Ilyan answered that accuracy depended on the traveler. Fishermen entered through the eastern channel and watched the reefs rise before the city walls. Traders measured distance from the warehouses. Children knew the harbor by the steps where they lowered jars for minnows. “A map begins where its maker stands,” he said, though he never allowed anyone to see where he had marked his own position.

After the governor ordered a new official map, Ilyan’s apprentice, Sera, began copying tax roads and property lines from public records. The work was precise, but each night she returned to the sideways chart. She noticed that alleys connecting workers’ homes to the docks were drawn as carefully as ceremonial avenues. The abandoned quarantine island carried three names: its legal title, the sailors’ nickname, and a word in a language Sera did not recognize.

She searched shipping ledgers and found the unfamiliar word beside lists of passengers who had died before reaching shore. An old medical report described the island as necessary protection; a sailor’s diary called it a place where voices vanished within sight of home. Sera copied both phrases into her notes. Neither source made the other disappear.

When Ilyan fell ill, the official commission asked Sera to finish the governor’s map. She completed it on time. Then she placed a smaller window in its corner: a second orientation showing the harbor from the eastern channel, with the island’s three names and a dotted path toward the city. The governor objected that the inset complicated a useful document. Sera replied, “Then let it teach usefulness to become more complete.”

Questions 1–6: Meaning and Inquiry

  1. Infer orientation in the final paragraph; explain two clues, verify the definition, and record the source.
  2. State the passage’s central tension.
  3. Identify three repeated patterns or details that could generate inquiry.
  4. Explain how the official map and sideways chart differ in purpose.
  5. What ambiguity surrounds Ilyan’s hidden position?
  6. How do the medical report and sailor’s diary complicate one another?

Questions 7–12: Research Question Design

  1. Write one broad topic suggested by the passage.
  2. Turn it into a focused how/why question.
  3. Revise the question so it names a specific pattern and interpretive tension.
  4. Explain why reasonable readers could disagree.
  5. Identify one leading assumption and remove it.
  6. Set the question’s scope: what will and will not be investigated?

Questions 13–18: Research Plan and Evaluation

  1. Create three subquestions.
  2. Identify two types of textual evidence required.
  3. Name one relevant contextual source and explain its purpose.
  4. Provide two precise search terms for that source.
  5. Explain how you would evaluate the source’s credibility and relevance.
  6. Write a five-sentence rationale explaining why your final question is interpretive, researchable, unbiased, and feasible.
