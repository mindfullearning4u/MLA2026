Direct Teaching: Designing a Literary Research Question

The Question Ladder

  1. Notice: identify a repeated pattern, contradiction, ambiguity, or surprising choice.
  2. Ask why or how: move beyond what happened.
  3. Name the text and feature: specify character, image, structure, voice, or conflict.
  4. Require interpretation: ensure reasonable readers could disagree.
  5. Add relevant context: identify what outside knowledge may clarify—not replace—the text.
  6. Test scope and feasibility: confirm the evidence is available and the question is manageable.
  7. Revise: remove assumptions and leading language.

Example 1 — From Topic to Inquiry

Topic: isolation in a novel.

Too broad: How is isolation used?

Focused: How does the novel’s shifting description of closed rooms complicate the protagonist’s claim that solitude is freely chosen?

The revision identifies a pattern, tension, speaker claim, and interpretive task.

Example 2 — Context Without Replacement

Question: How does the speaker’s use of household-accounting language challenge expectations for women’s public authority in the period?

A historical source can clarify social expectations; the literary evidence must still show how diction produces the challenge.

Example 3 — Multiple Genres

  • Poem: How does the repeated seasonal image shift from comfort to warning?
  • Drama: How do interruptions in the trial scene redistribute authority among speakers?
  • Memoir: How does adult hindsight complicate the narrator’s childhood interpretation?

Common Mistakes

  • Factual only: “When was the novel published?”
  • Leading: “Why is the narrator clearly dishonest?”
  • Unbounded: “How does history affect literature?”
  • Research replaces reading: using biography to dictate meaning.
  • Unavailable evidence: requiring sources or manuscripts you cannot access.

Responsible Outside Research

Use reputable library databases, museums, universities, government archives, dictionaries, or encyclopedias. AI may help generate search terms or possible subquestions, but verify every claim and source independently. Research must answer the lesson’s interpretive question—not create unrelated work.
