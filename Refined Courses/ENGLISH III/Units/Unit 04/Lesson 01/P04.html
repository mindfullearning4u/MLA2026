Worked Example: From Close Reading to a Research Plan

Model Passage — “The Observatory Key”

Mara’s grandfather had locked the hilltop observatory each winter, though the telescope had not worked in years. “Some doors protect what is inside,” he told her, “and some protect us from learning what is gone.” After his death, Mara carried the brass key for three months before climbing the hill. She expected dust. Instead, she found the dome freshly swept and a notebook open beneath the cracked lens. Each page recorded the date, the weather, and one sentence beginning, “Tonight I could not see—.”

Mara read entries about clouds, broken gears, city glare, and, finally, failing eyesight. The last sentence stopped after the dash. She placed the key beside the notebook but did not open the dome. At dawn, she returned with a box, a soft cloth, and the address of the regional astronomy society.

Step 1 — Notice Patterns and Tensions

The passage repeats barriers to seeing: the lock, cracked lens, weather, glare, broken equipment, and eyesight. Yet the final action suggests that acknowledging loss may lead to restoration rather than closure.

Step 2 — Generate Candidate Questions

  • What does the key symbolize? Too familiar and underspecified.
  • Why did the grandfather lock the observatory? Useful, but may invite plot speculation.
  • How does the passage transform failed observation from evidence of loss into a motive for communal action? Interpretive and evidence-rich.

Step 3 — Refine the Working Question

Working question: How do the passage’s repeated images of obstructed vision and Mara’s final actions transform private grief into a decision to preserve shared knowledge?

The question names two evidence streams, establishes a tension, and remains open to qualification.

Step 4 — Identify Research Needs

  • A reputable astronomy or museum source on preserving community observatories.
  • A dictionary or encyclopedia entry distinguishing observatory, telescope, and dome functions.
  • No biographical research is needed because the passage is fictional and self-contained.

Step 5 — Build Subquestions

  1. How does the grandfather’s statement frame knowledge as both desired and painful?
  2. How does the unfinished sentence affect the meaning of the notebook?
  3. What changes when Mara contacts a society rather than repairing the site alone?

Step 6 — Test Feasibility and Bias

The text supplies repeated imagery and a clear final action; accessible contextual sources can clarify preservation. The question does not assume Mara succeeds or that the grandfather intended her decision.

Second Model and Counterexample

Poem question: How does a river image shift across three stanzas to complicate the speaker’s confidence in inherited memory?

Counterexample: “How does the river prove memory is unreliable?” is leading because it declares the conclusion before analysis.
