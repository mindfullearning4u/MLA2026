Worked Example: Evaluating a Civic Essay

Model Passage

Opening: Each summer the eastern plaza becomes too hot to cross at midday. Thermometers record surface temperatures above 120 degrees, yet the city’s cooling plan begins with decorative banners.

How the Heat Builds: Dark pavement absorbs sunlight; sparse trees provide little shade; bus riders wait beside reflective glass. Emergency calls rise during heat waves.

What Other Cities Changed: Comparable cities planted canopy trees, installed shaded transit stops, and replaced sections of dark pavement.

A Practical Sequence: Begin with transit stops, then tree corridors, then pavement replacement during scheduled repairs.

Conclusion: A banner may announce concern. Shade demonstrates it.

Map the Structure

  1. Concrete problem and ironic contrast.
  2. Cause-and-effect explanation.
  3. Comparison with precedents.
  4. Phased solution ordered by feasibility.
  5. Memorable concluding contrast.

Fully Modeled Reasoning

Claim: The essay’s problem–cause–comparison–solution sequence is effective for city officials because it converts an observable complaint into a feasible policy argument.

Evidence: The writer explains heat mechanisms before describing other cities and ranks local actions by implementation.

Reasoning: Readers first understand why the problem occurs, then see that solutions have precedent, and finally receive an order that acknowledges budget and scheduling constraints.

Alternative Analysis

For residents already convinced of the danger, the long causal section may feel unnecessary; a narrative from a bus rider could create stronger urgency. This counterinterpretation does not invalidate the structure—it evaluates effectiveness for a different audience.

Second Quick Model

A speech that places a concession before its strongest evidence may build credibility with skeptical listeners, while the same delay could weaken urgency for an already supportive crowd. Structural effectiveness depends on audience and purpose.
