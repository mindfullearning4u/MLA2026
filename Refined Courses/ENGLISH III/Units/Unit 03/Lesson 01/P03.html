Direct Teaching: Structure as an Authorial Decision

A Six-Step Evaluation

  1. State the author’s purpose and intended audience.
  2. Outline the major sections in your own words.
  3. Name the dominant structure and any embedded structures.
  4. Identify transitions and text features that signal movement.
  5. Trace what the order makes readers notice, expect, or accept.
  6. Evaluate effectiveness using a criterion: clarity, emphasis, credibility, logic, accessibility, or emotional impact.

Example 1 — Historical Speech

A speaker repeats “We have waited” before three examples of denied rights, then shifts to “We will act.” The refrain unifies evidence and makes the structural turn from patience to action unmistakable.

Example 2 — Scientific Essay

An essay begins with a local algae bloom, explains nutrient runoff through cause and effect, and ends with three solutions ranked by feasibility. The concrete opening creates relevance; causal structure prevents the solutions from seeming arbitrary.

Example 3 — Ambiguous Memoir

A memoir reveals the ending first, then reconstructs earlier choices through flashbacks. This sacrifices suspense about what happened but creates a different question: why the narrator failed to recognize warning signs.

Common Mistake and Correction

Weak: “The author uses chronological order.”

Stronger: “Chronological order allows each failed compromise to accumulate, so the final demand appears reasoned rather than impulsive.”

Targeted Research and AI Use

When a passage contains an unfamiliar historical reference or word, infer meaning from context first. Then verify only what the lesson requires using a reputable online dictionary or encyclopedia. If AI is permitted, treat it as a research aid, verify its claims, record the source consulted, and write the explanation in your own words.
