Worked Example: Two Reports on the Same Pilot Program

Shared Information

A weekend bus pilot carried 2,400 rides over eight weeks, cost $18 per ride, served four neighborhoods, and produced 61 survey responses.

Report A — Expansion Memo

“More than two thousand rides connected four underserved neighborhoods. Most survey respondents supported continuation.”

Report B — Budget Brief

“The pilot cost $18 per ride, and only 61 riders completed the survey. Demand remains uncertain.”

Modeled Analysis

  • A emphasizes total use, geography, and positive response; it omits cost and response rate.
  • B emphasizes cost and uncertainty; it omits neighborhood access and total rides.
  • Both are factually compatible, but each selects information serving a different decision frame.

Evaluation

A may persuade equity-focused officials; B may persuade budget reviewers. Neither is fully fair without comparison to regular-route costs, unique rider counts, and survey response rate. The strongest decision document would contextualize all measures.

Second Model

“The river returned to its floodplain” and “the river invaded residential streets” use the same event to center ecological history or human loss. Verbs assign agency and implied normality.
