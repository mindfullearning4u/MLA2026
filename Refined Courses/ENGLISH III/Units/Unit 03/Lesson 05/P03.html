Direct Teaching: Facts Do Not Frame Themselves

Comparison Process

  1. List information both texts share.
  2. Mark unique inclusions and omissions.
  3. Compare labels, verbs, order, headings, and baselines.
  4. Identify each audience and purpose.
  5. Trace the response each frame invites.
  6. Check accuracy and relevant missing context.
  7. Evaluate fairness separately from effectiveness.

Example 1 — Statistics

“Complaints fell 50%” and “complaints fell from four to two” describe the same data. The percentage emphasizes improvement; raw numbers emphasize small scale.

Example 2 — Historical Event

A mayor calls a march “an interruption of commerce”; an organizer calls it “a public demand after private requests failed.” Both may report the same closure but contextualize its meaning differently.

Example 3 — Ethical Complexity

A safety campaign selects worst-case images to motivate caution. It may be effective but unfair if it hides typical outcomes. Evaluate purpose, accuracy, proportionality, and audience impact separately.

Research Boundary

Infer unfamiliar wording first. Verify required definitions or factual context with a reputable dictionary or encyclopedia and record the source. Independently check AI-generated leads.

Common Mistake

Different framing is not automatically deception. Prove distortion by identifying inaccurate language, missing relevant context, or a misleading comparison.
