P03 Notebook Task - Part 2

Apply the organizer to a Classical-style passage.

Practice Passage

Citizens, a city is not guarded by stone alone. It is guarded by laws that can be understood, by leaders who can be questioned, and by neighbors who can speak without fear. If the gate is strong but the voice is chained, the city has defended its walls and surrendered its spirit.

Notebook Directions

  1. Write the central idea in one sentence.
  2. Identify one structural move or repeated pattern.
  3. Identify one rhetorical choice.
  4. Explain the effect of the rhetorical choice.
  5. Write one integrated analysis sentence.

Model Start

Central idea: A city needs understandable law and free voices, not only physical protection.

Rhetorical choice: The author contrasts a strong gate with a chained voice.

Your turn: Explain the effect and connect it to the argument.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: Giving a broad answer without identifying the exact evidence, rhetorical move, literary choice, or author purpose.

Correct: Name the specific evidence or choice and explain how it supports meaning, argument, style, or reader effect.

Why this matters: English III mastery requires clear reasoning. The reader must see how your evidence proves the claim.

Teachable Explanation

Compare the incorrect and correct versions by checking the exact passage details, the lesson standard, and the meaning that must stay accurate.

Deeper Learning Support

Lesson focus: Integrated Classical text, argument, and rhetoric analysis

Mapped standard focus: MLA.ENG3.R.12

Use this second-pass routine to check the quality of your reasoning before you submit your notebook work.

  1. Identify the speaker or writer, audience, purpose, and occasion before judging the argument or rhetoric. These details shape what the choices are meant to do.
  2. Separate claim, evidence, reasoning, and rhetorical technique. A strong analysis names the move and explains how it affects the audience.
  3. Evaluate effectiveness, not just presence. Ask whether the structure, appeal, diction, repetition, or contrast actually helps the author achieve the purpose.
  4. Use precise academic language such as claim, validity, reasoning, appeal, effect, audience, and purpose so your analysis sounds mature and defensible.
