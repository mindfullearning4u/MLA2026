Worked Example: Integrating Argument and Rhetoric

Model Passage — “The Public Square”

Citizens often praise public debate while quietly avoiding the places where debate becomes possible. The old courthouse square is one such place. Its stones are uneven, its fountain needs repair, and its meetings can be inconvenient. Yet inconvenience is not evidence of uselessness. A square is not valuable because every gathering is orderly; it is valuable because disagreement can occur without exile.

Some propose leasing half the square for private events. They correctly note that fees could fund repairs. But a gate that opens only after a ticket is purchased changes more than revenue. It changes who may appear, who may listen, and whose presence must be justified. A public square cannot remain fully public if access becomes a privilege rented by the hour.

Repair the fountain. Publish the maintenance costs. Schedule private events only when free public access remains substantial. Let the square earn revenue without surrendering its civic purpose. The question is not whether public space may change. The question is whether change will preserve the right that made the space public in the first place.

1. Rhetorical Situation

The speaker addresses residents and officials deciding how to finance repairs. The purpose is not to reject all private events but to preserve meaningful public access while acknowledging financial constraints.

2. Line of Reasoning

  1. The square enables visible civic disagreement.
  2. Private fees could support repairs, but paid access changes who may participate.
  3. Therefore, revenue measures are acceptable only if substantial free access remains.

The crucial warrant is that public status depends on practical access, not ownership alone.

3. Rhetorical Choices

The opening concession about physical flaws establishes realism. The antithesis “inconvenience is not evidence of uselessness” rejects a weak inference. The repeated clause “who may…” converts an administrative change into questions of belonging. Short imperatives in paragraph 3 create a practical, controlled cadence.

4. Evaluation

The passage fairly acknowledges revenue benefits and offers a compromise, strengthening credibility. Its weakness is missing evidence: it gives no repair estimate, proposed fee schedule, or access data. The argument is conceptually strong but empirically incomplete.

5. Integrated Synthesis

The writer combines concession, antithesis, repetition, and a concrete compromise to argue that financial adaptation must preserve civic access. These choices make the argument sound principled yet practical; however, the proposal requires cost and usage evidence before officials could judge feasibility.

Alternative Interpretation

A reader could argue that limited private rentals would not meaningfully exclude the public. That interpretation is plausible because duration matters. The passage anticipates it partly through “substantial” free access, but the undefined term becomes a point requiring policy evidence.

Second Quick Model

Claim: A school archive should include oral histories.

Strategy: An analogy compares official records to a map that shows roads but not journeys.

Evaluation: The analogy clarifies what records omit, but it cannot prove that every memory is accurate; corroboration remains necessary.
