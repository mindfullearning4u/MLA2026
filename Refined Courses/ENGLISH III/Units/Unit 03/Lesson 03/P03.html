Direct Teaching: Purpose Beyond “Inform” or “Persuade”

A Precise Purpose Statement

Use this pattern: The author seeks to [specific effect] among [audience] by [major rhetorical strategy] so that [desired response or understanding].

Analysis Process

  1. Identify occasion and exigence.
  2. Describe the actual and implied audiences.
  3. Track what the author wants the audience to believe, feel, reconsider, or do.
  4. Separate primary and secondary purposes.
  5. Analyze ethos, pathos, logos, structure, and diction in combination.
  6. Notice purpose shifts.
  7. Evaluate effectiveness using audience-specific evidence.
  8. Consider ethical costs or exclusions.

Example 1 — Funeral Speech

A speaker praises a leader but repeatedly names unfinished reforms. The purpose is not only commemoration; it converts grief into obligation and positions listeners as heirs to incomplete work.

Example 2 — Civic Essay

An essay calmly defines taxation, concedes waste, and compares services received. Its purpose is to move skeptical property owners from resentment toward conditional support, not to celebrate taxation generally.

Example 3 — Ethical Tension

A wartime speech uses fear to motivate preparedness. It may be effective, yet analysis should ask whether exaggerated threats manipulate the audience or silence dissent.

Targeted Research

Infer unfamiliar passage words and allusions first. Verify only context necessary for the purpose analysis with a reputable dictionary or encyclopedia; record the source. AI may suggest leads but must be independently checked.

Common Mistake

Weak: “The purpose is to persuade.” Stronger: “The speaker seeks to transform private concern into coordinated civic action by pairing local evidence with a shared ethical obligation.”
