Direct Teaching: Testing Arguments Fairly

Evaluation Process

  1. State each conclusion neutrally.
  2. List premises and evidence.
  3. Identify hidden assumptions.
  4. Test evidence for relevance, credibility, and sufficiency.
  5. Check whether conclusions logically follow.
  6. Examine counterclaims and concessions.
  7. Identify fallacies without using labels as shortcuts.
  8. Compare arguments with stated criteria.

Example 1 — Valid but Unsound

If every old building is unsafe, and the theater is old, then it is unsafe. The form is valid, but the first premise is unsupported; therefore the argument is not sound.

Example 2 — Relevant but Insufficient

One student improved after tutoring. The evidence is relevant to tutoring’s possible benefit but insufficient to prove every student will improve.

Example 3 — Competing Criteria

One transit argument prioritizes total ridership; another prioritizes access for neighborhoods without alternatives. Neither can be evaluated fairly until the decision-maker states whether efficiency, equity, or both are the criteria.

Research Boundary

Infer unfamiliar words first, then verify required definitions or factual premises with a reputable dictionary or encyclopedia. Record the source; verify AI-generated leads independently.

Common Mistake

Disagreement with a conclusion does not prove invalid reasoning. Test the logical connection and premises separately.
