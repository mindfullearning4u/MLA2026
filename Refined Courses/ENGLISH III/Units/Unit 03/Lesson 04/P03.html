P03 Notebook Task - Part 2

Apply argument comparison to paired passages.

Shared Topic

Should the city repair the old public fountain or replace it with a new water station?

Argument A

The city should repair the old fountain. It stands in the center of the square and reminds citizens of shared history. Repairing it would preserve a public symbol while restoring water access.

Argument B

The city should replace the fountain with a new water station. The old fountain is difficult for children and older citizens to use. A modern station would provide cleaner access and serve more people safely.

Notebook Task

  1. Identify the claim in Argument A.
  2. Identify the claim in Argument B.
  3. Explain the reasoning in Argument A.
  4. Explain the reasoning in Argument B.
  5. Which reasoning is more effective for public safety? Explain.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: Both arguments are about water, so they are the same.

Why it is weak: Shared topic is not the same as shared reasoning.

Correct: Argument A values history and symbolism, while Argument B values access and safety.

Why it works: It compares reasoning instead of only naming the topic.

Teachable Explanation

Compare the incorrect and correct versions by checking the exact passage details, the lesson standard, and the meaning that must stay accurate.

Deeper Learning Support

Lesson focus: Multiple arguments, claims, validity, and reasoning

Mapped standard focus: MLA.ENG3.R.08

Use this second-pass routine to check the quality of your reasoning before you submit your notebook work.

  1. Identify the speaker or writer, audience, purpose, and occasion before judging the argument or rhetoric. These details shape what the choices are meant to do.
  2. Separate claim, evidence, reasoning, and rhetorical technique. A strong analysis names the move and explains how it affects the audience.
  3. Evaluate effectiveness, not just presence. Ask whether the structure, appeal, diction, repetition, or contrast actually helps the author achieve the purpose.
  4. Use precise academic language such as claim, validity, reasoning, appeal, effect, audience, and purpose so your analysis sounds mature and defensible.
