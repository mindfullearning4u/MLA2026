Direct Teaching: From Comparison to Synthesis

A Six-Step Synthesis Method

  1. Identify the shared question.
  2. Map each source separately: claim, reasoning, evidence, rhetoric, context.
  3. Choose common criteria so the comparison remains fair.
  4. Locate convergence, divergence, and tension.
  5. Explain the relationship: support, complicate, qualify, extend, or contradict.
  6. Form a qualified synthesis claim supported by evidence from both texts.

Example 1 — Topic Is Not Yet Synthesis

Weak: Both writers discuss public parks.

Stronger: Both defend public access, but one treats access as a legal right while the other treats it as a condition for community health. Together, they show that access can be justified through distinct civic values.

Example 2 — Meaningful Divergence

Source A argues that emergency decisions require speed and centralized authority. Source B warns that authority without public reporting becomes difficult to correct. A synthesis need not choose one absolutely: rapid action may be justified initially, while transparency becomes essential as the emergency continues.

Example 3 — Evidence Relationships

A speech’s personal story can illustrate stakes, while a report’s statistics establish scale. The sources corroborate only if they address the same claim; vividness alone does not equal proof.

Example 4 — Context and Rhetoric

A wartime appeal and a later historical essay may praise sacrifice for different purposes. Analyze the immediate audience and constraints before treating similar diction as identical reasoning.

Common Mistakes

  • Writing two separate summaries with no relationship.
  • Comparing unrelated features.
  • Assuming disagreement means one source is useless.
  • Flattening important qualifications.
  • Using only one source to support a “synthesis.”
  • Declaring one text more effective without criteria.

Passage Vocabulary Research

Infer each passage-specific word from context, then verify it with a reputable online dictionary or encyclopedia and record the source. AI may support searching, but independently verify and keep the research aligned to the assigned synthesis.
