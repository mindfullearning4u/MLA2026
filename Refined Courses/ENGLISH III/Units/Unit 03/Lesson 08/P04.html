Worked Example: Synthesizing Two Civic Arguments

Shared Question

When a historic public building no longer serves its original function, what obligations should guide its future use?

Source A — Preservation Committee Statement

The Union Hall was built by workers who donated one day’s wages for each row of brick. Its meeting room held contract debates, citizenship classes, and winter food drives. To reduce the building to its resale value would mistake price for worth. Restore the hall as a public history center, and require that its largest room remain free for neighborhood meetings. A community that forgets how ordinary people built its institutions may soon imagine that institutions are gifts from the powerful.

Source B — Housing Coalition Testimony

History deserves more than a plaque, but reverence cannot become vacancy. The Union Hall stands three blocks from families living in temporary rooms. Converting the upper floors into affordable apartments would not erase the workers’ legacy; it would extend their belief that collective action should meet practical needs. Preserve the meeting room and exterior, but let the building shelter living neighbors as well as remembered ones.

1. Map Each Argument

A: Preserve the hall primarily as a history center because civic memory sustains democratic understanding. Its analogy contrasts price with worth, and its final warning appeals to historical agency.

B: Combine preservation with housing because honoring collective action should address current need. Its antithesis contrasts “plaque” with “vacancy” and reframes adaptation as extension rather than erasure.

2. Convergence and Divergence

Both preserve the meeting room and workers’ legacy. They diverge over primary use: A prioritizes public historical education; B prioritizes housing while retaining selected historic features. The deeper tension is between preserving a site’s meaning and adapting that meaning through present action.

3. Evaluate Evidence and Context

Both rely mainly on values and historical description. A needs evidence about operating costs and community use. B needs feasibility, renovation, and housing-capacity evidence. Neither passage alone resolves the practical decision.

4. Synthesis Claim

Together, the sources suggest that responsible preservation should protect the hall’s public memory while testing adaptive uses that continue its tradition of collective service. This conclusion is qualified: housing conversion is justified only if feasibility evidence supports it and meaningful public access remains.

Alternative Synthesis

A different defensible synthesis could prioritize the history center if evidence showed housing conversion would destroy essential architecture or provide too few units to justify the loss. The best conclusion depends on criteria and evidence, not on combining proposals mechanically.

Second Quick Model

A disaster speech supplies urgency; an engineering report supplies risk estimates. Their relationship is complementary when both support the same preparedness claim, but the report should determine technical feasibility.
