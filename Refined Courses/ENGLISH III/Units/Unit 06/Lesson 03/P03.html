Lesson: Turn Research Into an Audible Interpretation

1. Build the Interpretive Spine

State the text, interpretive claim, two or three reasons, and the significance of the reading. Every quotation, scholarly source, and visual must attach to that spine.

2. Synthesize, Do Not Stack Sources

Organize by ideas: “Both the novel’s imagery and Rivera’s historical study show…; however, Chen’s reading complicates this by…” This creates a conversation instead of three unrelated summaries.

3. Frame and Analyze Evidence

Name the author or source aloud, provide only the necessary quotation, then analyze a specific word, image, structure, or contrast. Follow the pattern frame → quote → interpret → connect.

4. Address Complexity

Present a counterinterpretation fairly, identify the evidence that makes it plausible, and explain why your claim better accounts for the complete pattern. Qualify conclusions when a source or text remains ambiguous.

5. Design for Listeners

Use one idea per slide, readable quotations, descriptive titles, source labels, high contrast, and alternative text. Speaker notes hold transitions and citations. Slides support the argument; they do not become the script.

Diverse Examples and Common Mistakes

Poetry: compare repeated sounds and a critic’s formal reading. Drama: pair stage directions with performance history. Novel: connect narrative perspective to historical context.

  • Plot summary replacing interpretation.
  • Long quotations without analysis.
  • Scholar names used as authority instead of evidence.
  • Counterarguments caricatured or ignored.
  • Crowded slides and unreadable citations.
