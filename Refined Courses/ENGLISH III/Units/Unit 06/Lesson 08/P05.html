Independent Work: “From Loading Dock to Learning Garden”

Directions

Read the complete source carefully. Answer all 18 questions in complete sentences. Use only research specifically requested in Question 4. Submit these responses with Page 2 vocabulary as Notebook Evidence.

Source

When the Northline Library announced that it would convert its unused loading courtyard into a public learning garden, the proposal sounded simple: remove cracked pavement, add shade, and create space for outdoor classes. Yet the planning archive revealed a more complicated site. The courtyard had once received shipments for neighborhood factories, and its retaining wall carried painted numbers used by drivers who could not see the dock from the alley. Several residents wanted those marks preserved as evidence of working life; others saw only decay.

A student media team was asked to create a digital feature before the final design meeting. They began with a dramatic title—“The Courtyard That Industry Abandoned”—but interviews complicated that language. Retired loader Elena Ruiz remembered exhausting shifts, yet she also described coworkers sharing meals on the dock. Librarian Marcus Bell explained that the library had purchased the building only after three factories closed, while city records showed that one manufacturer had relocated rather than failed. The students changed the title to “From Loading Dock to Learning Garden” because the original phrasing compressed different histories into a single claim.

The team assembled photographs, zoning maps, oral histories, a two-minute ambient audio recording, and soil-test results. Their first draft placed a looping machinery sound beneath every section. During testing, listeners said it made interview speech difficult to understand and implied that machinery had operated continuously. The editor limited the sound to the opening, lowered its volume, and added a visible pause control and transcript note. A scanned photograph showed workers near the numbered wall, but its handwritten date conflicted with a newspaper caption. Rather than choose silently, the team labeled the date “circa 1978” and explained the discrepancy in the source note.

Accessibility testing produced further revisions. A screen-reader user could not tell whether the before-and-after images were decorative or evidentiary. The team rewrote the alt text to identify the wall numbers, loading lanes, and new garden plan. Keyboard testing revealed that the interactive timeline trapped focus, so the developer replaced it with a simpler list and offered a downloadable text version. The color palette was adjusted after contrast testing, and every speaker received a transcript heading.

The final section compared three design choices: erase the wall markings, preserve them unchanged, or stabilize the wall and interpret the markings beside the new garden. The feature did not tell readers which option to select. Instead, it stated evaluation criteria—safety, historical integrity, learning value, cost, and accessibility—and linked each criterion to evidence. Before release, the team verified permissions, removed a private phone number visible in a scanned letter, recorded eight changes, and obtained sign-off from the librarian, student editor, accessibility reviewer, and source checker. The published feature invited readers to the meeting and provided a text-only fallback if interactive media failed.

Publication Synthesis Questions

  1. State the communication brief: audience, purpose, central claim, product, and two constraints.
  2. Explain why the original title was misleading and why the revision is more accurate.
  3. Identify two details that complicate a simple “abandonment” narrative.
  4. In context, define ambient. Verify the definition with two reputable online dictionary or encyclopedia sources and record their titles.
  5. Distinguish what the oral histories establish from what the city records establish.
  6. Explain why the team used “circa 1978” rather than choosing one date.
  7. Analyze the intended and unintended effects of the looping machinery sound.
  8. Explain how the final audio revision improves coherence and accessibility.
  9. Evaluate the revised alt text: what information must it communicate and why?
  10. Explain why replacing the interactive timeline was a stronger decision than merely warning users about it.
  11. Identify two source-integrity checks completed by the team.
  12. Identify two privacy or permission risks and how they were resolved.
  13. Explain how the final section supports informed judgment without dictating an answer.
  14. Choose the strongest piece of evidence for preserving the wall markings and defend your choice.
  15. Propose one useful visual or audio element not already included; state its exact function and accessibility support.
  16. Create an eight-entry change log. For each entry, name the problem, revision, and reason.
  17. Write a 250–320 word reflective rationale explaining how the package integrates evidence, media, accessibility, ethics, and audience needs.
  18. Make a release decision—release, revise, or hold—and defend it with at least four checklist findings.
