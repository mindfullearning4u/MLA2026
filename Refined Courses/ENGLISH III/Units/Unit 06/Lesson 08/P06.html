Checkpoint: “A Hall Rewritten by Its Community”

Directions

Read the different source below and answer all 12 questions. Submit these responses only in the separate Checkpoint Submission. They are not part of Notebook Evidence.

Source

The Harbor Arts Council plans a mobile exhibition about a century-old market hall. A student team argues that the hall survived because residents repeatedly adapted it: produce stalls became wartime supply tables, later served immigrant-owned shops, and now host weekend performances. The draft combines a 1924 floor plan, excerpts from merchants’ letters, census summaries, a short video interview, and present-day photographs.

Testing reveals problems. The opening video autoplays with music louder than the speaker. A map uses color alone to distinguish decades. One letter excerpt omits the writer’s uncertainty about whether a shop would remain open. A photograph credit names the archive but not the photographer or license. The conclusion claims that “every generation saved the hall,” although records show a ten-year period when the building was vacant and nearly demolished. On small screens, captions cover important parts of photographs, and the navigation labels “Past,” “Change,” and “Now” do not tell users what content each section contains.

The team pauses release. It restores the omitted qualification, revises the conclusion, creates descriptive headings, adds a transcript and player controls, redesigns the map with patterns and labels, verifies the image license, and tests the revised package with a merchant, a historian, and two students. The historian finds one date inconsistent with the floor plan; the merchant asks for translations of two quoted phrases. Both changes are documented before sign-off.

Mastery Questions

  1. State the central claim and intended audience.
  2. Identify the strongest evidence for adaptation over time and explain why.
  3. Explain how omitting the letter writer’s uncertainty damages source integrity.
  4. Revise the conclusion so it remains accurate and appropriately qualified.
  5. Analyze why the autoplay video weakens both usability and communication.
  6. Describe the specific accessibility repair required for the map.
  7. Write improved navigation headings and explain the sequence.
  8. List the complete credit and permission information needed for the photograph.
  9. Explain why the three testers provide useful but different usability evidence.
  10. Create a six-entry change log naming each problem, revision, and reason.
  11. Write a 160–220 word reflective rationale connecting audience, evidence, media, access, and ethics.
  12. Make and defend the final release decision with at least three verified findings.
