Lesson: Rhetoric That Serves Reasoning

Start With Function

Before choosing a device, name the needed function: clarify a relationship, create memorable emphasis, contrast alternatives, connect with shared values, or mark the urgency of the moment. The device must strengthen a supported idea.

Pattern and Emphasis

Anaphora: “We measured the water. We mapped the drains. We tested the soil.” Repetition emphasizes disciplined process.

Parallelism and tricolon: “The plan is practical, measurable, and reversible.” Balanced form makes three criteria easy to remember.

Delivery: repeat the opening phrase consistently, pause between units, and stress the final term.

Contrast and Clarification

Antithesis: “The question is not whether the bridge remembers the past, but whether the city will preserve its evidence.” The balanced contrast sharpens the decision.

Analogy: “A pilot program is a laboratory, not a lifetime contract.” The analogy clarifies limited testing, but it should not replace cost and outcome evidence.

Questions, Allusions, and Timing

A rhetorical question should focus attention: “If the archive disappears, what evidence will remain?” An allusion can activate shared knowledge, but explain unfamiliar references and avoid assuming one cultural background. Kairos connects the claim to a real decision or moment without inventing urgency.

Ethical Test

  1. Is the underlying claim supported?
  2. Does the device clarify rather than conceal a limitation?
  3. Could the language unfairly stereotype, frighten, or shame listeners?
  4. Does the speaker distinguish emotional illustration from factual evidence?
  5. Would the message remain defensible without the device?

Common Mistakes

  • Stacking devices until the speech sounds artificial.
  • Using a rhetorical question as a substitute for proof.
  • Comparing unlike situations through a false analogy.
  • Creating false urgency or certainty.
  • Repeating a slogan while ignoring source limitations.
