Worked Example: Editing a Public-Facing Research Article

Original Draft

A student article titled The Park Project Everyone Wanted claims that a vacant rail corridor “immediately united the neighborhood” when residents proposed a walking trail. Its opening buries the claim beneath dates. A survey is described as representing “the community,” although only 43 online responses were collected. Two paragraphs repeat benefits, objections are summarized dismissively, links say “here,” and the conclusion introduces an unsupported claim that the trail will reduce crime.

Step 1 — Structural Edit

The editor reorganizes the article into: present proposal and qualified claim; corridor history; evidence of support; evidence of concern; comparison of design responses; conclusion and next steps. The new title—How Residents Are Debating the Rail-Trail Proposal—matches the evidence and avoids false consensus.

Step 2 — Evidence and Fairness Edit

The sentence “Everyone supported the plan” becomes: “In a 43-response online survey shared by the neighborhood association, 31 respondents supported a trail, while meeting records documented concerns about lighting, parking, and displacement.” The article identifies the sample limitation and gives objections the same evidentiary treatment as support.

Step 3 — Line Edit

Before: “There were many residents who were saying that the project would be something that could benefit the area.”

After: “Several meeting speakers argued that the trail could improve access to parks and shops.”

The revision names the speakers and source context, uses a stronger verb, removes padding, and preserves the claim’s uncertainty.

Step 4 — Digital Publication Edit

Headings become “What the Survey Shows,” “Concerns in the Meeting Record,” and “How the Revised Design Responds.” Links identify destinations and formats. A comparison table receives column headings and a text summary. Paragraphs are shortened at logical boundaries, not reduced to disconnected fragments.

Step 5 — Final Quality and Ethics Audit

The editor removes the crime-reduction claim because no source supports it; verifies quotations against recordings; confirms image permission; checks names and private information; tests links and keyboard navigation; reviews mobile display; and records each substantive change. A source checker and accessibility reviewer sign off before release.

Final Release Rationale

The article is ready because its title and claim now match the evidence, opposing perspectives receive fair treatment, qualifications remain intact, prose is concise without losing complexity, and the digital format is navigable and accessible. The revision improves trustworthiness—not simply correctness at the sentence level.
