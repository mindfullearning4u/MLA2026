Lesson: Build an Argument Listeners Can Follow

1. Define the Speaking Situation

State the purpose, audience, time limit, and action or understanding you want listeners to leave with. Convert a broad topic into a controlling idea that can be defended in one presentation.

Too broad: “Public transportation.” Focused: “The city should extend the Route 8 evening schedule because employment and ridership records show unmet demand after 9 p.m.”

2. Build an Audible Structure

Use a simple architecture: opening and claim; two or three reasons; credible evidence and explanation for each reason; concession or limitation; conclusion. Signposts such as “First,” “The strongest evidence,” and “A reasonable concern” help listeners track the argument.

3. Evaluate and Integrate Evidence

  1. Check the source’s author, expertise, date, method, purpose, and corroboration.
  2. Select evidence that directly supports the reason, not merely the topic.
  3. Use an oral citation: “According to the city transportation department’s 2025 ridership report…”
  4. Explain the evidence immediately so listeners know why it matters.
  5. Qualify claims when evidence is limited or mixed.

Diverse Evidence Examples

Literary presentation: quote a repeated image, identify the work and author aloud, then explain how the image develops a theme.

Scientific presentation: summarize a study’s sample and result, but do not turn correlation into causation.

Historical presentation: distinguish a primary source’s firsthand perspective from a historian’s later interpretation.

4. Plan Delivery Without Writing a Script

Use brief cue cards with key phrases, oral citations, and delivery cues. Mark pauses before important evidence, slow down for complex numbers, and display only visuals that clarify the claim. Rehearse aloud and revise any sentence that is difficult to say or understand once.

Common Mistakes

  • Source dumping: naming facts without explaining their relevance.
  • Invisible structure: changing reasons without a signpost.
  • Credential inflation: calling a source an “expert” without relevant qualifications.
  • Overclaiming: presenting a limited survey as proof of what everyone believes.
  • Slide dependence: reading crowded text instead of communicating with listeners.
