Independent Work: “A Quiet Zone for the Harbor”

Directions

Study the complete speaking situation and source set. Answer all 18 questions in complete sentences. For passage-specific vocabulary, use a reputable online dictionary or encyclopedia when directed and record the source title. Then create a focused speaking plan rather than a full script.

Speaking Situation

The town council is considering a seasonal “quiet zone” that would limit amplified music and engine idling near Harbor Marsh between 6:00 a.m. and 9:00 a.m. from March through June. You will prepare a five-minute public-comment presentation recommending, opposing, or revising the proposal. Your audience includes residents, marina businesses, bird-watchers, and council members.

Source A — University Field Study

A coastal ecology team observed the marsh on 24 mornings during the previous spring. On mornings when recorded sound levels exceeded 70 decibels for more than twenty minutes, observers counted fewer feeding shorebirds in the two coves nearest the marina. Counts in the far cove changed little. The report calls the relationship “consistent with disturbance” but warns that weather, boat traffic, and food availability also affect bird location. The study was funded by the state university and publishes its observation schedule and raw counts.

Source B — Marina Association Survey

The marina association emailed a voluntary survey to 160 slip holders and received 63 responses. Forty-eight respondents opposed a restriction on engine idling, and 39 said early departures were important during fishing season. The survey did not ask how long engines usually idle or distinguish safety-related warming from convenience. The association’s summary states that “marina users overwhelmingly reject the quiet zone.”

Source C — Harbor Operations Memorandum

The harbor manager reports that rescue, patrol, ferry, and maintenance vessels would need exemptions. She recommends signs, a two-week education period, and sound measurements before issuing fines. Enforcement during the first season would require approximately 110 staff hours. The memorandum does not estimate whether reduced complaints or fewer wildlife disturbances would offset that cost.

Source D — Resident Testimony

Mateo Ruiz, who has lived beside the marsh for 17 years, says amplified music sometimes begins before sunrise, but he also worries that an inflexible rule could penalize workers preparing boats safely. He favors a pilot focused on prolonged idling and amplified sound, with exemptions and a public report after the nesting season. His testimony offers long experience, but it represents one resident’s perspective.

Part A — Focus, Meaning, and Passage Vocabulary

  1. Identify the decision the council must make and the competing interests involved.
  2. Write a defensible controlling idea for your five-minute presentation.
  3. Using context, define prolonged as used in Source D. Verify the definition with a reputable online dictionary or encyclopedia and record the source title.
  4. What does the phrase “consistent with disturbance” allow the researchers to claim, and what does it not prove?
  5. Identify the most important limitation in each of Sources A–D.

Part B — Credibility and Evidence

  1. Evaluate Source A’s credibility using author expertise, method, transparency, and limitations.
  2. Explain why Source B’s word “overwhelmingly” may overstate the survey results.
  3. Which evidence is most relevant to a claim about enforceability? Explain.
  4. Which source is best for acknowledging a reasonable opposing concern?
  5. Identify one claim that should not be made from this source set and explain why.

Part C — Speaking Plan

  1. Create a five-part outline: opening, controlling idea, two or three reasons, concession/response, and conclusion.
  2. Write three clear signposts that make your organization audible.
  3. Write two accurate oral citations that qualify the sources rather than inflate them.
  4. For two pieces of evidence, explain immediately how each supports your controlling idea.
  5. Plan one simple visual and explain why it clarifies rather than distracts.

Part D — Delivery and Integrity

  1. Create a 180–230 word speaking excerpt containing an opening, one evidence paragraph, an oral citation, and a concession.
  2. Add at least five delivery cues for pause, pace, emphasis, volume, or visual reference.
  3. Complete a source-integrity check: distinguish fact from inference, identify remaining verification needs, and explain how your language avoids overclaiming.
