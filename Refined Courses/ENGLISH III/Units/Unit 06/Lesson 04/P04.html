Worked Example: Redesigning “A River Divided”

The Draft and Communication Goal

A student team creates a six-screen digital presentation for residents and council members. Its claim is that a new floodwall protected downtown businesses but also reduced river access for the Eastbank neighborhood. The draft contains a title screen, an unlabeled historical map, two dense quotation slides, a chart with five similar colors, an autoplay video, and a final screen saying “Learn More.”

Step 1 — Diagnose the Audience Experience

Audience task: understand the tradeoff and evaluate two proposed access improvements.

Observed problems: viewers cannot orient themselves on the map; long quotations obscure the claim; the chart is unreadable; autoplay interrupts reading; and “Learn More” does not identify the destination.

Priority: repair comprehension before adding any new media.

Step 2 — Rebuild the Reasoning Path

  1. Open with a present-day photograph and a one-sentence qualified claim.
  2. Use a labeled before/after map to establish geography.
  3. Present flood-loss data in a high-contrast chart.
  4. Pair one resident quotation with a photograph of the closed access point.
  5. Compare the two proposals with the same criteria: cost, access, safety, and maintenance.
  6. End with a clearly labeled link to the public proposal documents.

This order moves from context to evidence to consequence to decision.

Step 3 — Make Element-Level Decisions

Map: add street names, orientation, a legend, and alt text explaining that access points fell from four to one.

Chart: replace similar colors with two high-contrast patterns, direct labels, and a text summary.

Quotation: reduce it to the sentence that directly supports the access claim; place full transcript context in the source note.

Video: disable autoplay, add captions and transcript, show duration, and place it after the related claim.

Step 4 — Test, Interpret, and Revise

Three users are asked to locate the historical access point, state the tradeoff, and compare the proposals. Two users misread the chart because its title names “losses” without units. The team changes the title to “Annual Flood-Damage Claims, 2012–2024,” adds dollar units, and rewrites the narration. A keyboard user cannot reach the comparison tabs, so the team replaces them with an accessible table.

Step 5 — Final Justification

The redesign is stronger because visual hierarchy, media, navigation, and accessibility now reinforce the same reasoning path. The team did not merely make the presentation more attractive; it made the evidence easier to interpret, the tradeoff harder to oversimplify, and the decision options easier to compare.
