Lesson: Designing a Digital Presentation for Audience Experience

A Five-Layer Design Process

  1. Define the audience task: decide what the audience must understand, compare, feel, or do.
  2. Build the reasoning path: sequence claim, context, evidence, explanation, qualification, and conclusion.
  3. Create visual hierarchy: make the most important idea easiest to locate without overwhelming the screen.
  4. Design interaction deliberately: every link, control, reveal, or transition must help the audience navigate or think.
  5. Test the experience: observe users, record confusion and friction, revise, and retest.

Example 1 — Literary Argument Slides

Weak: A slide about isolation in a novel contains a paragraph, three decorative photographs, and a quotation in small type.

Stronger: The slide states one claim, displays a short quotation beside a labeled image of the setting, and uses speaker narration to explain the relationship. The audience can see the evidence while listening to the reasoning.

Why: The redesign reduces split attention and makes the evidence-to-claim connection visible.

Example 2 — Civic Data Story

A student argues that bus access affects after-school participation. A map shows route gaps; a bar chart compares attendance by neighborhood; a short quotation adds lived experience. The sequence moves from pattern, to consequence, to human perspective. Alt text conveys the chart’s conclusion, not merely its colors.

Example 3 — Museum Timeline

An interactive timeline initially hides all source information behind hover effects. Keyboard users cannot reach it, and mobile users miss the dates. The revision uses descriptive headings, visible dates, expandable source notes, and a complete linear text alternative.

Common Mistakes and Repairs

  • Decoration replaces evidence: remove or reassign every element without an analytical function.
  • Everything competes: establish one focal idea per screen and use consistent hierarchy.
  • Navigation is vague: replace “Next” or “More” with descriptive labels when users must choose.
  • Animation controls attention: use motion only to reveal a meaningful sequence and provide pause controls.
  • Testing asks for opinions: give users tasks and observe behavior instead of asking only whether they “like” the design.
