Direct Teaching: Precise Academic Language Across Modes

1. Match Language to Audience and Purpose

Experts may understand juxtaposition without definition; a community audience may need a brief explanation. Adapt support, not the accuracy of the term.

2. Use Terms as Analytical Tools

Do not display vocabulary. Use it to name a relationship and explain an effect: “The speaker’s concession acknowledges cost, which strengthens credibility before the proposal.”

3. Signpost Spoken Analysis

Listeners cannot reread. Use phrases such as “My central claim is…,” “The strongest evidence is…,” and “This matters because….” Signposting should clarify structure without becoming repetitive.

4. Qualify and Emphasize

Qualifiers such as primarily, in this scene, or suggests keep claims accurate. Emphasis should highlight the reasoning, not turn a qualified claim into an absolute one.

5. Translate Without Diluting

Written: The juxtaposition exposes incompatible definitions of progress. Spoken adaptation: By placing the new tower beside the damaged homes, the speaker creates a sharp contrast—what literary analysis calls juxtaposition—and asks whose version of progress counts.

Common Mistakes

  • Using inflated words that do not fit the meaning.
  • Assuming listeners know every specialized term.
  • Replacing analysis with a string of definitions.
  • Speaking in long written-style sentences with no signposts.
  • Removing all qualification for dramatic effect.
