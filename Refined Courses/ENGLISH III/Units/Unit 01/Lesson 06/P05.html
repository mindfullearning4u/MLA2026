Independent Work: “Three Minutes for the River”

Directions

Read the source notes and speech draft, then answer all 18 questions. Submit responses with Page 2 vocabulary as Notebook Evidence.

Source Notes and Draft

Source notes: A river survey found that summer water temperature rose four degrees near unshaded banks. Native tree roots reduced erosion at three monitored sites. The city can plant only two miles this year.

Draft: Everybody knows the river is basically dying, and the city must obviously fix all of it now. Riparian restoration demonstrates ecological resiliency through vegetative infrastructure. First, trees are good. Second, erosion is bad. Finally, we should act. The survey proves planting trees will solve every river problem.

Audience: a mixed public meeting of residents, students, business owners, and city staff. Purpose: recommend a realistic first-year planting priority.

Audience, Vocabulary, and Accuracy

  1. Identify two places where the register does not fit the audience.
  2. Replace basically dying with precise language supported by the notes.
  3. Explain how to define riparian briefly without removing the term.
  4. Identify two unsupported absolute claims.
  5. Write qualified replacements.
  6. Choose two discipline-specific terms that genuinely improve precision.

Organization and Delivery

  1. Write a clear signpost stating the central claim.
  2. Adapt the temperature evidence for listeners.
  3. Adapt the erosion evidence and explain its significance.
  4. Use a transition showing cause and effect.
  5. Use emphasis to identify the most practical first step.
  6. Explain where elaboration is necessary for this audience.

Complete Revision

  1. Write a precise, qualified central claim.
  2. Produce a 150–200 word revised speech.
  3. Mark three signposts or transitions.
  4. Explain how the revision adapts written research for listeners.
  5. Explain how it preserves the evidence’s limits.
  6. Identify the strongest use of academic vocabulary and defend the choice.
