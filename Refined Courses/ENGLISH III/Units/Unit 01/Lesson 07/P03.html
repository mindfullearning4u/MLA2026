Direct Teaching: The Integrated Analysis Process

1. Read for Meaning and Tension

Identify the central idea, repeated patterns, shifts, and details that complicate a simple reading. Strong interpretation accounts for both supporting evidence and counterevidence.

2. Form and Qualify an Interpretation

State what the text suggests and why. Limit the claim to the relevant scene, speaker, or pattern. A qualified claim is stronger because it matches the evidence’s actual scope.

3. Build an Evidence Set

  1. Select the strongest supporting detail.
  2. Add a second detail that corroborates or develops it.
  3. Identify counterevidence or ambiguity.
  4. Decide whether to revise, narrow, or defend the claim.

4. Paraphrase and Integrate

Preserve context and qualifications, change wording and syntax, attribute the evidence, and place it in a sentence that advances the claim.

5. Explain and Synthesize

Reasoning must explain how evidence creates meaning. Synthesis combines details into an insight that none provides alone. End with significance, not repetition.

Academic Voice and Editing

  • Use precise analytical vocabulary.
  • Prefer qualified claims over absolutes.
  • Maintain coherent transitions and correct sentence boundaries.
  • Eliminate vague pronouns, inflated diction, and unsupported certainty.

Common Mistakes

  • Ignoring evidence that complicates the interpretation.
  • Listing two details without synthesizing them.
  • Using patchwriting or quotation drops.
  • Confusing objective summary with analysis.
  • Editing grammar in a way that changes meaning.
