The Ledger and the Lantern

On the first cold evening of October, Eli climbed the narrow stairs above his aunt’s hardware store to inventory boxes left by the previous owner. The town historical society planned an exhibit about the 1978 ice storm, and everyone repeated the same story: Mayor Vale had kept the town alive by opening the municipal warehouse and distributing fuel.

In a dented cash box, Eli found delivery receipts bearing the names of six neighborhood shopkeepers. Each receipt recorded kerosene, blankets, or canned food sent to the warehouse before the mayor’s announcement. A clothbound ledger listed families who received supplies. Beside several entries appeared the initials M.R.—the initials of Mara Ruiz, a laundromat owner whom the official exhibit described only as “a volunteer.”

Eli hurried downstairs, ready to declare that the familiar story was false. His aunt read the receipts twice. “They prove the shopkeepers supplied goods,” she said. “They do not prove the mayor did nothing.” She pointed to a council memo authorizing emergency access to the warehouse and suspending payment rules. “History can hold more than one useful action.”

Over the next week, Eli compared newspaper reports, council minutes, and three recorded interviews. Mara’s daughter remembered her mother organizing deliveries by lantern light. A former clerk recalled the mayor persuading the council to release the warehouse. One newspaper praised Vale without naming the shopkeepers; another listed donations but confused two dates.

At the exhibit meeting, Eli proposed replacing the single heroic portrait with a wall-sized timeline. His captions attributed each claim and distinguished written records from later recollections. The final display showed Vale signing the emergency order, shopkeepers supplying goods, Mara organizing distribution, and residents carrying parcels through sleet. In the center stood the old lantern. Its light belonged to no single portrait.

Part A — Evidence Selection

  1. State the story’s central idea.
  2. What claim does the town’s familiar version make?
  3. Identify two details that complicate—but do not entirely disprove—that version.
  4. Why are the receipts relevant to Eli’s revised interpretation?
  5. Why is the council memo relevant?
  6. Which detail would be least relevant to a claim about shared leadership? Explain.

  5. Paraphrase the aunt’s statement “History can hold more than one useful action” in context.
  6. Identify and correct any patchwriting in your draft.

Part C — Integration and Reasoning

  4. Write a three- to four-sentence objective summary.
  5. Explain the lantern’s symbolism using two relevant details.
  6. Choose the strongest evidence that responsible history requires attribution and qualification. Defend your choice.
