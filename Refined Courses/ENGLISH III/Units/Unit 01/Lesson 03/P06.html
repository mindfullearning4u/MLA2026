The Orchard Survey

The school board considered selling a neglected orchard beside Westfield High. A consultant’s report called the property “nonproductive land” because annual fruit sales did not cover maintenance. Student researcher Amara noticed that the calculation included pruning and irrigation costs but counted only money earned at a weekend market.

Amara reviewed five years of harvest logs. Roughly one third of the fruit had gone to the cafeteria, another portion to a food pantry, and several hundred pounds to biology classes studying pollination and soil health. She also found that two diseased rows required removal. The records therefore supported neither the claim that the orchard produced nothing nor the claim that every tree should be preserved.

At the hearing, Amara summarized the evidence carefully. She explained that the financial report measured direct sales accurately but used too narrow a definition of productivity. She proposed removing the diseased rows, building needed classrooms on that section, and retaining the healthy trees for food and instruction. The board postponed the sale and requested a cost analysis of her mixed-use plan.

Evidence and Paraphrase

  1. State Amara’s central claim.
  2. Which evidence most directly challenges “nonproductive land”?
  3. Which evidence qualifies a claim for complete preservation?
  4. Explain why the consultant’s sales calculation is accurate but incomplete.
  5. Paraphrase the second paragraph accurately.
  6. Name two essential details your paraphrase preserves.
  7. Explain how you changed wording and structure.
  8. Identify and correct any phrase that remains too close to the source.

Integration and Synthesis

  1. Integrate one relevant paraphrased detail into a claim about the orchard’s educational value.
  2. Explain the reasoning that connects your evidence to that claim.
  3. Write a three-sentence objective summary.
  4. Why is Amara’s mixed-use proposal better supported than either extreme position? Use evidence.
