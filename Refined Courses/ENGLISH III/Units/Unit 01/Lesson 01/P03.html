Direct Teaching: Close Reading and Accurate Paraphrasing

What Close Reading Requires

Close reading is purposeful rereading. On the first read, establish the situation and gist. On later reads, examine patterns, shifts, word choices, relationships, and details that develop the central idea. A strong reader separates what the text explicitly states from what the evidence reasonably implies.

Step-by-Step Close-Reading Process

  1. Preview: identify the genre, title, speaker or narrator, and relevant context.
  2. Read for the gist: state what happens or what the passage explains in one sentence.
  3. Reread and annotate: mark repeated ideas, contrasts, shifts, unfamiliar words, and striking details.
  4. State the central idea: explain the most important idea the whole passage develops.
  5. Select essential details: choose evidence that directly develops the central idea.
  6. Make an inference: combine a textual clue with logical reasoning.
  7. Explain the evidence: show how the chosen detail supports the inference or central idea.
  8. Check accuracy: remove any claim the text does not support.

How to Paraphrase Accurately

  1. Read the complete source until you understand its meaning.
  2. Identify the central idea and details that must remain.
  3. Look away from the source and explain the meaning in simple language.
  4. Change both the wording and the sentence structure.
  5. Compare your version with the source.
  6. Correct copied phrases, missing ideas, exaggerations, or changed relationships.
  7. Cite the source when the paraphrase is used in academic writing.

Source: Because the storm had loosened stones above the trail, rangers closed the path until engineers could examine the slope.

Accurate paraphrase: Rangers temporarily blocked access to the route after severe weather made the rocky hillside potentially unsafe and required an engineering inspection.

Literary Inference Example

Text: Elena placed the unopened letter beneath the loose floorboard, but each evening she checked that it remained there.

Inference: Elena fears what the letter may reveal, yet she cannot dismiss its importance.

Evidence explanation: Hiding the letter shows avoidance, while checking it repeatedly shows that it continues to occupy her thoughts.

Common Mistakes

  • Patchwriting: replacing a few words while preserving the source sentence.
  • Oversimplifying: omitting a condition, contrast, or essential detail.
  • Adding interpretation: inserting an opinion the source never states.
  • Confusing summary and paraphrase: a summary condenses a larger text; a paraphrase restates a particular passage at roughly similar detail.
  • Quoting without explanation: evidence is not analysis until the writer explains its significance.

Accuracy Check

Ask four questions: Does my version preserve the original central idea? Does it retain every essential relationship? Is the language and structure genuinely new? Have I avoided adding unsupported meaning?
