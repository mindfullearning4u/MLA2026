Direct Teaching: Reading and Writing with Academic Vocabulary

1. Build Meaning from Word Parts

Break an unfamiliar term into meaningful parts. In juxtaposition, the root juxta means “next to,” and position refers to placement. The term therefore suggests placing elements beside one another. Confirm the predicted meaning with the sentence and surrounding paragraph.

2. Test Meaning in Context

  1. Read the entire sentence and identify the grammatical role.
  2. Notice definitions, examples, contrasts, causes, or restatements nearby.
  3. Substitute a possible meaning and reread.
  4. Check whether that meaning fits the passage’s tone and logic.

3. Separate Denotation from Connotation

Childlike and childish both relate to children, but their associations differ. Childlike curiosity suggests openness; childish refusal suggests immaturity. When analyzing diction, name the precise association and explain its effect.

4. Choose the Exact Analytical Term

A repeated lighthouse image is a motif, not automatically a theme. Two opposing images placed together create juxtaposition. A speaker who admits one reasonable opposing point makes a concession. Exact labels must be followed by an explanation of how the technique develops meaning.

5. Move from Identification to Analysis

Identification: The writer uses a rhetorical question.

Analysis: The question “Who benefits when silence is mistaken for agreement?” challenges the audience to question passive acceptance and sharpens the speech’s urgent tone.

6. Control Register and Qualifiers

Academic claims avoid casual wording and unsupported certainty. Replace “The author totally proves…” with “The passage suggests…” or “The repeated image primarily emphasizes….” Qualifiers make a claim appropriately precise; they do not weaken strong evidence.

Common Mistakes

  • Definition dumping: naming a term without explaining its function.
  • Thesaurus substitution: choosing a formal-looking word that changes the meaning.
  • Overclaiming: using always, proves, or everyone when evidence supports a narrower claim.
  • Confusing topic and theme: “memory” is a topic; a complete insight about memory is a theme.
