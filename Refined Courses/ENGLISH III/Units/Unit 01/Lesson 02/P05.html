Independent Work: “Inventory of Small Noises”

Directions

Read the prose-poem and answer all 18 questions in complete sentences. Submit these responses with Page 2 vocabulary as Notebook Evidence.

Inventory of Small Noises

Before sunrise, the apartment building speaks in minor keys: a radiator ticking behind plaster, a key testing the front lock, water worrying the pipes. On the third floor, Mrs. Alvarez drags a chair toward the window and listens for the bakery truck. Across the hall, Dev practices the same four measures on his violin, stopping before the note that always escapes him.

The city calls the building obsolete. A notice in the lobby describes “underused residential capacity” and “future commercial potential.” The phrases are polished stones; they reveal no fingerprints. They do not mention the chalk marks beside apartment 4B, where three generations measured their height, or the brass mail slot that still bears the name of a tenant who translated letters for half the block.

At noon, a developer tours the hallway. “Imagine the quiet,” he says, describing sealed windows and private offices. Mrs. Alvarez almost laughs. Quiet is the one thing the building has never promised. Its noises are not defects but evidence: spoons against cups, arguments softened by closing doors, music repeating until difficulty becomes memory.

That evening, Dev reaches the missing note. It is not beautiful. It arrives thin and startled, then steadies. Doors open along the corridor. No one applauds. For several seconds, neighbors simply listen, as if the building has asked a question whose answer must be made together.

Part A — Vocabulary in Context

  1. What does minor keys suggest about the building’s early-morning mood?
  2. Explain the connotation of “water worrying the pipes.”
  3. Determine the meaning of obsolete from context and explain which details complicate that label.
  4. Why does the speaker call the notice’s phrases “polished stones”?
  5. Compare the registers of the official notice and the residents’ remembered details.

Part B — Literary and Rhetorical Analysis

  1. Identify the central idea.
  2. Explain how sound functions as a motif.
  3. Analyze the juxtaposition between “future commercial potential” and the chalk marks/mail slot.
  4. What does the developer mean by quiet, and how does the passage challenge that meaning?
  5. Analyze the rhetorical effect of the sentence “They reveal no fingerprints.”
  6. How does Dev’s repeated practice contribute to the central idea?
  7. What nuance does startled add to the description of the missing note?

Part C — Academic Expression

  1. Write a qualified analytical claim about the official notice’s diction.
  2. Use connotation accurately in an evidence-based sentence.
  3. Use juxtaposition accurately to explain one contrast.
  4. Revise “The developer is totally wrong because the building is special” into academic register.
  5. Write a three- to four-sentence objective summary.
  6. Explain the final sentence, using at least two lesson vocabulary terms accurately.
