Lesson: Choosing Words with Evidence

The Precision Protocol

  1. State the intended meaning: identify exactly what the sentence must communicate.
  2. Generate candidates: use memory or a thesaurus, treating results as possibilities.
  3. Check full entries: compare senses, usage labels, grammar, connotation, and examples.
  4. Test collocation: consult authentic examples or a corpus when conventional combinations matter.
  5. Check the field: use a disciplinary glossary for specialized claims.
  6. Return to the sentence: choose the word whose meaning, tone, and syntax best fit.

Example 1 — “Claim,” “Argue,” and “Assert”

Claim can name a position without proving it. Argue emphasizes reasoning and evidence. Assert emphasizes confident declaration and can imply insufficient support. The most precise verb depends on what the writer actually does.

Example 2 — “Historic” and “Historical”

Historical means related to history. Historic usually means important in history. A dictionary usage note and authentic examples prevent the common substitution error.

Example 3 — Intensity Scale

Concerned, alarmed, terrified occupy different points on an intensity scale. A character who quietly checks a locked door may be concerned or uneasy; calling the character terrified would overstate the evidence.

Example 4 — Disciplinary Meaning

Theory can mean an unconfirmed idea in everyday speech, but in science it denotes a well-supported explanatory framework. A disciplinary reference is essential when the field-specific sense controls the claim.

Common Mistakes

  • Selecting the first thesaurus result without checking context.
  • Ignoring usage labels or grammatical patterns.
  • Assuming synonyms are interchangeable.
  • Using a general dictionary when a specialized glossary is required.
  • Copying an AI-suggested word without verifying its sense and collocations.
