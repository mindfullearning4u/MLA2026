Independent Work: “The Committee’s Quiet Room”

Directions

Read the complete passage and answer all 18 questions. Build tone claims from patterns of diction, connotation, register, syntax, imagery, and shifts. For the passage-specific vocabulary item, infer meaning first, verify it with a reputable online dictionary or encyclopedia, and record the source.

The Committee’s Quiet Room

The council called the basement chamber a listening room. Its door was padded, its carpet absorbed footsteps, and a small lamp replaced the fluorescent tubes used elsewhere in city hall. Residents entered one at a time to record comments about the proposed highway.

The official brochure praised the room as “a calm environment for uninterrupted public participation.” The phrase sounded generous. No council member would stand across the table, no clock would limit a speaker, and no audience would laugh or applaud. A red light indicated that the recorder was working.

Mara Voss visited on a Tuesday morning. Her family’s grocery stood inside the proposed construction zone. She had prepared a five-minute statement, but the room contained no visible timer. She spoke for twelve minutes about deliveries, neighbors, and the apartment above the store where her parents had lived. When she finished, the red light remained on. She waited for a voice to thank her. Ventilation hummed behind the wall.

At the next council meeting, the planning director summarized 312 recordings as “substantial community engagement.” His presentation contained a chart dividing comments into support, concern, and nonresponsive material. Mara’s statement appeared under concern. The twelve minutes had become one blue segment in a circle.

The director explained that the listening room had increased access. Evening workers could record during lunch; residents who feared public speaking could participate privately. Both claims were true. He did not mention that recordings longer than three minutes were summarized by software before staff review. The brochure had promised uninterrupted participation, not uninterrupted listening.

Mara requested her transcript. Several passages were marked “[personal history omitted].” The label was not hostile. It was clean, procedural, and almost tender in its refusal to argue. Her parents’ apartment, the winter the store gave food on credit, and the names of elderly customers disappeared beneath the same neutral phrase.

She returned to the basement with a folding chair. Instead of recording another statement, she sat outside the padded door and played her original audio from a portable speaker. People slowed in the hallway. A custodian stopped his cart. Two clerks emerged from an office. The listening room continued absorbing sound while the corridor carried it upward.

Security asked Mara to lower the volume. She did. The request was courteous, and her compliance was immediate. Yet when the council meeting began upstairs, several members had already heard the story that the chart called a concern.

A week later, the brochure was revised. “Uninterrupted participation” became “flexible comment collection.” The new wording was more accurate and less noble. Mara taped both versions in the grocery window beneath a handwritten label: Before listening. After listening.

Part A — Denotation and Connotation

  1. Compare the denotation and connotation of listening room.
  2. What associations are created by “calm,” “uninterrupted,” and “participation” in the brochure?
  3. Analyze the connotative difference between statement, comment, and concern.
  4. Explain why “[personal history omitted]” feels “almost tender” despite its effect.
  5. Compare “uninterrupted participation” with “flexible comment collection.”

Part B — Tone and Register

  1. Describe the opening tone using two precise adjectives and evidence.
  2. How does the bureaucratic register of the planning director shape the passage’s attitude?
  3. Identify the tonal shift when Mara plays the recording in the corridor.
  4. Analyze how the repeated courteous language contributes to controlled criticism rather than open anger.
  5. Explain the effect of the final handwritten label.

Part C — Vocabulary and Pattern

  1. Use context to define procedural. Verify the definition with a reputable online dictionary or encyclopedia and name the source.
  2. Identify a semantic field related to silence or containment and list at least four examples.
  3. Explain how the red light changes from a sign of access to a sign of unanswered speech.
  4. Analyze the contrast between the room absorbing sound and the corridor carrying it upward.
  5. Does the narrator accuse the council of deliberate deception? Distinguish intention from textual effect.

Part D — Synthesis

  1. State the central idea in one precise sentence.
  2. Write a well-developed paragraph explaining how diction, register, and tonal shifts develop that idea.
  3. Evaluate the revised brochure. Is less “noble” language more ethical in this context? Support your judgment.
