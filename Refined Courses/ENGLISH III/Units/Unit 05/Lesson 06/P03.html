Lesson: Rebuild the Argument, Not Just the Paragraphs

The Structural Revision Protocol

  1. Reverse-outline: state the claim and function of every paragraph as written.
  2. Test the line: ask what each paragraph makes possible for the next.
  3. Find gaps and duplication: mark missing warrants, repeated summary, and evidence without purpose.
  4. Choose a governing pattern: cause/effect, problem/response, comparison, chronological development, or claim/counterclaim.
  5. Reorder and rebuild: move, combine, split, or delete paragraphs.
  6. Add relational transitions: name contrast, extension, cause, qualification, or consequence.
  7. Recheck opening and conclusion: ensure they match the revised reasoning.

Example 1 — Evidence Order

A draft analyzes an ending before explaining the symbol introduced earlier. Reordering by the symbol’s development allows evidence to accumulate and makes the final transformation meaningful.

Example 2 — Counterargument Placement

A counterinterpretation placed before the main claim confuses purpose. Placed after initial evidence, it tests the emerging interpretation and creates a pivot toward a qualified conclusion.

Example 3 — Transition Diagnosis

Adding Furthermore cannot fix paragraphs that actually contradict one another. The writer must identify the conflict, decide whether to resolve or preserve it, and use a transition such as Yet this evidence complicates...

Example 4 — Conclusion

A weak conclusion repeats the thesis. A synthesis conclusion explains what the analysis changes: for example, a story’s preservation theme may imply that accuracy requires visible gaps rather than seamless reconstruction.

Common Mistakes

  • Reordering paragraphs without changing transitions or claims.
  • Using chronology when the argument requires comparison.
  • Giving every paragraph multiple unrelated jobs.
  • Saving all qualification for the conclusion.
  • Treating AI-generated outlines as authoritative rather than testing them against evidence and task.
