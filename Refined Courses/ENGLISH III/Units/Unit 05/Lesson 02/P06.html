Naming the Storm

The coastal archive contained five names for the storm of 1889. Shipping records called it a cyclone. A minister’s diary called it a visitation. Newspapers preferred catastrophe, while fishermen interviewed decades later called it the turning tide. In family letters, it was simply the September weather.

Researcher Lena Moss initially treated the terms as interchangeable references to one event. Then she arranged them by date, speaker, and purpose. Cyclone classified a weather system. Visitation framed the storm as an event with moral or spiritual meaning. Catastrophe emphasized destruction and attracted readers. Turning tide described not only water but the economic changes that followed.

The word catastrophe interested Lena because its earlier dramatic use referred to an overturning or final event in a plot. The newspaper writers may not have known that history, but the term still gave the storm narrative shape: life before, violent reversal, life after.

In her article, Lena did not choose the “best” name. She argued that the vocabulary formed a map of perspectives. The storm was a meteorological event, a spiritual interpretation, a public disaster, an economic boundary, and ordinary weather endured inside private life. Synonyms had not merely repeated the same meaning. They had organized attention.
