Worked Example: Tracing “Discriminate” Across a Word Family

Model Passage

The curator discriminated between repairs that restored the manuscript and alterations that merely made it look new. Her criteria were not immediately visible to visitors, who initially described the decisions as arbitrary. After the exhibit displayed ultraviolet images and conservation notes, the distinctions became intelligible.

Step 1 — Contextual Meaning

In this passage, discriminated means recognized or made a careful distinction. The object introduced by between and the later reference to “distinctions” support this meaning.

Step 2 — Morphology and History

The word descends through Latin forms associated with separating or distinguishing. A reputable etymological dictionary confirms the relationship to discern and a root meaning to separate. The historical information fits the curator’s act of classification.

Step 3 — Word-Family Comparison

  • discriminate (verb): distinguish; in other contexts, treat unjustly based on group identity.
  • discrimination (noun): careful perception or unjust differential treatment, depending on context.
  • discriminating (adjective): showing refined judgment.
  • indiscriminate (adjective): done without careful distinction.

Step 4 — Semantic Development

The broader “distinguish” meaning remains active, while the social and legal meaning of unjust treatment has become highly prominent. A reader must use syntax and context rather than assuming only one sense.

Step 5 — Analytical Application

The curator’s discrimination is presented as disciplined rather than prejudicial because she uses evidence-based criteria, contrasts restoration with cosmetic alteration, and later makes those criteria visible. The word’s multiple senses create a useful moment of possible ambiguity that the passage resolves.

Alternative Reading and Accuracy Check

A skeptical reader might initially hear a negative connotation because the criteria are hidden. The exhibit’s evidence shifts that interpretation. This shows why connotation can guide a question but cannot replace syntactic and textual analysis.

  • Was the word divided into documented morphemes?
  • Was the etymology verified?
  • Does the analysis prioritize current context?
  • Are word-family members distinguished accurately?
