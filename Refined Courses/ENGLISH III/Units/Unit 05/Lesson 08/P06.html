The Lantern Map

When the shoreline path reopened after the storm, volunteers found dozens of small metal tags nailed to the old railing. Each tag carried a number, but no public map explained it. Archivist Rosa Chen located a maintenance ledger from 1967 showing that lighthouse keepers had used the numbers to report damaged lamps along the harbor. The ledger was practical, repetitive, and incomplete: several pages had been removed, and some entries recorded repairs without identifying who completed them.

A student design team proposed turning the tags into an interactive history walk. Their first draft promised that visitors would “discover the complete story of every harbor light.” Chen asked them to revise the claim because the surviving evidence could not support completeness. The team instead built a map that paired verified ledger entries with oral-history excerpts. When accounts disagreed, the display identified the difference rather than selecting the most dramatic version. The designers also provided printed transcripts and high-contrast route markers so the project would not depend on a smartphone or perfect vision.

At the final review, one student wrote: “There is a way in which the missing pages are an indication of the fact that history has gaps.” The idea was sound, but the sentence buried it. The team revised it to: “The missing pages reveal the gaps in the harbor’s record.” That concise statement became the project’s organizing principle. The map invited visitors to follow evidence, notice uncertainty, and understand that responsible publication sometimes makes limits more visible rather than hiding them.
