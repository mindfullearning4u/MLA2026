Lesson: An Integrated Editorial Workflow

1. Read for Meaning Before Editing

State the writer’s claim, purpose, audience, and essential evidence. Do not polish a sentence whose idea is inaccurate or misplaced. Ask: What must remain true after revision?

2. Revise the Structure

Test the order of ideas, paragraph focus, transitions, and balance of evidence and explanation. Move or combine material when the reader would otherwise have to reconstruct the reasoning.

Example: In an argument about a public mural, place the permit evidence before the claim that the approval process was transparent.

3. Refine Style and Precision

Replace vague nouns and inflated phrases with exact language; vary sentence patterns; preserve purposeful repetition and the writer’s individual voice.

Literary example: Retain a repeated image of “thresholds” when it develops a theme. Informational example: Replace “made an adjustment to” with “adjusted.”

4. Edit Conventions and Consistency

Check grammar, punctuation, usage, parallel structure, verb tense, capitalization, numbers, citations, and style-sheet decisions. Read aloud to catch missing words and awkward rhythm.

5. Verify Integrity and Publication Readiness

Confirm quotations against the source, distinguish fact from inference, credit borrowed language, verify passage-specific vocabulary with a reputable dictionary or encyclopedia, and inspect headings, links, alternative text, contrast, and readable formatting.

AI use: AI may suggest alternatives, but the student must verify accuracy, preserve the writer’s meaning, and explain the final choice.

Common Mistakes

  • Editing only grammar: a correct sentence can still support a weak or misplaced idea.
  • Flattening voice: replacing every unusual choice may erase deliberate style.
  • Trusting a confident suggestion: verify definitions, quotations, and factual claims.
  • Proofreading too early: sentence-level polishing is wasted when paragraphs still need restructuring.
