P03 — Examples and Guided Practice

Use scenario details as evidence; do not make assumptions.

A Reliable Analysis Process

  1. Underline specific scenario details.
  2. Connect each detail to one or more wellness dimensions.
  3. Explain how two dimensions may influence one another.
  4. Identify a protective factor and a risk factor.
  5. Recommend one realistic action supported by the evidence.
  6. Use cautious language when an outcome is possible rather than certain.

Modeled Example 1: Several Nights of Poor Sleep

Scenario: Jordan has slept about five hours on several school nights. Jordan reports low energy, has difficulty concentrating during an online lesson, becomes impatient during a group chat, and postpones an assignment.

How the conclusion is derived

  1. Extract evidence before naming dimensions. The usable details are “five hours,” “low energy,” “difficulty concentrating,” “impatient,” and “postpones an assignment.”
  2. Classify each detail. Sleep and energy connect to physical health; concentration and completing a task connect to mental health; impatience connects to emotional regulation and may affect social communication.
  3. Look for a sequence. The details occur after several short nights. This supports a possible chain: reduced sleep → low energy and weaker focus → frustration or impatience → communication and task difficulties.
  4. Limit the claim. The scenario does not prove a diagnosis or show that sleep is the only cause. Therefore, use may rather than caused.
  5. Choose an action that addresses the evidence. A consistent wind-down routine responds directly to the repeated short sleep. Seeking support is appropriate if the difficulty continues because some barriers may require resources beyond a routine change.

Defensible conclusion: Repeated insufficient sleep is a risk factor that may affect Jordan's physical energy and mental concentration. Those difficulties could also affect emotional regulation, social interaction, and decision-making.

Modeled Example 2: Walking with a Friend

Scenario: Priya and a friend begin taking a comfortable 20-minute walk after school three days each week. Priya enjoys the conversation, reports that the routine helps create a break between schoolwork and evening tasks, and prepares water before leaving.

How the conclusion is derived

  1. Physical evidence: Walking is movement, and preparing water is a health-supporting preparation behavior.
  2. Social evidence: Priya walks with a friend and enjoys the conversation. That directly supports classifying the relationship as a social factor.
  3. Mental/emotional evidence: Priya says the routine creates a break between sets of tasks. The scenario supports saying the break may help with transition or stress management; it does not prove a universal emotional outcome.
  4. Interaction: The friend may make the routine more enjoyable or consistent. In return, the routine creates regular time for both movement and connection. One protective factor can therefore support several dimensions.
  5. Conclusion check: Every claim points back to a stated detail. Nothing in the analysis depends on appearance, athletic ability, or an assumed diagnosis.

Defensible conclusion: Priya's planned walking routine combines physical activity, hydration preparation, social support, and a structured transition between tasks. These protective factors may reinforce one another, although individual outcomes can differ.

Guided Practice

Write these responses in your notebook under “P03 Guided Practice.” For each scenario, record the dimension, exact evidence, interaction, protective factor, and health-supporting action. Use the reasoning guidance, but complete the final sentences yourself.

Scenario A — Study Space

Mateo usually studies beside a loud television and is often interrupted. A family member helps him identify a quieter corner, and Mateo begins placing needed materials there before class. He reports that it is easier to follow directions and finish work.

Derivation: “Loud television” and “quieter corner” describe surroundings, so they are environmental evidence. “Easier to follow directions and finish work” describes attention and task completion, so it is mental evidence. The before-and-after details support the interaction.

Complete: The quieter space may support Mateo's mental health because _____. The family member and prepared materials are protective factors because _____.

Scenario B — Online Comparison

Avery spends long periods viewing edited fitness posts and begins believing that appearance proves health. Avery later reads a school resource explaining that wellness includes five dimensions and decides to limit that account while following evidence-based sources.

Derivation: The posts are a media influence. The belief about appearance is a misconception, and the school resource supplies more reliable evidence. Limiting the account reduces exposure to the misleading influence; following evidence-based sources adds a protective resource.

Complete: Avery's decision is health-supporting because _____. It may affect the mental and emotional dimensions by _____.

Scenario C — New School Routine

Sam recently changed schools. Sam misses familiar friends, has not joined any activities, and feels distracted during class. A counselor provides information about a student interest group and a quiet lunchtime space.

Derivation: Missing friends is social evidence; feelings connected with the change may involve emotional health; distraction is mental evidence; the quiet space is an environmental resource. The counselor and interest group are possible protective factors because they provide access to support and connection.

Complete: One possible interaction is _____. One realistic action is _____ because _____.

Check Your Reasoning

A strong response names the dimension, cites the exact detail, and explains a possible connection. “This affects health” is too general. “The quieter space may support concentration because Mateo reports that following directions became easier” uses evidence and reasoning.
