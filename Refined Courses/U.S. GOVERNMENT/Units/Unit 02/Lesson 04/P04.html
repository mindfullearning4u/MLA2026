Worked Examples

Unit 2 · Lesson 4: Federal Courts and Judicial Review

Worked Example 1: Classify the power

Prompt: A federal district court finds a statute unconstitutional. The losing party argues that the trial court misunderstood Supreme Court precedent and appeals.

  1. Underline the government action—not merely the topic.
  2. Identify the actor and its claimed authority.
  3. Locate the rule in the federal court hierarchy diagram.
  4. Test the action against every required procedure or limit.
  5. Write a conclusion narrow enough to follow from the evidence.
Reasoned result: The court of appeals reviews the legal ruling and the relevant record; it does not normally start the entire trial over. It asks whether the district court used the correct legal rule and treated binding Supreme Court precedent properly.

Worked Example 2: Diagnose weak reasoning

“This action is constitutional because the government has power.”

This response is inadequate. It does not identify which government actor, the source and scope of power, the required procedure, or a competing limit. Repair it by citing the relevant constitutional feature and explaining how the facts satisfy—or fail to satisfy—its requirements.

Worked Example 3: Handle an ambiguous fact

If a scenario omits a decisive fact, state a conditional conclusion: “If ___, then ___ because ___; however, if ___, then ___.” Constitutional analysis is stronger when it acknowledges what the record cannot establish.

Notebook repair: Rewrite the weak response as a four-sentence CER analysis. Sentence 4 must address a limit, counterexample, or missing fact.
Constitutional featureWhat the evidence establishes
Article IIIVests federal judicial power in one Supreme Court and inferior courts Congress establishes, while defining categories of federal cases and controversies.
District courtsFederal trial courts receive evidence, determine facts through judges or juries, and apply law in the first instance.
Courts of appealsRegional appellate courts review district-court decisions for legal error; they generally do not hear new witnesses or retry facts.
Supreme CourtExercises limited original jurisdiction and mostly discretionary appellate review; denial of review is not a ruling endorsing the lower court’s reasoning.
Judicial reviewMarbury v. Madison articulated the judiciary’s duty to apply the Constitution when a statute conflicts with superior constitutional law.
LimitsCourts need jurisdiction and an actual justiciable dispute; judges do not initiate legislation or issue general advisory policy opinions.
