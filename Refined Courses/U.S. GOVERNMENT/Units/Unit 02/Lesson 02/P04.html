Worked Examples

Unit 2 · Lesson 2: Congress and the Lawmaking Process

Worked Example 1: Classify the power

Prompt: The House passes a transportation bill, but the Senate approves a version with different funding rules. The President supports the policy goal.

  1. Underline the government action—not merely the topic.
  2. Identify the actor and its claimed authority.
  3. Locate the rule in the bill-to-law flowchart.
  4. Test the action against every required procedure or limit.
  5. Write a conclusion narrow enough to follow from the evidence.
Reasoned result: The bill is not ready for presidential action because the chambers have not passed identical text. Congress must reconcile the versions and each chamber must approve the same final language before presentment. Presidential support cannot substitute for bicameral passage.

Worked Example 2: Diagnose weak reasoning

“This action is constitutional because the government has power.”

This response is inadequate. It does not identify which government actor, the source and scope of power, the required procedure, or a competing limit. Repair it by citing the relevant constitutional feature and explaining how the facts satisfy—or fail to satisfy—its requirements.

Worked Example 3: Handle an ambiguous fact

If a scenario omits a decisive fact, state a conditional conclusion: “If ___, then ___ because ___; however, if ___, then ___.” Constitutional analysis is stronger when it acknowledges what the record cannot establish.

Notebook repair: Rewrite the weak response as a four-sentence CER analysis. Sentence 4 must address a limit, counterexample, or missing fact.
Constitutional featureWhat the evidence establishes
RepresentationHouse seats are apportioned by population and members serve two-year terms; each state has two senators serving staggered six-year terms.
Enumerated powersArticle I, Section 8 lists powers including taxation, borrowing, regulating interstate and foreign commerce, coining money, and raising and supporting armed forces.
Necessary and Proper ClauseCongress may make laws necessary and proper for carrying its enumerated powers into execution; it is not an unlimited independent grant.
Committee stageCommittees and subcommittees gather information, hold hearings, revise bills, and decide whether measures advance. Many bills stop here.
Bicameral passageThe House and Senate must approve identical text. Disagreement may be resolved by amendment exchanges or a conference process.
PresentmentThe President may sign or veto a bill. Congress can override a veto with two-thirds in each chamber; constitutional timing rules also govern unsigned bills.
