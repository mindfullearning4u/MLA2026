Worked Examples

Unit 4 · Lesson 4: Public Opinion, Polling, and Civic Discourse

Worked Example 1: Classify the power

Prompt: An online poll posted by a candidate asks followers whether they support the “job-destroying tax plan.” Eighty-eight percent select “no,” and the campaign calls the result representative of all voters.

  1. Underline the government action—not merely the topic.
  2. Identify the actor and its claimed authority.
  3. Locate the rule in the poll interpretation table.
  4. Test the action against every required procedure or limit.
  5. Write a conclusion narrow enough to follow from the evidence.
Reasoned result: The result cannot support that population claim. The voluntary follower sample creates selection and coverage problems, and the wording is leading. Report only what the responding group selected; a representative estimate requires defensible sampling, neutral measurement, transparent methods, and uncertainty.

Worked Example 2: Diagnose weak reasoning

“This action is constitutional because the government has power.”

This response is inadequate. It does not identify which government actor, the source and scope of power, the required procedure, or a competing limit. Repair it by citing the relevant participation principle and explaining how the facts satisfy—or fail to satisfy—its requirements.

Worked Example 3: Handle an ambiguous fact

If a scenario omits a decisive fact, state a conditional conclusion: “If ___, then ___ because ___; however, if ___, then ___.” Constitutional analysis is stronger when it acknowledges what the record cannot establish.

Notebook repair: Rewrite the weak response as a four-sentence CER analysis. Sentence 4 must address a limit, counterexample, or missing fact.
Participation principleWhat the evidence establishes
Sampling frameAsk who could be selected, who was excluded, how respondents were reached, and whether nonresponse may differ systematically.
Question designOrder, wording, definitions, response choices, and interviewer or mode effects can change measured opinion.
UncertaintyMargins of error describe sampling uncertainty, not every error source; small subgroup estimates usually have greater uncertainty.
Timing and contextEvents, information, campaign activity, and field dates affect what a poll measures and whether comparison across polls is valid.
Message verificationSeparate claim, evidence, source, context, emotional technique, omitted information, and independent corroboration.
Constructive discourseState claims precisely, cite evidence, represent opposing views fairly, correct errors, and distinguish factual disagreement from value conflict.
