Notebook Task — Part 2

Evidence Set: Poll interpretation table

Scenario: An online poll posted by a candidate asks followers whether they support the “job-destroying tax plan.” Eighty-eight percent select “no,” and the campaign calls the result representative of all voters.

Participation principleWhat the evidence establishes
Sampling frameAsk who could be selected, who was excluded, how respondents were reached, and whether nonresponse may differ systematically.
Question designOrder, wording, definitions, response choices, and interviewer or mode effects can change measured opinion.
UncertaintyMargins of error describe sampling uncertainty, not every error source; small subgroup estimates usually have greater uncertainty.
Timing and contextEvents, information, campaign activity, and field dates affect what a poll measures and whether comparison across polls is valid.
Message verificationSeparate claim, evidence, source, context, emotional technique, omitted information, and independent corroboration.
Constructive discourseState claims precisely, cite evidence, represent opposing views fairly, correct errors, and distinguish factual disagreement from value conflict.

Guided Notes

  1. Claim: State a narrow answer that can be proven from the printed facts.
  2. Evidence 1: Quote or paraphrase the decisive scenario fact.
  3. Evidence 2: Select the exact rule from the visual.
  4. Reasoning: Explain how the fact satisfies, fails, or qualifies the rule.
  5. Limit: State what the evidence does not prove or what competing rule remains.

Model Analysis

The result cannot support that population claim. The voluntary follower sample creates selection and coverage problems, and the wording is leading. Report only what the responding group selected; a representative estimate requires defensible sampling, neutral measurement, transparent methods, and uncertainty.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: “The government may do this because it has authority.” This does not identify the source, scope, procedure, evidence, or limit.
Correct: A large response count does not automatically make a poll representative. Systematic bias can remain large even when thousands participate, while a smaller probability sample may better estimate a population.

Teachable explanation: A strong answer is narrower than the topic. It matches one actor and action to one controlling rule, then explains how a printed fact changes the conclusion.
