Unit 3 · Lesson 4: Citizenship, Responsibility, and Rule of Law

Worked Example 1: Apply the Rule

Evidence Set: Responsibility evidence chart

Scenario: A mayor orders city employees to ignore a court judgment, claiming that a recent election gives the administration authority to disregard any legal limit opposed by a majority.

Rights principleWhat the evidence establishes
Sources of citizenshipThe Constitution recognizes birth and naturalization as paths to citizenship; legal status is distinct from the quality or frequency of civic participation.
Rights and responsibilitiesConstitutional rights protect participation and dissent, while duties and voluntary responsibilities help institutions function and remain accountable.
Informed participationResponsible citizens compare evidence, distinguish fact from assertion, understand institutional authority, and revise beliefs when reliable evidence changes.
Voting and representationVoting is a protected political right and a mechanism of accountability, but other lawful forms of participation also shape agendas and oversight.
Jury service and legal complianceJuries place citizens inside the justice system; compliance with valid law supports order while lawful challenge and reform remain possible.
Rule-of-law safeguardsGenerality, publicity, stable procedures, independent adjudication, equal application, and lawful remedies reduce arbitrary power.

Use this evidence only: Every fact, term, and rule needed for the task is printed in this panel. No other page or lesson is required.

  1. Step 1 — Identify the action: Underline what the government actor did. Why: rights and powers attach to actions, not broad topics.
  2. Step 2 — Select evidence: Match the action to the most specific row in the visual. Why: a nearby concept is not automatically controlling.
  3. Step 3 — Test the facts: Find one supporting fact and one limiting fact. Why: civic conclusions require both authority and constraint.
  4. Step 4 — Conclude narrowly: State what follows and identify any missing decisive fact. Why: evidence cannot support an absolute claim unless the rule is absolute.
Interpreted answer: Electoral legitimacy grants authority within law, not authority above law. The rule of law requires officials to comply with binding judgments or pursue lawful appeal and reform. Citizens can support policy change while rejecting defiance that eliminates neutral legal limits and remedies.

Worked Example 2: Repair Weak Reasoning

Incorrect: “This policy is valid because government must solve problems.”
  1. Replace “government” with the named actor.
  2. Replace “solve problems” with the exact authority or protection.
  3. Add the decisive printed fact.
  4. Add the procedure, limit, or counterclaim.

Worked Example 3: Handle Uncertainty

Problem: Suppose the scenario does not tell whether a required procedure occurred.

  1. Name the missing fact.
  2. Write: “If the procedure occurred, then ___ because ___.”
  3. Write: “If it did not occur, then ___ because ___.”
  4. Identify the record, document, or testimony that would resolve the uncertainty.

Interpretation: Conditional reasoning is more accurate than guessing. It shows exactly how new evidence would change the conclusion.
