Worked Examples

Unit 3 · Lesson 4: Citizenship, Responsibility, and Rule of Law

Worked Example 1: Classify the power

Prompt: A mayor orders city employees to ignore a court judgment, claiming that a recent election gives the administration authority to disregard any legal limit opposed by a majority.

  1. Underline the government action—not merely the topic.
  2. Identify the actor and its claimed authority.
  3. Locate the rule in the responsibility evidence chart.
  4. Test the action against every required procedure or limit.
  5. Write a conclusion narrow enough to follow from the evidence.
Reasoned result: Electoral legitimacy grants authority within law, not authority above law. The rule of law requires officials to comply with binding judgments or pursue lawful appeal and reform. Citizens can support policy change while rejecting defiance that eliminates neutral legal limits and remedies.

Worked Example 2: Diagnose weak reasoning

“This action is constitutional because the government has power.”

This response is inadequate. It does not identify which government actor, the source and scope of power, the required procedure, or a competing limit. Repair it by citing the relevant rights principle and explaining how the facts satisfy—or fail to satisfy—its requirements.

Worked Example 3: Handle an ambiguous fact

If a scenario omits a decisive fact, state a conditional conclusion: “If ___, then ___ because ___; however, if ___, then ___.” Constitutional analysis is stronger when it acknowledges what the record cannot establish.

Notebook repair: Rewrite the weak response as a four-sentence CER analysis. Sentence 4 must address a limit, counterexample, or missing fact.

Sources of citizenshipThe Constitution recognizes birth and naturalization as paths to citizenship; legal status is distinct from the quality or frequency of civic participation.
Rights and responsibilitiesConstitutional rights protect participation and dissent, while duties and voluntary responsibilities help institutions function and remain accountable.
Informed participationResponsible citizens compare evidence, distinguish fact from assertion, understand institutional authority, and revise beliefs when reliable evidence changes.
Voting and representationVoting is a protected political right and a mechanism of accountability, but other lawful forms of participation also shape agendas and oversight.
Jury service and legal complianceJuries place citizens inside the justice system; compliance with valid law supports order while lawful challenge and reform remain possible.
Rule-of-law safeguardsGenerality, publicity, stable procedures, independent adjudication, equal application, and lawful remedies reduce arbitrary power.
