Notebook Task — Part 2

Evidence Set: Rights scenario table

Scenario: A city requires a permit for every public demonstration, gives officials no written standards, and denies a permit after criticizing the group’s message.

Rights principleWhat the evidence establishes
First AmendmentProtects religion, speech, press, peaceful assembly, and petition; protection is strong but does not make every act or expression immune from neutral law.
Second AmendmentProtects an individual right to keep and bear arms, while constitutional doctrine also permits some regulation consistent with the amendment.
Fourth AmendmentGuards against unreasonable searches and seizures and requires warrants to be supported by probable cause and particularity.
Fifth AmendmentProtects due process, bars compelled self-incrimination and double jeopardy, and governs federal takings of private property.
Sixth and Seventh AmendmentsProtect fair criminal procedures and preserve civil jury trial in covered federal cases.
Eighth through Tenth AmendmentsAddress bail, fines, punishment, unenumerated rights, and powers reserved to states or the people.

Guided Notes

  1. Claim: State a narrow answer that can be proven from the printed facts.
  2. Evidence 1: Quote or paraphrase the decisive scenario fact.
  3. Evidence 2: Select the exact rule from the visual.
  4. Reasoning: Explain how the fact satisfies, fails, or qualifies the rule.
  5. Limit: State what the evidence does not prove or what competing rule remains.

Model Analysis

The city may use content-neutral time, place, and manner rules to coordinate safety, but unbounded discretion and message-based denial threaten speech and assembly. A strong analysis separates the legitimate permitting purpose from discriminatory application and asks whether clear, neutral standards and review procedures exist.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: “The government may do this because it has authority.” This does not identify the source, scope, procedure, evidence, or limit.
Correct: A constitutional right is not merely a personal preference, but neither is it automatically absolute. Identify the protected liberty, the government actor, the kind of restriction, and the constitutional standard before reaching a conclusion.

Teachable explanation: A strong answer is narrower than the topic. It matches one actor and action to one controlling rule, then explains how a printed fact changes the conclusion.
