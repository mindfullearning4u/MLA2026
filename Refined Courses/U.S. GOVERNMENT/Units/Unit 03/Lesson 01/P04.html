Unit 3 · Lesson 1: Bill of Rights and Civil Liberties

Worked Example 1: Apply the Rule

Evidence Set: Rights scenario table

Scenario: A city requires a permit for every public demonstration, gives officials no written standards, and denies a permit after criticizing the group’s message.

Rights principleWhat the evidence establishes
First AmendmentProtects religion, speech, press, peaceful assembly, and petition; protection is strong but does not make every act or expression immune from neutral law.
Second AmendmentProtects an individual right to keep and bear arms, while constitutional doctrine also permits some regulation consistent with the amendment.
Fourth AmendmentGuards against unreasonable searches and seizures and requires warrants to be supported by probable cause and particularity.
Fifth AmendmentProtects due process, bars compelled self-incrimination and double jeopardy, and governs federal takings of private property.
Sixth and Seventh AmendmentsProtect fair criminal procedures and preserve civil jury trial in covered federal cases.
Eighth through Tenth AmendmentsAddress bail, fines, punishment, unenumerated rights, and powers reserved to states or the people.

Use this evidence only: Every fact, term, and rule needed for the task is printed in this panel. No other page or lesson is required.

  1. Step 1 — Identify the action: Underline what the government actor did. Why: rights and powers attach to actions, not broad topics.
  2. Step 2 — Select evidence: Match the action to the most specific row in the visual. Why: a nearby concept is not automatically controlling.
  3. Step 3 — Test the facts: Find one supporting fact and one limiting fact. Why: civic conclusions require both authority and constraint.
  4. Step 4 — Conclude narrowly: State what follows and identify any missing decisive fact. Why: evidence cannot support an absolute claim unless the rule is absolute.
Interpreted answer: The city may use content-neutral time, place, and manner rules to coordinate safety, but unbounded discretion and message-based denial threaten speech and assembly. A strong analysis separates the legitimate permitting purpose from discriminatory application and asks whether clear, neutral standards and review procedures exist.

Worked Example 2: Repair Weak Reasoning

Incorrect: “This policy is valid because government must solve problems.”
  1. Replace “government” with the named actor.
  2. Replace “solve problems” with the exact authority or protection.
  3. Add the decisive printed fact.
  4. Add the procedure, limit, or counterclaim.
Correct: The city may use content-neutral time, place, and manner rules to coordinate safety, but unbounded discretion and message-based denial threaten speech and assembly. Worked Example 3: Handle Uncertainty

Problem: Suppose the scenario does not tell whether a required procedure occurred.

  1. Name the missing fact.
  2. Write: “If the procedure occurred, then ___ because ___.”
  3. Write: “If it did not occur, then ___ because ___.”
  4. Identify the record, document, or testimony that would resolve the uncertainty.

Interpretation: Conditional reasoning is more accurate than guessing. It shows exactly how new evidence would change the conclusion.

Verification: Each example contains an actual problem, numbered reasoning, why each step matters, and an interpreted conclusion.
