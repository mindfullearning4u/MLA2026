Worked Examples

Unit 3 · Lesson 1: Bill of Rights and Civil Liberties

Worked Example 1: Classify the power

Prompt: A city requires a permit for every public demonstration, gives officials no written standards, and denies a permit after criticizing the group’s message.

  1. Underline the government action—not merely the topic.
  2. Identify the actor and its claimed authority.
  3. Locate the rule in the rights scenario table.
  4. Test the action against every required procedure or limit.
  5. Write a conclusion narrow enough to follow from the evidence.
Reasoned result: The city may use content-neutral time, place, and manner rules to coordinate safety, but unbounded discretion and message-based denial threaten speech and assembly. A strong analysis separates the legitimate permitting purpose from discriminatory application and asks whether clear, neutral standards and review procedures exist.

Worked Example 2: Diagnose weak reasoning

“This action is constitutional because the government has power.”

This response is inadequate. It does not identify which government actor, the source and scope of power, the required procedure, or a competing limit. Repair it by citing the relevant rights principle and explaining how the facts satisfy—or fail to satisfy—its requirements.

Worked Example 3: Handle an ambiguous fact

If a scenario omits a decisive fact, state a conditional conclusion: “If ___, then ___ because ___; however, if ___, then ___.” Constitutional analysis is stronger when it acknowledges what the record cannot establish.

Notebook repair: Rewrite the weak response as a four-sentence CER analysis. Sentence 4 must address a limit, counterexample, or missing fact.

First AmendmentProtects religion, speech, press, peaceful assembly, and petition; protection is strong but does not make every act or expression immune from neutral law.
Second AmendmentProtects an individual right to keep and bear arms, while constitutional doctrine also permits some regulation consistent with the amendment.
Fourth AmendmentGuards against unreasonable searches and seizures and requires warrants to be supported by probable cause and particularity.
Fifth AmendmentProtects due process, bars compelled self-incrimination and double jeopardy, and governs federal takings of private property.
Sixth and Seventh AmendmentsProtect fair criminal procedures and preserve civil jury trial in covered federal cases.
Eighth through Tenth AmendmentsAddress bail, fines, punishment, unenumerated rights, and powers reserved to states or the people.
