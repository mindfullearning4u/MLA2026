Unit 3 · Lesson 5: Synthesis: Rights, Responsibilities, and Constitutional Limits

Worked Example 1: Apply the Rule

Evidence Set: Rights balancing matrix

Scenario: A city bans all demonstrations near government buildings for one year after several events caused traffic delays. The ban applies to every viewpoint, but it eliminates the principal public forum for petitioning officials and leaves only remote locations.

Rights principleWhat the evidence establishes
Identify the claimant and actorDetermine who asserts a right and whether the challenged conduct is attributable to federal, state, or local government.
Define the protected interestName the specific liberty, equality guarantee, procedure, or participation right rather than invoking freedom generally.
Characterize the burdenAsk whether government targets content or identity, imposes a neutral incidental burden, denies process, or applies a rule unequally.
Test justification and fitIdentify the government interest, the applicable constitutional standard, and whether the means are appropriately connected and no broader than permitted.
Select institutional responseCourts, legislatures, executives, agencies, states, communities, and citizens possess different lawful powers and remedies.
Evaluate implementationA formal ruling or statute matters only if institutions communicate, fund, administer, monitor, and enforce the protection.

Use this evidence only: Every fact, term, and rule needed for the task is printed in this panel. No other page or lesson is required.

  1. Step 1 — Identify the action: Underline what the government actor did. Why: rights and powers attach to actions, not broad topics.
  2. Step 2 — Select evidence: Match the action to the most specific row in the visual. Why: a nearby concept is not automatically controlling.
  3. Step 3 — Test the facts: Find one supporting fact and one limiting fact. Why: civic conclusions require both authority and constraint.
  4. Step 4 — Conclude narrowly: State what follows and identify any missing decisive fact. Why: evidence cannot support an absolute claim unless the rule is absolute.
Interpreted answer: Viewpoint neutrality answers one concern but not the entire First Amendment inquiry. The city has legitimate safety and access interests, yet a yearlong blanket ban may burden substantially more expression than necessary and leave inadequate alternatives. Analysis must test scope, duration, forum, alternatives, evidence, and less restrictive options.

Worked Example 2: Repair Weak Reasoning

Incorrect: “This policy is valid because government must solve problems.”
  1. Replace “government” with the named actor.
  2. Replace “solve problems” with the exact authority or protection.
  3. Add the decisive printed fact.
  4. Add the procedure, limit, or counterclaim.

Worked Example 3: Handle Uncertainty

Problem: Suppose the scenario does not tell whether a required procedure occurred.

  1. Name the missing fact.
  2. Write: “If the procedure occurred, then ___ because ___.”
  3. Write: “If it did not occur, then ___ because ___.”
  4. Identify the record, document, or testimony that would resolve the uncertainty.

Interpretation: Conditional reasoning is more accurate than guessing. It shows exactly how new evidence would change the conclusion.
