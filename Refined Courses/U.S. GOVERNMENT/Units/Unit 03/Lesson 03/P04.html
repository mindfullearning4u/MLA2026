Unit 3 · Lesson 3: Civil Rights Movements and Government Responses

Worked Example 1: Apply the Rule

Evidence Set: Rights-response timeline

Scenario: A state uses a literacy test administered only to selected voter-registration applicants. A coalition documents the pattern, brings a constitutional lawsuit, organizes public demonstrations, and asks Congress for nationwide enforcement rules.

Rights principleWhat the evidence establishes
Reconstruction AmendmentsThe Thirteenth abolished slavery, the Fourteenth established citizenship and equality protections, and the Fifteenth prohibited racial discrimination in voting.
Legal resistance and retrenchmentAfter Reconstruction, discriminatory laws, violence, administrative barriers, and narrow enforcement restricted promised rights.
Movement strategiesOrganizers combined litigation, boycotts, marches, voter registration, testimony, media, coalition building, and direct action.
Judicial responsesCourts can invalidate unconstitutional government action and order remedies in actual cases, but implementation may require executive and local compliance.
Legislative responsesCongress enacted major civil-rights and voting laws using its constitutional powers, creating rules and enforcement mechanisms beyond a single lawsuit.
Executive and administrative responsesPresidents and agencies enforced court orders and statutes, deployed federal authority, investigated violations, and shaped implementation.

Use this evidence only: Every fact, term, and rule needed for the task is printed in this panel. No other page or lesson is required.

  1. Step 1 — Identify the action: Underline what the government actor did. Why: rights and powers attach to actions, not broad topics.
  2. Step 2 — Select evidence: Match the action to the most specific row in the visual. Why: a nearby concept is not automatically controlling.
  3. Step 3 — Test the facts: Find one supporting fact and one limiting fact. Why: civic conclusions require both authority and constraint.
  4. Step 4 — Conclude narrowly: State what follows and identify any missing decisive fact. Why: evidence cannot support an absolute claim unless the rule is absolute.
Interpreted answer: The selective test burdens voting and raises Fifteenth and Fourteenth Amendment concerns. Litigation can challenge the state practice, collective action can expose the pattern, Congress can legislate using constitutional enforcement authority, and the executive branch can implement valid federal remedies. Each strategy has a different institutional function.

Worked Example 2: Repair Weak Reasoning

Incorrect: “This policy is valid because government must solve problems.”
  1. Replace “government” with the named actor.
  2. Replace “solve problems” with the exact authority or protection.
  3. Add the decisive printed fact.
  4. Add the procedure, limit, or counterclaim.

Worked Example 3: Handle Uncertainty

Problem: Suppose the scenario does not tell whether a required procedure occurred.

  1. Name the missing fact.
  2. Write: “If the procedure occurred, then ___ because ___.”
  3. Write: “If it did not occur, then ___ because ___.”
  4. Identify the record, document, or testimony that would resolve the uncertainty.

Interpretation: Conditional reasoning is more accurate than guessing. It shows exactly how new evidence would change the conclusion.
