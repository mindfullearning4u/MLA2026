Worked Examples

Unit 3 · Lesson 3: Civil Rights Movements and Government Responses

Worked Example 1: Classify the power

Prompt: A state uses a literacy test administered only to selected voter-registration applicants. A coalition documents the pattern, brings a constitutional lawsuit, organizes public demonstrations, and asks Congress for nationwide enforcement rules.

  1. Underline the government action—not merely the topic.
  2. Identify the actor and its claimed authority.
  3. Locate the rule in the rights-response timeline.
  4. Test the action against every required procedure or limit.
  5. Write a conclusion narrow enough to follow from the evidence.
Reasoned result: The selective test burdens voting and raises Fifteenth and Fourteenth Amendment concerns. Litigation can challenge the state practice, collective action can expose the pattern, Congress can legislate using constitutional enforcement authority, and the executive branch can implement valid federal remedies. Each strategy has a different institutional function.

Worked Example 2: Diagnose weak reasoning

“This action is constitutional because the government has power.”

This response is inadequate. It does not identify which government actor, the source and scope of power, the required procedure, or a competing limit. Repair it by citing the relevant rights principle and explaining how the facts satisfy—or fail to satisfy—its requirements.

Worked Example 3: Handle an ambiguous fact

If a scenario omits a decisive fact, state a conditional conclusion: “If ___, then ___ because ___; however, if ___, then ___.” Constitutional analysis is stronger when it acknowledges what the record cannot establish.
