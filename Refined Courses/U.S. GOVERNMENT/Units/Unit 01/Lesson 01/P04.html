P04 Civic Evidence and Reasoning: Worked Examples

Natural Rights, Rule of Law, and Limited Government

Standard for This Page

MLA.USG.FND.1: Explain natural rights, consent of the governed, republicanism, limited government, rule of law, and civic virtue as foundations of American constitutional government.

Student-friendly meaning: I can explain where government authority comes from, which rights government must protect, and why leaders and citizens must follow constitutional rules.

How to Use the Examples

For each example, follow the same reasoning routine: identify the government action, locate the strongest evidence, match the evidence to a principle, eliminate nearby but weaker concepts, and explain the result.

Worked Example 1: A Leader Above the Law

Scenario: A mayor orders police to seize a critic's property even though no law authorizes the seizure.

  1. Identify the action: Property is seized without lawful authority.
  2. Find the strongest clue: The mayor acts as if personal office creates unlimited power.
  3. Match the principle: Limited government is violated because the official lacks legal authority. Rule of law is also violated because power is not exercised through known law.
  4. Choose the strongest answer: If only one answer is allowed, select limited government when the question emphasizes the boundary on power; select rule of law when it emphasizes lawful procedure and accountability.
  5. Explain: The mayor cannot replace law with personal will.

Worked Example 2: Authority from the People

Scenario: Residents elect council members, attend hearings, and vote on a charter amendment.

  1. Identify the action: The public authorizes representatives and participates in constitutional change.
  2. Find the clue: Authority flows from citizens through lawful procedures.
  3. Match the principle: Consent of the governed is primary; republicanism explains the use of elected representatives.
  4. Explain why: The government is legitimate because the people authorize and structure its power.

Worked Example 3: Responsible Self-Government

Scenario: Before voting on a local proposal, a citizen reads the proposal, checks evidence from multiple sources, listens respectfully to opposing views, and participates lawfully.

  1. Identify the behavior: Informed, responsible participation.
  2. Match the principle: Civic virtue.
  3. Reject a weak answer: Natural rights protect participation, but the scenario emphasizes the citizen's responsible habits.
  4. Explain why: Constitutional government works better when citizens use freedom responsibly and consider the common good.
Principle Ask yourself Evidence to look for Non-example
Natural rights What rights belong to every person? Life, liberty, equality, safety, property, or pursuit of happiness A privilege that a leader may remove for any reason
Consent of the governed Who gives government legitimate authority? Elections, representation, public approval, or the people creating government A ruler claiming power without public authorization
Republicanism How do citizens govern in a large nation? Citizens choose representatives who act under law Officials inherit permanent power
Limited government What prevents government from using unlimited power? A constitution, enumerated powers, rights, procedures, and enforceable limits Officials may do anything they choose
Rule of law Who must obey the law? Citizens and officials are accountable to known, fairly applied laws Leaders are above the law
Civic virtue What habits help self-government work? Informed participation, responsibility, honesty, respect, and concern for the common good Ignoring lawful duties while demanding benefits

Transfer the Method

When two principles appear possible, do not guess. Ask which principle the question emphasizes: the source of authority, the use of representatives, the boundary on power, lawful accountability, basic rights, or responsible citizenship.
