Unit 5 · Lesson 4: Budgeting, Taxing, Spending, and Tradeoffs

Worked Example 1: Apply the Rule

Evidence Set: Budget tradeoff table

Scenario: A county has $12 million available for flood protection. One plan funds a barrier protecting the largest tax base; another distributes drainage upgrades across lower-income neighborhoods with repeated smaller losses.

Policy principleWhat the evidence establishes
Revenue choicesIncome, payroll, sales, property, excise, user-fee, and other systems differ in base, rate, administration, stability, incidence, and incentives.
Budget authorityLegislatures authorize taxes and appropriations; executives propose and administer budgets within law; agencies obligate and spend funds.
Baseline and time horizonA budget comparison depends on current-law assumptions, inflation, population, economic conditions, and the years included.
Distribution and incidenceLegal liability may differ from who ultimately bears a tax or receives a benefit; effects vary across income, place, age, and industry.
TradeoffsEvery allocation has opportunity costs involving services, taxes, borrowing, reserves, timing, quality, equity, and risk.
AccountabilityPublic budgets require transparent categories, legal authorization, controls, audits, performance measures, and comparison of planned and actual results.

Use this evidence only: Every fact, term, and rule needed for the task is printed in this panel. No other page or lesson is required.

  1. Step 1 — Identify the action: Underline what the government actor did. Why: rights and powers attach to actions, not broad topics.
  2. Step 2 — Select evidence: Match the action to the most specific row in the visual. Why: a nearby concept is not automatically controlling.
  3. Step 3 — Test the facts: Find one supporting fact and one limiting fact. Why: civic conclusions require both authority and constraint.
  4. Step 4 — Conclude narrowly: State what follows and identify any missing decisive fact. Why: evidence cannot support an absolute claim unless the rule is absolute.
Interpreted answer: A defensible choice compares expected losses avoided, people and critical facilities protected, useful life, maintenance, uncertainty, distribution, legal duties, matching funds, and opportunity cost. Maximizing protected property value and maximizing equitable risk reduction are different objectives that must be stated openly.

Worked Example 2: Repair Weak Reasoning

Incorrect: “This policy is valid because government must solve problems.”
  1. Replace “government” with the named actor.
  2. Replace “solve problems” with the exact authority or protection.
  3. Add the decisive printed fact.
  4. Add the procedure, limit, or counterclaim.

Worked Example 3: Handle Uncertainty

Problem: Suppose the scenario does not tell whether a required procedure occurred.

  1. Name the missing fact.
  2. Write: “If the procedure occurred, then ___ because ___.”
  3. Write: “If it did not occur, then ___ because ___.”
  4. Identify the record, document, or testimony that would resolve the uncertainty.

Interpretation: Conditional reasoning is more accurate than guessing. It shows exactly how new evidence would change the conclusion.
