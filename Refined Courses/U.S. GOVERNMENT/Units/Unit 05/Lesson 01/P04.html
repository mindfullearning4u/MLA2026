Worked Examples

Unit 5 · Lesson 1: How Public Policy Is Made

Worked Example 1: Classify the power

Prompt: A city reports rising pedestrian injuries and proposes lower speed limits, redesigned intersections, automated enforcement, and a public-education campaign.

  1. Underline the government action—not merely the topic.
  2. Identify the actor and its claimed authority.
  3. Locate the rule in the policy cycle.
  4. Test the action against every required procedure or limit.
  5. Write a conclusion narrow enough to follow from the evidence.
Reasoned result: A sound process begins with reliable crash, location, exposure, and enforcement data; identifies city and state authority; compares costs and distributional effects; funds administration; defines outputs and injury outcomes; and schedules evaluation. Choosing a popular intervention before diagnosing the pattern risks treating the wrong cause.

Worked Example 2: Diagnose weak reasoning

“This action is constitutional because the government has power.”

This response is inadequate. It does not identify which government actor, the source and scope of power, the required procedure, or a competing limit. Repair it by citing the relevant policy principle and explaining how the facts satisfy—or fail to satisfy—its requirements.

Worked Example 3: Handle an ambiguous fact

If a scenario omits a decisive fact, state a conditional conclusion: “If ___, then ___ because ___; however, if ___, then ___.” Constitutional analysis is stronger when it acknowledges what the record cannot establish.

Notebook repair: Rewrite the weak response as a four-sentence CER analysis. Sentence 4 must address a limit, counterexample, or missing fact.
