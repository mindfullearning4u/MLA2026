Unit 5 · Lesson 3: Landmark Supreme Court Cases

Notebook Task — Part 2

Evidence Set: Case reasoning chart

Scenario: A school disciplines students for wearing small symbols supporting a policy cause. Officials cite possible controversy but provide no evidence of disruption or interference with others’ rights.

Policy principleWhat the evidence establishes
Marbury v. MadisonEstablished judicial review in a concrete dispute by treating the Constitution as superior law when a statute conflicts with it.
McCulloch v. MarylandRead the Necessary and Proper Clause to permit appropriate means for enumerated powers and rejected a state tax interfering with a federal instrument.
Brown v. Board of EducationHeld state-mandated racial segregation in public schools inconsistent with equal protection, rejecting separate educational facilities as inherently unequal.
Gideon v. WainwrightApplied the Sixth Amendment right to appointed counsel in felony cases to states through the Fourteenth Amendment.
Tinker v. Des MoinesProtected student expression that did not materially and substantially disrupt school operations while recognizing the school context.
Case-reading methodSeparate facts, procedural posture, question, constitutional text, rule, reasoning, holding, remedy, concurrence or dissent, and later application.

Use this evidence only: Every fact, term, and rule needed for the task is printed in this panel. No other page or lesson is required.

Guided Notes

  1. Claim: State a narrow answer that can be proven from the printed facts.
  2. Evidence 1: Quote or paraphrase the decisive scenario fact.
  3. Evidence 2: Select the exact rule from the visual.
  4. Reasoning: Explain how the fact satisfies, fails, or qualifies the rule.
  5. Limit: State what the evidence does not prove or what competing rule remains.

Model Analysis

Tinker supplies a relevant student-speech framework, but accurate analysis compares material facts rather than quoting a slogan. Ask whether the expression caused or could reasonably forecast material and substantial disruption, whether the rule targets viewpoint, and whether later precedent or a different school context changes the rule.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: “The government may do this because it has authority.” This does not identify the source, scope, procedure, evidence, or limit.
Correct: A case name is not analysis. The holding must be stated narrowly, connected to decisive facts and reasoning, and distinguished from dicta, separate opinions, and later applications.

Teachable explanation: A strong answer is narrower than the topic. It matches one actor and action to one controlling rule, then explains how a printed fact changes the conclusion.
