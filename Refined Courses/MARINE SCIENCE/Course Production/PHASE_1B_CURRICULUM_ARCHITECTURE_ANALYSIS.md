# Marine Science Phase 1B: Curriculum Architecture Analysis

## Executive Summary

Marine Science must function as a mastery-based high-school laboratory science course centered on ocean systems. The course architecture develops scientific inquiry, oceanographic data skills, physical ocean systems, marine chemistry, marine geology, marine ecosystems, biodiversity, food webs, fisheries, human impacts, conservation, and ocean exploration.

The approved six-unit structure is instructionally sound and maintains Marine Science identity when each unit remains anchored in ocean evidence rather than drifting into general Biology, Environmental Science, Earth/Space Science, or Chemistry.

## Course Identity

| Area | Architecture Requirement |
|---|---|
| Primary discipline | Marine Science |
| Primary setting | Oceans, coasts, estuaries, reefs, marine zones, and marine data systems |
| Required science mode | Laboratory, field-data, simulation, map, model, graph, and evidence-based reasoning |
| Boundaries | Biology, Environmental Science, Earth/Space Science, and Chemistry appear only as support concepts required to explain marine systems |

## Skill Strand Recommendations

| Strand | Why It Belongs | Mastery Expectation | Progression |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scientific Inquiry | Marine Science relies on evidence from field, lab, remote-sensing, and exploration contexts. | Students ask marine questions, evaluate claims, and communicate evidence-based explanations. | Unit 1 foundation, spiraled through all units. |
| Marine Data and Lab Skills | Ocean systems require maps, water-quality data, graphs, models, sampling, and uncertainty reasoning. | Students interpret and explain marine evidence responsibly. | Unit 1 foundation, applied in Units 2-6. |
| Physical Ocean Systems | Ocean basins, currents, tides, waves, zones, and climate interactions define marine conditions. | Students explain how physical ocean conditions shape marine environments. | Unit 2 primary, revisited through ecosystems and global systems. |
| Marine Chemistry | Salinity, density, nutrients, gases, pH, and water properties shape ocean habitability. | Students explain chemical controls in marine systems without drifting into general chemistry. | Unit 2 primary, spiraled through ecosystems and impacts. |
| Marine Geology and Coasts | Seafloor, sediments, coastal processes, estuaries, and sea-level change affect habitats and communities. | Students connect geologic and coastal processes to marine systems. | Unit 2 primary, revisited in conservation and global systems. |
| Marine Ecology | Food webs, populations, limiting factors, niches, and energy flow drive ocean life. | Students analyze marine ecosystem structure and function. | Units 3-4 primary. |
| Marine Biodiversity | Reefs, estuaries, mangroves, seagrasses, deep sea, open ocean, and polar seas require biodiversity reasoning. | Students compare habitats and explain organism distribution and adaptation. | Unit 3 primary, spiraled in Units 4-5. |
| Human Impacts and Conservation | Fisheries, pollution, climate impacts, monitoring, restoration, and sustainability are major Marine Science applications. | Students evaluate impact evidence and propose conservation decisions. | Unit 5 primary, reinforced in Unit 6 synthesis. |
| Ocean Exploration and Technology | Marine Science depends on tools that extend human observation into ocean environments. | Students interpret sonar, satellite, ROV, sensor, and mapping evidence. | Unit 6 primary, supported throughout. |

## Recommended Six-Unit Architecture

| Unit | Unit Title | Purpose |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Marine Science Foundations and Ocean Inquiry | Establish inquiry, safety, field/lab data, maps, models, evidence, and ocean-system overview. |
| 2 | Ocean Structure, Chemistry, and Physical Systems | Develop ocean basins, zones, currents, water chemistry, marine geology, and coastal systems. |
| 3 | Marine Ecosystems and Biodiversity | Develop marine habitats, biodiversity, ecosystem components, adaptations, and organism distribution. |
| 4 | Marine Processes, Food Webs, and Energy Flow | Develop populations, limiting factors, trophic systems, productivity, cycles, and ecosystem change. |
| 5 | Human Impacts, Fisheries, and Marine Conservation | Develop fisheries, pollution, monitoring, biodiversity loss, restoration, conservation, and sustainability. |
| 6 | Ocean Exploration, Marine Technology, and Global Ocean Systems | Develop exploration tools, remote sensing, global ocean systems, career communication, and full-course synthesis. |

## Text, Data, and Source Recommendations

Marine Science lessons should use ocean maps, bathymetry profiles, current maps, tide/wave data, water-quality tables, marine organism profiles, food webs, fisheries data, coral reef monitoring records, coastal erosion evidence, NOAA-style data displays, marine conservation case studies, and technology diagrams. These are architecture expectations only and do not create lesson content.

## Writing and Communication Progression

Students should progress from short evidence explanations to data-supported claims, source evaluation, lab/field notes, graph interpretation, conservation recommendations, and technical communication. Writing should remain scientific and evidence-based.

## Language and Vocabulary Progression

Vocabulary should move from foundational ocean terms to domain-specific terms: salinity, density, bathymetry, upwelling, pelagic, benthic, estuary, trophic level, productivity, ocean acidification, fisheries, bycatch, remote sensing, and conservation. Vocabulary must be taught in marine contexts.

## Architecture Recommendation

The six-unit architecture is approved for crosswalk and mapping development. It provides adequate coverage of Florida/CPALMS Marine Science expectations, college/workforce readiness, ACT/SAT science-reading readiness, and scientific literacy while preserving Marine Science identity.
