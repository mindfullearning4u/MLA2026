P03 — Historical Evidence Lab

How Historians Read Constitutional Evidence

  1. Source: identify creator and type.
  2. Claim: state the legal or historical principle.
  3. Change: explain what became different.
  4. Protection: identify who or what gained protection.
  5. Unresolved: identify what the text did not settle.
  6. Limitation: explain what this source alone cannot prove.

Evidence A — Thirteenth Amendment

Key language: “Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude, except as a punishment for crime...” Congress received enforcement authority.

Worked example: Claim—slavery became constitutionally prohibited nationwide. Change—abolition no longer depended on wartime executive power. Protected—people held in slavery. Unresolved—land, suffrage, education, and safety. Limitation—the text establishes law but does not prove enforcement or lived equality.

National Archives: 13th Amendment

Pause and Review — Notebook Task 1

Using the worked example, explain in one sentence why constitutional text is powerful evidence but incomplete evidence of lived experience.

Evidence B — Fourteenth Amendment

Key language: persons born or naturalized in the United States are citizens; no state may deprive a person of life, liberty, or property without due process or deny equal protection.

ClauseAccurate paraphraseHistorical importance
CitizenshipBirth or naturalization establishes national and state citizenship.Reversed exclusion from national membership.
Due processStates must use lawful process before major deprivations.Placed limits on state action.
Equal protectionStates must provide equal legal protection.Created a federal constitutional equality principle.

National Archives: 14th Amendment

Evidence C — Fifteenth Amendment

Key language: voting rights cannot be denied “on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude.” Congress received enforcement authority.

Worked analysis: It protected political participation against specified racial discrimination, but its wording did not bar every type of voting restriction and did not enfranchise women.

National Archives: 15th Amendment

Evidence D — African American Perspective

Freedpeople’s petitions and convention resolutions called for citizenship, voting, equality, land security, and federal protection. These sources show that formerly enslaved people were political actors who defined freedom as more than the absence of slavery.

Usefulness: reveals demands and lived stakes. Limitation: one petition cannot represent every freedperson.

Evidence E — Opposing or Limiting Perspective

State-centered opponents argued that expanded federal authority threatened state control and resisted Black political rights. Such evidence helps explain why constitutional guarantees faced political resistance.

Usefulness: reveals opposition. Limitation: partisan argument may rely on prejudice, exaggeration, or propaganda and does not establish that its claims were correct.

Evidence F — Secondary Interpretation

Historians describe the Reconstruction Amendments as a constitutional transformation because they nationalized abolition, citizenship, rights protection, and federal enforcement power. A secondary account synthesizes many sources but remains an interpretation that must be checked against primary evidence.
