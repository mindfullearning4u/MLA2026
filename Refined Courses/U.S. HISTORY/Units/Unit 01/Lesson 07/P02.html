Pause and Review 1

Why was this more than a list of inventions? Steel, oil, electricity, railroads, and communication worked together with capital, resources, labor, and expanding markets. Each part helped firms produce and sell on a national scale.

Part 2 — Industry and business

Steel supported rails, bridges, buildings, and machinery. Oil created refining and energy industries. Electricity and communication sped production and coordination. Railroads connected raw materials, factories, workers, and consumers.

Corporations gathered capital for large enterprises. Carnegie’s steel operations illustrate vertical integration; Standard Oil’s consolidation of competing refiners illustrates horizontal integration. Large firms expanded production and markets, but their power raised concerns about competition and inequality.

Pause and Review 2

Worked connection: Railroads bought steel, transported oil and goods, opened markets, and moved workers. Industry therefore expanded railroads while railroads expanded industry—a reinforcing cause-and-effect relationship.

Part 3 — Workers and labor conflict

Industrial jobs offered wages and opportunity, but conditions varied by industry, skill, race, gender, age, and immigration status. Long hours, accidents, low bargaining power, and factory discipline led some workers to organize. The Knights of Labor and American Federation of Labor pursued worker goals; collective bargaining and strikes applied pressure. Haymarket, Homestead, and Pullman show that workers, employers, government, and the press could interpret the same conflict differently.

Part 4 — Immigration and cities

Industrial demand joined push factors and family networks to shape immigration. Many European immigrants settled in Eastern cities and worked in factories; Chinese immigrants in the West worked in railroads, mining, agriculture, and services. Both built communities and faced discrimination, but regional jobs and laws differed. The Chinese Exclusion Act made discrimination federal policy.

Immigration and internal migration contributed to urbanization. Cities supplied work, transportation, services, businesses, churches, mutual-aid groups, newspapers, and cultural communities. Dense tenements could also face sanitation, fire, and safety problems.

Notebook Summary — copy this

The Second Industrial Revolution grew from interacting technology, capital, resources, transportation, communication, labor, and markets. Corporations increased production and opportunity while concentrating market power and wealth. Industrial jobs drew migrants and immigrants, accelerated urbanization, and produced varied community life and labor conflict. A strong historical explanation weighs both opportunities and costs and connects economic changes to social consequences.
