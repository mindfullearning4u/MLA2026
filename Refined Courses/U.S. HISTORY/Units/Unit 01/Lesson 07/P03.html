P03 • Historical Evidence Lab

How historians read evidence

  1. Source: Who created it and what type is it?
  2. Detail: What does it show about industrial change?
  3. Inference: What reasonable conclusion follows?
  4. Limitation: What can this evidence not prove by itself?

Pause and Review — notebook task

Copy and answer: Why do historians pair a claim with a limitation?

Model: A photograph can support a claim about visible factory or tenement conditions at a selected place and moment, but it cannot show how common the conditions were or what happened outside the frame.

Additional evidence to notice

  • Labor platform: goals and grievances; limitation—advocates workers’ interests.
  • Immigrant letter/oral account: work, community, discrimination; limitation—individual experience.
  • Industrial map: links rail lines, resources, cities, and industry; limitation—map categories simplify.
  • Secondary interpretation: connects developments; limitation—historians may emphasize different consequences.

Notebook Summary — copy this

Evidence becomes useful when historians identify the source, extract a specific detail, make a limited inference, and explain what the source cannot establish alone. Comparing business, worker, labor, immigrant, visual, quantitative, and secondary sources prevents a one-sided story and strengthens cause-and-effect conclusions.
