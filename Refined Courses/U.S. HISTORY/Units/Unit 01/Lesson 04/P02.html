Part 1 — Freedom Was Actively Constructed

Freedpeople sought family reunification, education, independent churches, fair labor, land, legal rights, voting, safety, and community development. Government policy mattered, but freedom was also built by families and communities exercising agency.

Part 2 — Family, Schools, and Churches

People searched for relatives separated by slavery through letters, notices, and Bureau records. Communities created and supported schools because literacy strengthened independence and citizenship. Black churches provided religious autonomy, leadership, education, political meetings, and mutual support.

Part 3 — Bureau, Labor, and Land

The Freedmen’s Bureau supported schools, recorded labor agreements, addressed disputes, provided emergency assistance, and kept family records, but it lacked resources and power to protect everyone. Freedpeople pursued control over labor and land. Sherman’s Special Field Orders No. 15 produced temporary redistribution in limited areas; there was no permanent federal promise of “40 acres and a mule” to every freed family.

Part 4 — Voting and Officeholding

The Fifteenth Amendment supported Black male voter registration and election participation. Political participation also included meetings, organizing, campaigning, conventions, and officeholding. Hiram Revels, Blanche K. Bruce, Robert Smalls, Joseph Rainey, and many local and state officials demonstrate broader political change—not isolated symbolism.

Part 5 — Reconstruction Governments

Republican state governments depended on coalitions of African American voters and leaders, white Southern Republicans, Northern-born Republicans, and the federal Reconstruction framework. They rewrote constitutions, expanded public education, built infrastructure, and pursued reform. Political violence and intimidation limited these gains.

Charged labels such as “carpetbagger” and “scalawag” expressed partisan hostility and should not be treated as neutral categories.
