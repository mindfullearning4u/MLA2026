Unit 1 • Lesson 4

P03 — Historical Evidence Lab

Evidence Process

For each source identify: historical actor | goal or action | dimension of freedom | what changed | limitation.

Evidence A — Family and Education Records

Family-search notices, school petitions, letters, and Freedmen’s Bureau records show freedpeople locating relatives and demanding education. These records demonstrate agency because people defined their own priorities and organized action.

Worked example: Actor—freedpeople supporting a school. Goal—literacy and independence. Dimension—education. Change—schooling became a community institution. Limitation—one record cannot represent every community.

National Archives: Freedmen’s Bureau records

Pause and Review — Notebook Task 1

Explain why a family-search notice is evidence of both slavery’s disruption and freedom’s new possibilities.

Evidence B — Political Participation

Convention resolutions, petitions, newspapers, and voting testimony reveal demands for suffrage, equal law, safety, and representation. Political documents are persuasive: they reveal goals but must be contextualized.

Evidence C — African American Officeholders

Speeches and congressional records from figures such as Joseph Rainey, Robert Smalls, Hiram Revels, and Blanche K. Bruce show Black leaders debating civil rights, education, public policy, and protection.

Limitation: one leader cannot represent every Black voter or officeholder.

U.S. House: Black Americans in Congress

Pause and Review — Notebook Task 2

Complete two rows for the political-participation and officeholder sources. Include the actor, goal, dimension of freedom, change, and limitation.

Evidence D — Freedmen’s Bureau Record

A labor contract, school report, or complaint record reveals federal involvement in daily transitions from slavery. Bureau records are useful but reflect an institutional perspective and cannot prove that assistance or protection was equal everywhere.

Evidence E — Reconstruction Government Source

A state constitution or education provision can document formal institutional change such as public-school funding. Formal policy does not prove equal access or implementation.

Evidence F — Opposition

Sources opposing Reconstruction political change reveal partisan and racial resistance. They help explain limits but may include hostility, propaganda, or exaggeration.

Evidence G — Secondary Interpretation

Historians emphasize African American agency and coalition government to explain how Reconstruction changed citizenship and public institutions. A synthesis is useful but remains an interpretation checked against primary evidence.

Pause and Review — Notebook Task 3

Complete four rows: Bureau record, state-government source, opposition source, and secondary interpretation. For every row include a limitation.
