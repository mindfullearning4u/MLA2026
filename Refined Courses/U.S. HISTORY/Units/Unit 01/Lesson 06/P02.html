Part 1 — Geography and an Inhabited West

The Plains, Rockies, Southwest, Pacific Coast, rivers, grasslands, and arid regions contained distinct Indigenous nations, Mexican American communities, and established economies. Geography shaped farming, ranching, mining, routes, and conflict.

Part 2 — Migration, Land, and Railroads

Land, mining, agriculture, ranching, federal policy, and railroads pulled diverse migrants. The Homestead Act had legal conditions and increased pressure on Indigenous lands. Railroads connected migration, markets, corporations, and resource extraction; Chinese labor was essential.

Part 3 — Treaties, Reservations, and Resistance

Treaties were political agreements with sovereign nations, but compliance was uneven. Reservation policy reduced territory and autonomy. Indigenous nations used diplomacy, negotiation, law, adaptation, cultural persistence, migration, and armed resistance.

Part 4 — Bison, Dawes, and Assimilation

Market hunting, railroads, settlement, and resource use transformed environments and harmed many Plains communities. The 1887 Dawes Act divided many collectively held lands, promoted assimilation, opened surplus land to non-Indigenous ownership, and contributed to land loss. Federal policy did not erase sovereignty or cultural continuity.
