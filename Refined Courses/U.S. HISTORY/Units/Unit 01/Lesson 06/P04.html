Unit 1 • Lesson 6

P04 — Historical Thinking: Geographic Reasoning

Seven-Step Process

  1. Identify location.
  2. Identify landforms, water, climate, resources, and transportation corridors.
  3. Identify who or what moved.
  4. Track territorial change.
  5. Identify economic incentives.
  6. Explain effects on migration, conflict, and policy.
  7. Use map evidence in a historical claim.

Worked Railroad Model

LocationPatternMovementConsequence
Cross-continent corridorsSettlement and markets clustered along routesWorkers, settlers, goods, and military forcesGrowth increased pressure on Indigenous homelands and resources

Reasoning: Geography was not background. Routes, land, resources, and distance directly shaped migration, markets, settlement, and conflict.

Pause and Review — Notebook Task 1

Complete a map organizer: location, geographic characteristics, movement, territorial change, incentives, consequences, and one map-based claim.

Misconception Check

  • The West was not empty.
  • Indigenous nations had different experiences.
  • Expansion involved policy, corporations, military power, and resources—not settlers alone.
  • Indigenous responses included diplomacy and continuity as well as warfare.
  • The Dawes Act promoted assimilation and land loss; it did not simply give land.

Notebook Summary — Geographic Reasoning

Write this supplied summary: Geographic reasoning uses location, physical characteristics, movement, territorial change, resources, routes, and spatial consequences. A strong claim cites a map pattern and explains how it affected people, policy, economy, or conflict.
