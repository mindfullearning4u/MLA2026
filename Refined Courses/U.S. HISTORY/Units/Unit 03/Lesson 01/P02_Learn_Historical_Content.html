Component 1 — From War to Recovery

1918: war ends. 1919: returning soldiers, falling military contracts, inflation, labor conflict, and production conversion create uncertainty. 1920–1921: recession and unemployment interrupt recovery. Afterward, industrial capacity, technology, construction, national markets, and consumer demand support growth.

Reasoning: prosperity was a process, not an instant result of peace.

Component 2 — The Growth Engine
Technology + organization → productivity → mass production → lower unit costs and greater supply → consumer access → demand and business growth
Credit and advertising amplified consumption.

Productivity means more output per worker or hour—not simply harder work. Ford did not invent the automobile or the assembly-line idea; Ford refined moving assembly-line production at large scale. Automobile growth also increased demand for steel, rubber, glass, oil, roads, construction, service stations, and tourism.

Component 3 — Consumption, Credit, and Risk

Radios, automobiles, refrigerators, washing machines, and other goods became more available, but ownership was not universal. Installment buying expanded access while creating future payment obligations. Advertising used status, convenience, and emotion to stimulate demand. Electrification expanded unevenly; many rural communities lagged behind urban areas.

Component 4 — Uneven Prosperity

Industrial growth existed alongside agricultural distress. Wartime farm expansion was followed by weaker foreign demand, falling crop prices, debt, and overproduction in some sectors. Workers, women, African Americans, farmers, owners, and urban/rural communities experienced different opportunities and barriers. Great Migration employment opportunities existed alongside discrimination, occupational segregation, and unequal wages.

Page Summary — Copy into Notebook

The 1920s boom grew from interacting causes: recovery from postwar disruption, productivity, mass production, automobile-linked industries, credit, advertising, electrification, and national markets. Cause-and-effect reasoning shows that no single factor created growth. Comparison and quantitative evidence reveal uneven prosperity: industrial expansion and consumer opportunity coexisted with farm distress, debt, discrimination, and unequal regional access.
