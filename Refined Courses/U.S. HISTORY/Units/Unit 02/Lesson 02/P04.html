P04 • Historical Thinking: Comparison

Labor Movements, Farmers, Populism, and Economic Reform

The comparison method

  1. Identify organized labor and farmer/Populist reform.
  2. Establish shared context: industrial and market transformation.
  3. Use identical categories.
  4. Identify similarities.
  5. Identify differences.
  6. Explain why differences existed.
  7. Determine significance.
  8. Support each judgment with evidence.
Same category Organized labor — model Farmer/Populist reform — model
Constituency Wage workers; organizations varied by skill and inclusion Farmers and coalition aspirations; varied by region, race, crop, ownership
Problem Wages, hours, safety, authority, security Debt, prices, railroad rates, credit, currency
Solution/organization Unions, bargaining, strikes, mutual support Cooperatives, alliances, regulation, party politics
Government relationship Often sought protection but faced courts or intervention Sought regulation and political/economic policy changes
Outcome Uneven short-term victories; durable union tradition Electoral defeat in 1896; several proposals influenced later reform

Worked comparison

Industrial workers and farmers both responded to conditions they believed limited bargaining power, but workers focused on employment terms while farmers focused on market, credit, transportation, and currency. Their positions in the economy explain the difference. Both used organization and sought influence over power, yet their strategies ranged from workplace bargaining to national party politics.

Correct misconceptions: no single union represented all workers; not all farmers supported Populism; Populism was not simply socialism; free silver addressed money supply, prices, and debt.

Pause and Review — notebook

Why must the same categories be used? Model: Identical categories prevent a false comparison—for example, comparing labor membership with Populist election results. The method tests equivalent features before reaching a judgment.

Historical Thinking Summary — copy

Comparison is a controlled reasoning process. It holds categories constant, connects similarities and differences to context, explains why they existed, and uses evidence to judge significance without treating either group as uniform.
