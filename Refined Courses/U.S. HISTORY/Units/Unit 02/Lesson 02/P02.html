Pressure: wages, long hours, dangerous work, employer authority, and job insecurity varied across industries and workers.

Organization: unions used collective bargaining, strikes, mutual aid, and political pressure. Knights of Labor organized broadly and pursued reform; the AFL under Samuel Gompers federated craft unions and focused on wages, hours, and conditions.

Pause and Review — notebook

Why must a strike analysis include government and public response? Model: A strike was not only a worker-employer dispute. Courts, officials, police or troops, newspapers, and public opinion could change bargaining power and the outcome.

Notebook Summary 1 — copy

Labor organization developed because individual workers often had limited leverage. The Knights and AFL differed in membership, structure, and goals, and labor conflicts show that employer resistance and government intervention shaped results.

Part 3 — Farmers and Populism

Commercial farmers depended on markets, credit, crop prices, and rail transportation. Conditions varied by region, crop, race, landownership, and farm size; not all farmers were poor or Populist.

The Grange promoted cooperation and railroad regulation. Farmers’ Alliances expanded cooperative and political organizing; Black farmers also organized, including the Colored Farmers’ Alliance, amid discrimination and incomplete biracial unity.

The People’s Party translated grievances into the Omaha Platform: railroad/public-control arguments, currency reform, graduated income tax, direct election of senators, and political reform.

Free silver: supporters expected a larger money supply and inflationary pressure that could raise prices and ease debts. It was a monetary-policy argument—not “free money.” Bryan’s 1896 campaign connected this rhetoric to Democratic-Populist overlap; McKinley’s victory limited the Populist presidential strategy while several reform ideas survived.

Pause and Review — notebook

Why did worker and farmer demands differ? Model: Workers negotiated mainly within wage employment; farmers sold crops and depended on credit, prices, and transportation. Their economic positions produced different immediate reforms despite shared concern about concentrated power.

Notebook Summary 2 — copy

Farm organizations moved from education and cooperation toward political reform. Populism was not socialism: it combined agrarian, political, currency, tax, and regulatory proposals. Its 1896 defeat did not erase its longer-term influence.
