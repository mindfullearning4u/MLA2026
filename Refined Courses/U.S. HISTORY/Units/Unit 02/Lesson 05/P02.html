Part 2 — Why Panama?

The narrow isthmus offered a shorter Atlantic–Pacific connection than sailing around South America. Trade, shipping time, naval transfer, security, and influence reinforced one another. The earlier French effort failed amid engineering, disease, and financial problems.

Panama was part of Colombia. Separatist sentiment existed before 1903, while U.S. naval support helped prevent Colombian suppression. The Hay–Bunau-Varilla Treaty—negotiated without a Panamanian representative—gave the United States extensive Canal Zone rights.

Part 3 — Construction, labor, and diplomacy

U.S. construction ran 1904–1914. Locks, excavation at Culebra/Gaillard Cut, terrain, climate, malaria, and yellow fever were major obstacles. Public-health measures reduced mosquito-borne disease. A large multinational workforce—especially West Indian/Caribbean laborers—worked within racially stratified pay and status systems.

President Policy Main tool Continuity/limit
Roosevelt Big Stick; Roosevelt Corollary Negotiation backed by force and intervention claims Expanded an earlier Monroe Doctrine; Latin American critics saw domination.

Pause and Review — notebook

What continuity crosses all three presidents? Model: Each used a different public label, yet each sought strategic influence and accepted interference in regional affairs.

Notebook Summary 2 — copy

The canal opened in 1914 and increased commercial and naval mobility. Its construction depended on diverse labor, while regional diplomacy increased U.S. intervention capacity and Latin American sovereignty concerns.
