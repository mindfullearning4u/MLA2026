P04 • Historical Thinking: Geographic Reasoning

Panama, Roosevelt, and an Expanding U.S. Global Role

Seven-step process

  1. Identify the strategic location.
  2. Identify the distance or movement problem.
  3. Trace economic and military routes.
  4. Explain why location mattered to policy.
  5. Identify political control required to use it.
  6. Analyze consequences for surrounding regions.
  7. Use map evidence in a historical claim.
Step Worked Panama example
Location Narrow isthmus between Caribbean/Atlantic and Pacific waters.
Movement problem Ships otherwise traveled around South America.
Routes Canal shortened commercial and naval movement.
Policy/control U.S. officials pursued treaty rights and Canal Zone control.
Consequences Greater U.S. power alongside regional sovereignty conflict.
Correct misconceptions: the canal was not only for convenience; Panamanian separatism predated 1903; the Corollary was not identical to the Monroe Doctrine; intervention was not universally welcomed; Caribbean labor was essential.

Pause and Review — notebook

How does a map become causal evidence? Model: The map shows spatial advantage; historians then connect that advantage to policy choices and corroborate the link with diplomatic evidence.

Historical Thinking Summary — copy

Geographic reasoning explains how location, distance, routes, control, and regional effects shaped policy. Geography creates constraints and opportunities; people and institutions make decisions within them.
