Progressive Reform and the Expansion of Democracy

Part 1 — Vocabulary: copy every term and definition

Term Definition
Progressivism Broad movement addressing political, economic, and social problems tied to industrialization, urbanization, and concentrated power.
Progressive Person or group supporting political, social, or economic reform.
Muckraker Journalist or writer investigating corruption, unsafe conditions, or social problems.
Settlement house Urban community institution providing services, education, and reform support.
Regulation Government rules or oversight influencing economic or social activity.
Antitrust Policy limiting unlawful monopoly power or anticompetitive practices.
Initiative Citizen proposal of a law or policy for public vote.
Referendum Voter approval or rejection of a law or policy.
Recall Voter removal of an official before a term ends.

Part 2 — Problems, methods, and reformers

Problem Methods and examples Important limit
Urban poverty/services Riis exposed framed evidence; Addams/Hull House provided education, childcare, immigrant support, research, and advocacy. Photographs select a scene; immigrant communities were active agents, not passive recipients.
Unsafe work/child labor Investigation, organizing, and state safety/hours/compensation laws; Triangle fire as a representative catalyst. No single tragedy or reformer alone caused legislation; enforcement varied.
Corporate power Tarbell’s Standard Oil investigation; antitrust enforcement and regulatory expansion. Reform targeted abuses and competition, not elimination of every large corporation.
Unsafe products Sinclair, public reaction, Pure Food and Drug Act, Meat Inspection Act. Sinclair’s broader labor/capitalism purpose differed from the food-safety focus of public response.

Pause and Review — notebook

Why should we avoid saying a muckraker “passed” a law? Model: Exposure could shape opinion, but activism, elections, officials, legislative negotiation, enforcement, and other evidence connected publicity to policy.

Notebook Summary 1 — copy

Progressive methods linked investigation and organization to public policy. Evidence of exposure must be separated from evidence of legislative action and enforcement.

Part 3 — Political participation and civil rights

Political process: civil service, direct primary, initiative, referendum, recall, and city reforms sought accountability; the Seventeenth Amendment created direct senatorial election.

Women’s suffrage: a long movement included NAWSA, state campaigns, the National Woman’s Party, congressional passage in 1919, and Nineteenth Amendment ratification in 1920. Constitutional change did not remove racial or other practical voting barriers.

African American activism: Ida B. Wells, W.E.B. Du Bois, Booker T. Washington, and the NAACP pursued different educational, economic, legal, political, and anti-lynching strategies. Avoid caricaturing their differences.

Presidents: evaluate selected policies of Roosevelt, Taft, and Wilson—not biography. Some Progressives fought exclusion, while others accepted or reinforced segregation, disenfranchisement, nativism, or unequal class and gender systems.

Pause and Review — notebook

How can a reform be significant and limited? Model: The Nineteenth Amendment changed constitutional voting protections nationwide, yet discriminatory barriers meant access remained unequal. Significance measures both scale of change and who was excluded.

Notebook Summary 2 — copy

Political and constitutional reforms expanded formal participation and accountability unevenly. A complete account includes women’s suffrage and African American activism while confronting continuing racial, gender, ethnic, and class exclusions.
