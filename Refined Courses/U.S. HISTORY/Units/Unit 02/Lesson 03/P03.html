P03 • Historical Evidence Lab

Progressive Reform and the Expansion of Democracy

Source routine

  1. Name creator, context, audience, and purpose.
  2. Identify the reform problem and proposed solution.
  3. Cite one exact detail showing change.
  4. Compare a competing or limiting perspective.
  5. State what the source cannot prove alone.
Evidence Problem / actor / solution Worked usefulness and limitation
A. Muckraker excerpt Riis, Sinclair, Tarbell, or Steffens exposes a problem to influence opinion. Useful for rhetoric and selected evidence; advocacy and selection limit total representation.
B. Reform law Government defines regulation or protection. Proves policy intent and authority, not complete enforcement or equal effect.
C. Suffrage source Advocates voting rights through speech, petition, testimony, or organization. Shows movement claim and strategy; one source cannot represent the entire movement.
D. Anti-suffrage source Opposes change, often using gender assumptions. Reveals opposition and rhetoric; compare factual claims with other evidence.
E. African American reform source Wells, Du Bois, Washington, or NAACP addresses racial violence/discrimination and rights. Centers activism often excluded from simplified Progressivism; strategies differed.
F. Presidential/policy source Official promotes a reform approach. Rhetoric may emphasize political goals; corroborate with law and implementation.
G. Photograph/cartoon Frames a workplace, tenement, or political argument. Visible details are evidence, but selection, staging, caption, and audience matter.
H. Secondary interpretation Historian judges achievements and limits. Compare its criteria and evidence with primary sources; interpretations may disagree.

Pause and Review — notebook

Compare B and F. Why are both needed? Model: A speech shows a leader’s public reasoning; a statute shows enacted authority. Neither alone proves enforcement or social effect, so implementation and affected-group evidence are also needed.

Evidence Lab Summary — copy

Reform evidence must connect advocacy, opposition, law, implementation, affected groups, visuals, and interpretation. Corroboration prevents publicity or policy intent from being mistaken for complete change.
