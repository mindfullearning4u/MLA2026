P04 • Historical Thinking: Cause and Effect

U.S. Imperialism and the Spanish-American War

Seven-step process

  1. Identify the outcome: war and overseas expansion.
  2. Separate long-term conditions from triggers.
  3. Categorize economic, strategic, ideological, political, and humanitarian/public-opinion causes.
  4. Identify trigger events.
  5. Analyze territorial, political, military, constitutional, and international consequences.
  6. Evaluate relative significance and interaction.
  7. Support every link with evidence.
Category Worked Maine example
Long-term conditions Cuban rebellion, interests, naval strategy, expansionist belief, public concern.
Trigger Maine explosion intensified public/political pressure; exact cause remained disputed.
Decision Political and diplomatic choices converted crisis into war.
Consequences Territorial acquisition/control, global role, Filipino resistance, sovereignty/citizenship debate.
Correct misconceptions: yellow journalism was not the sole cause; Spanish responsibility for the Maine was not proven; U.S. influence in Cuba continued; many Filipinos resisted; imperialism faced organized opposition.

Pause and Review — notebook

Why must causes be ranked and connected? Model: A list does not explain mechanism. Ranking and interaction show which condition carried the most weight and how factors made one another more powerful.

Historical Thinking Summary — copy

Cause/effect analysis distinguishes underlying conditions, catalysts, decisions, and consequences; categorizes motives; tests relative weight; and avoids mistaking temporal sequence for proof.
