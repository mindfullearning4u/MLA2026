P03 • Historical Evidence Lab

U.S. Imperialism and the Spanish-American War

Evidence routine

  1. Source creator, context, audience, and goal.
  2. Identify position and claimed cause/consequence.
  3. Connect geographic or sovereignty issue.
  4. Compare opposing/local perspective.
  5. Name limitation.
Evidence Actor/goal and worked use Limitation
A. Expansionist source Mahan/politician advocates strategic or economic expansion. Policy advocacy does not prove motive or consensus by itself.
B. Newspaper source Headline/cartoon shapes audience reaction through rhetoric. Sensationalism and competition can distort; not sole cause.
C. Cuban source Martí/independence voice asserts Cuban goals and agency. Does not alone establish U.S. motives or all Cuban views.
D. Treaty/government Establishes formal war decision or territorial policy. Does not show every local experience or implementation.
E. Filipino source Aguinaldo/independence statement asserts self-determination. Political advocacy; compare U.S. and other Filipino evidence.
F. Anti-imperialist Challenges annexation through consent, constitutional, cost, or domination arguments. Opponents had different—and sometimes conflicting—motives.
G. Political cartoon Symbols/labels communicate a claim about power and empire. Exaggeration and symbolism require contextual decoding.
H. Historian Explains why 1898 changed global involvement. Historians weigh economic, strategic, ideological, and humanitarian motives differently.

Pause and Review — notebook

Why pair D with C or E? Model: Government documents establish formal policy; Cuban or Filipino sources reveal how affected peoples understood sovereignty and self-determination.

Evidence Lab Summary — copy

A U.S.-only evidence set hides local agency. Corroborated causation uses policy, advocacy, journalism, geographic evidence, affected peoples, opposition, and later interpretation.
