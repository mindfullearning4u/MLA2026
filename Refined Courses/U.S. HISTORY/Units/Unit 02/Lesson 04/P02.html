U.S. Imperialism and the Spanish-American War

Part 1 — Vocabulary: copy every term and definition

Term Definition
Imperialism Extension of political, military, or economic control or influence over other territories or peoples.
Expansionism Support for extending national territory or influence.
Annexation Formal addition of territory to a country.
Yellow journalism Sensationalized or exaggerated reporting intended to attract and influence readers.
Spanish-American War 1898 U.S.–Spain war fought mainly in the Caribbean and Pacific.
USS Maine U.S. battleship whose Havana Harbor explosion increased pressure for war.
Treaty of Paris 1898 treaty ending war and transferring former Spanish territories to U.S. control.
Protectorate Country/territory retaining formal government while a stronger power exercises major control.
Platt Amendment U.S.-imposed conditions limiting Cuban sovereignty and permitting intervention in specified circumstances.
Sovereignty Authority of a nation or people to govern itself.
Anti-Imperialist League Organization opposing U.S. overseas imperial expansion.
Philippine-American War Conflict between U.S. forces and Filipino independence fighters after U.S. acquisition.
Sea power Naval forces and maritime infrastructure used to protect or project influence.
Colony Territory governed or controlled by another country.
Self-determination Principle that people should determine their political status and government.

Cause category Instruction and example Limit

Pause and Review — notebook

Why is overseas expansion multi-causal? Model: Markets explain opportunity, strategy explains geography, ideology supplied justification, and politics shaped decisions; no category alone accounts for timing and territorial choices.

Notebook Summary 1 — copy

U.S. expansion rested on older industrial, naval, ideological, and political conditions. Geographic evidence links bases and routes to strategy, while Hawaiian evidence requires Native political perspective and contested sovereignty.

Part 3 — Cuba, war, and consequences

Cuba: Cuban independence activism and Spanish colonial conflict had their own agency; U.S. interests and humanitarian concern interacted with sensational reporting. The February 1898 Maine explosion was a trigger amid disputed causation—not proof Spain deliberately sank it.

War: 1898 action occurred in Cuba/Caribbean and the Philippines/Pacific. Manila Bay, San Juan Heights, and Santiago show two theaters and rapid U.S. victory. Black regular troops served in Cuba despite discrimination at home.

Treaty: Spain relinquished Cuba; Puerto Rico and Guam were ceded; the Philippines transferred for payment. Cuba gained formal independence but Platt restrictions preserved U.S. influence. Territorial paths differed.

Philippines: Emilio Aguinaldo and many Filipinos expected independence and resisted U.S. rule in the Philippine-American War (1899–1902 conventional period).

Debate: expansionists cited markets, strategy, prestige, and mission; anti-imperialists cited consent, constitutional principles, costs, racism, and domination. Motives varied within each side.

Pause and Review — notebook

Why is the Maine a trigger rather than a complete cause? Model: It intensified pressure during an existing Cuban crisis and expansion debate; longer-term interests and political decisions explain why the explosion produced war pressure.

Notebook Summary 2 — copy

The war accelerated U.S. territorial and naval involvement. Its outcomes created different political statuses and sharpened the contradiction between self-government ideals and control without equal political authority.
