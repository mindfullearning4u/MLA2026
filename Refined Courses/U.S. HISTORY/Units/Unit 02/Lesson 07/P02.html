Part 2 — Mobilizing a nation

Area Federal action Effect/tension
Military Selective Service and AEF deployment Rapid force growth; unequal service conditions.
Industry War Industries Board coordination Expanded federal-economic direction.
Finance/resources Liberty Bonds, taxes, food/fuel conservation Linked civilians to the war effort.

Pause and Review — notebook

How did mobilization expand federal power? Model: National agencies coordinated people, production, finance, resources, and information on a scale beyond ordinary peacetime practice.

Notebook Summary 1 — copy

Mobilization reached far beyond combat. It increased federal coordination while raising questions about coercion, propaganda, unequal service, and civil liberties.

Part 3 — Diverse experiences and social change

Wartime industrial demand accelerated the Great Migration. African Americans pursued jobs and community change while facing segregation, violence, and military discrimination. Women expanded industrial, nursing, military-support, and reform roles without gaining equal opportunity. German Americans faced suspicion; Native, Hispanic, Asian, Jewish, immigrant, and dissenting Americans experienced different mixtures of service, opportunity, exclusion, and pressure.

Quantitative evidence can show change in production, service, migration, or employment, but numbers require context and cannot represent every experience.

Pause and Review — notebook

Why compare opportunity and restriction together? Model: Wartime change could open work or civic roles while discrimination and coercive institutions persisted.

Notebook Summary 2 — copy

The war accelerated demographic and social change without eliminating inequality. Continuity/change analysis asks what shifted, what persisted, for whom, and with what lasting effect.

Part 4 — Armistice, treaty, and League debate

The November 1918 armistice stopped fighting; it was not the final treaty. Wilson's Fourteen Points emphasized principles including a League of Nations. The Treaty of Versailles reflected Allied compromises and differed from Wilson's program.

Position Reasoning
Wilson Supported the treaty and League as a collective-security system.
Reservationists, including Lodge Wanted changes protecting congressional authority and U.S. sovereignty.
Irreconcilables Opposed membership more fundamentally.

The Constitution gives the president a negotiating role and requires two-thirds Senate consent. Partisanship, reservations, sovereignty concerns, Article X, and Wilson's unwillingness to accept key changes contributed to failure. The U.S. did not join the League.

Pause and Review — notebook

Why is “Wilson versus isolationists” too simple? Model: Senators held different positions, including conditional support, and disputed constitutional authority, commitments, sovereignty, and party politics.

Notebook Summary 3 — copy

Treaty failure resulted from interacting constitutional, diplomatic, partisan, sovereignty, and leadership conflicts—not one person or one motive.
