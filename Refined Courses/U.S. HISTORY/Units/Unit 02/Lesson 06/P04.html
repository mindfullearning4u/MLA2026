U.S. HISTORY • UNIT 2 • LESSON 6

P04 • Historical Thinking: Cause and Effect

World War I: Causes, Neutrality, and U.S. Entry

Eight-step process

  1. Identify the outcome: April 1917 declaration.
  2. Identify long-term conditions.
  3. Identify intermediate developments.
  4. Identify immediate triggers.
  5. Categorize military/security, diplomatic, economic, political/ideological, and public-opinion factors.
  6. Explain interaction.
  7. Rank significance.
  8. Support conclusions with evidence.

Worked example

The Lusitania crisis increased tension, but the United States did not enter in 1915. A stronger explanation includes renewed unrestricted submarine warfare and the Zimmermann Telegram in 1917, while economic ties and neutral shipping shaped U.S. interests. Entry accumulated through several interacting factors.

Correct misconceptions: Sarajevo alone did not cause the European war; alliances did not act mechanically; Lusitania did not cause immediate U.S. entry; the Telegram was not Mexican acceptance; economics was not the sole cause.

Pause and Review — notebook

Why must a causal explanation use chronology? Model: Timing tests explanatory power: if an event occurred in 1915 but entry came in 1917, later developments must explain the policy shift.

Historical Thinking Summary — copy

Cause/effect reasoning separates conditions, developments, triggers, and decisions; compares categories; explains interaction; and uses chronology to reject weak single-cause claims.
