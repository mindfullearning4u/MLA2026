World War I: Causes, Neutrality, and U.S. Entry

Part 1 — Vocabulary: copy every term and definition

Term Definition
World War I Global conflict beginning in Europe in 1914; the United States entered in 1917.
Neutrality Policy of not formally joining a war between other countries.
Militarism Strong emphasis on military power and preparedness.
Nationalism Strong identification with a nation or national group.
Alliance Agreement among nations to cooperate for strategic purposes.
Central Powers Coalition centered on Germany and Austria-Hungary.
Allied Powers Coalition including Britain, France, Russia, and others.
Unrestricted submarine warfare Submarine attacks in designated war zones without traditional warning.
U-boat German submarine.
Lusitania British passenger liner sunk by a German submarine in 1915.
Preparedness Movement advocating greater U.S. military readiness.
Sussex Pledge 1916 German commitment to restrict submarine attacks.
Zimmermann Telegram 1917 German proposal for cooperation with Mexico if the U.S. entered war.
Intervention Direct involvement beyond a country's borders.
Neutral rights Rights claimed by nations not participating in war, including shipping rights.
Associated Power U.S. wartime relationship with the Allies without formal membership in their alliance.

Part 2 — Why war began in Europe

Nationalism, imperial competition, militarism, alliances, and Balkan tensions created long-term instability. The assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand triggered the July Crisis, but leaders still made choices; neither the assassination nor alliances mechanically caused war.

Geography and alliance patterns helped turn a regional crisis into a wider conflict.

Part 3 — Neutrality to intervention

Date Development Why it mattered
1914 Wilson declares neutrality; divided public opinion Distance, tradition, diversity, and interests made nonentry viable.
1915 Lusitania sinking Raised submarine and neutral-rights conflict; did not cause immediate entry.
1916 Sussex Pledge; preparedness debate; election Temporarily eased crisis while readiness debate continued.
Early 1917 Renewed unrestricted submarine warfare Threatened U.S. shipping and neutral rights.
1917 Zimmermann Telegram German proposal alarmed Americans; Mexico did not accept it.
April 1917 Wilson war message and congressional declaration U.S. entered as an Associated Power.

Trade and finance increasingly connected the U.S. to the Allies, while the British blockade shaped commerce and information. Economic ties mattered but were not the sole cause.

Pause and Review — notebook

Why did policy change in 1917 rather than 1915? Model: Renewed submarine warfare and the Telegram altered the immediate security/diplomatic context after two years of accumulated disputes and interests.

Notebook Summary 2 — copy

Entry resulted from a chronology of military, diplomatic, economic, political, and public-opinion pressures. A strong explanation distinguishes earlier tensions from 1917 triggers.
