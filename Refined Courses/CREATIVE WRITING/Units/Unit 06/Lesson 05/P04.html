P04 Worked Example

Study each example. Notice that the formatting choice is matched to the genre and explained through reader effect.

Worked Example 1: Poem Formatting

Step-by-step explanation: Follow each modeled step carefully and notice how the thinking moves from the task to evidence, reasoning, and a clear response.

Draft problem: The poem has a title, but every line is crowded together in one paragraph.

Step 1: Identify the genre. This is a poem, so line breaks and stanza breaks are part of meaning.

Step 2: Read for natural pauses, images, and turns. The student notices three image groups: the room, the rain, and the decision.

Step 3: Format the poem with line breaks that slow the reader at important images and stanza breaks that separate each image group.

Final explanation: I separated the poem into three stanzas so the reader can move from setting to feeling to decision. The line breaks create pauses around the strongest images.

Worked Example 2: Story Formatting

Step-by-step explanation: Follow each modeled step carefully and notice how the thinking moves from the task to evidence, reasoning, and a clear response.

Draft problem: A short story scene is one long block, so the reader cannot track action and dialogue.

Step 1: Identify the genre. This is prose fiction, so paragraph breaks guide action, dialogue, and pacing.

Step 2: Mark where the speaker changes and where the action shifts.

Step 3: Start a new paragraph when a new speaker talks and when the scene moves to a new beat.

Final explanation: I revised the paragraphing so each speaker and action beat is clear. This helps the reader follow the scene without rereading.

Worked Example 3: Script Formatting

Step-by-step explanation: Follow each modeled step carefully and notice how the thinking moves from the task to evidence, reasoning, and a clear response.

Draft problem: A dramatic scene has dialogue, but the character names and stage directions are inconsistent.

Step 1: Identify the genre. This is a script, so the reader needs to know who speaks and what action happens.

Step 2: Put character labels in a consistent style before each line of dialogue.

Step 3: Put stage directions in a consistent format so they do not look like spoken dialogue.

Final explanation: I made the character labels consistent and separated stage directions from dialogue. This makes the scene easier to read and perform.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: "I changed the font because it looked cool."

Why this is incorrect: This explanation does not connect the format to the genre, audience, readability, or meaning. Publication formatting must be purposeful.

Correct Version

Correct: "I used consistent paragraph breaks and dialogue spacing so the reader can follow the scene without confusion."

Teachable Explanation: This explanation names a specific formatting choice and explains how it supports the reader.
