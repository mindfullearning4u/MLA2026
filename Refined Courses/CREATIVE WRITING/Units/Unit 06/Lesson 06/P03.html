P03 Notebook Task - Part 2

Continue your notebook notes. This page teaches presentation sequence, speaking preparation, and a common mistake to avoid.

Step 4: Organize the Presentation Sequence

A clear presentation follows a predictable path. The audience should never wonder why you are reading a section or what they should listen for.

  1. Introduce the title and genre. Tell the audience what kind of piece it is.
  2. Give brief context. Explain only what the audience needs before the excerpt.
  3. Name the listening focus. Tell the audience what craft choice to notice.
  4. Read or present the excerpt. Use pacing that fits the piece.
  5. Explain craft reasoning. Connect your choices to effect.
  6. Reflect on revision or growth. Explain one change that improved the work.

Step 5: Prepare Speaking Notes

Speaking notes are not a full essay. They help you remember the sequence without reading every word in a flat voice. Write notes in short phrases so you can look up at the audience.

  • Use key words instead of long paragraphs.
  • Mark where you will pause before the excerpt.
  • Underline the craft terms you must say clearly.
  • Practice once for timing and once for clarity.

If you lose your place, return to the sequence: title, context, excerpt, craft choice, reader effect, revision growth.

Common Mistake: Only Summarizing

Incorrect: "My story is about a person who moves to a new town. Then she meets someone. Then they argue. Then she learns a lesson."

Why this is incorrect: This tells what happens, but it does not explain the author's choices. The audience does not learn how the writer used voice, pacing, dialogue, imagery, structure, or revision.

Correct Approach

Correct: "My story follows a character entering a new town, but I want the audience to listen for the dialogue. I revised the conversation so the character avoids direct answers, which shows discomfort without naming it. That choice helps the reader infer tension."

Why this is correct: This gives enough context, names a craft choice, explains a revision, and connects the choice to reader effect.

Teachable Explanation: The correct example works because it applies the lesson skill deliberately and explains the reasoning instead of only naming an answer.
