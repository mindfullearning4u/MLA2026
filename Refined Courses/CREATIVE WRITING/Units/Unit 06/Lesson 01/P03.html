Lesson: Select with Evidence, Explain with Precision

Step 1 — Define the Portfolio Purpose

Name what the portfolio must demonstrate: strongest current craft, growth across time, range, readiness for publication, or a combination. Selection criteria must follow that purpose.

Step 2 — Build an Evidence Matrix

CriterionEvidenceQuestion
Craft controlDiction, imagery, syntax, pacing, formWhere is the choice sustained?
GrowthBefore/after passage and feedbackWhat problem did revision solve?
RangeDifferent risks or techniquesWhat does this add that others do not?

Pause and Choose: Select by Criterion

A writer loves a personal poem, but another piece shows stronger craft control and a documented revision breakthrough. The portfolio’s main purpose is to demonstrate growth. Which choice is better supported?

A. Select the favorite poem because emotional attachment is enough evidence.
Weaker choice. Personal value can matter, but it does not demonstrate the stated criterion. The decision lacks observable evidence of growth.
B. Select the revised piece and document the problem, the before-and-after change, and the improvement it produced.
Stronger choice. This selection follows the portfolio purpose and gives specific evidence that an evaluator can examine.

Retry: If your first choice is weaker, close it and select the other choice before continuing. Compare both explanations so you can name why the stronger answer works.

Step 3 — Separate Preference from Judgment

Preference: “This is my favorite because it is personal.” Judgment: “This belongs because its recurring harbor image develops the speaker’s divided loyalty, and revision made that pattern structurally coherent.” Personal meaning may matter, but it is not sufficient evidence.

Step 4 — Write the Rationale

  1. Claim: name why the piece belongs.
  2. Craft evidence: quote or describe an exact decision.
  3. Revision evidence: compare a meaningful before-and-after change.
  4. Effect: explain what readers can perceive or experience.
  5. Portfolio contribution: show what the work adds to the collection.
  6. Limitation: acknowledge one remaining weakness or tradeoff.

Pause and Choose: Make the Rationale Specific

Which rationale is unmistakably tied to one artifact?

A. “This story belongs because moving the unopened letter from the ending to the first scene created a recurring choice that now drives the narrator’s conflict.”
Stronger choice. It identifies an exact revision, a structural function, and its effect on conflict. The claim could not simply be pasted onto any piece.
B. “This is one of my best pieces because I worked hard and improved it.”
Weaker choice. Effort and improvement are asserted but not demonstrated. The statement lacks a named decision, evidence, and reader effect.

Retry: If your first choice is weaker, close it and select the other choice before continuing. Compare both explanations so you can name why the stronger answer works.

Quality Test

Remove the title and ask whether the rationale could describe any piece. If yes, it is too vague. Add exact evidence until the reasoning is unmistakably tied to this artifact.
