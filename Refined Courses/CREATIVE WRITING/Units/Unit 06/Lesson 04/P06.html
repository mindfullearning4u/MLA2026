Purpose

Show that you can understand the lesson concept, apply it in original work, and explain your decisions. Complete every numbered item and submit your responses with your Checkpoint work.

New Source: The Decorative Line

A poem about grief repeatedly uses ocean imagery, but one stanza suddenly compares memory to fireworks, machinery, and a locked drawer. Each comparison is vivid alone; together they compete with the poem’s controlling pattern and voice.

A controlling image is a central image or pattern that organizes a poem. Style coherence occurs when diction, imagery, syntax, sound, and structure create a purposeful overall effect.

Part 1: Analyze the Source

  1. Why can individually strong images weaken a poem when they do not work together?
  2. Propose a revision that preserves the meaning of the conflicting stanza while bringing it into the poem’s image system.

Use this response pattern: Make a precise claim, cite the relevant detail, and explain how the detail proves the claim.

Part 2: Choose A or B

3. Which choice demonstrates disciplined poetic revision?

A. Keep every vivid comparison because removing creative language makes poetry less original.
B. Evaluate each image by its contribution to the poem’s controlling pattern, cutting or transforming even attractive lines when necessary.

Record A or B in your submission and explain your choice in 2–3 sentences. The answer is not revealed here because this item is part of your graded written work.

Part 3: Create and Apply

4. Complete a substantial revision of one original 24–40 line poem or short sequence.

  1. Diagnose: Name the intended effect, controlling image, and one weakness in structure or style.
  2. Reshape: Revise lineation, stanza movement, turn, and ending so the poem develops rather than merely accumulates.
  3. Refine: Strengthen diction, connotation, figurative pattern, sound, and purposeful ambiguity.
  4. Cut: Remove at least one line or image that is attractive but unnecessary.
  5. Reflection: Present three before-and-after examples and explain the effect of each revision.

Plan before drafting: Decide what the audience should understand, how each section will build that understanding, and which details will do the most work.

Part 4: Revise With Purpose

5. Read the poem aloud twice. Revise one sound or rhythm problem and one image that explains too much; justify both decisions.

Do more than correct spelling. Revise organization, meaning, evidence, or craft so the work becomes clearer and more effective.
