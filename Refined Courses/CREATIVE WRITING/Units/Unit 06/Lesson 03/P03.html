Lesson: Diagnose and Revise the Narrative System

Step 1 — Translate Feedback into a Diagnosis

Reader symptomPossible causeQuestion
“I lost interest.”Unclear desire, repetitive obstacle, delayed consequenceWhere did attention fall, and what changed there?
“The ending came from nowhere.”Missing setup or causal linkWhich earlier choice should prepare this result?
“I do not know who is speaking.”Uncontrolled focalization or weak cuesWhose knowledge and language govern the scene?

Step 2 — Map Desire to Consequence

For every major scene, complete: Character wants ___, but ___, so the character chooses ___, causing ___. “And then” signals chronology; “therefore” or “but” signals causality.

Pause and Choose: Strengthen Narrative Causality

Which scene plan creates a causal chain rather than a list of events?

A. “Mara misses the bus, and then it rains, and then she finds a key, and then she enters the house.”
Weaker choice. Events occur in sequence, but the plan does not show how desire, obstacle, and choice produce consequences.
B. “Mara needs shelter before the storm, but the station closes, so she uses the key she promised not to touch, causing the locked house to become her only option.”
Stronger choice. Desire meets an obstacle, the character chooses under pressure, and that choice creates the next problem. The plot develops through causality.

Retry: If your first choice is weaker, close it and select the other choice before continuing. Compare both explanations so you can name why the stronger answer works.

Step 3 — Control Point of View

  • Choose what the focal character can perceive and know.
  • Match diction and comparisons to that consciousness.
  • Signal intentional shifts through scene breaks, headings, or unmistakable voice.
  • Remove convenient knowledge inserted only to solve the plot.

Step 4 — Revise Pacing by Importance

Use scene for consequential choices and discoveries. Use summary for routine travel, repeated attempts, or lower-impact time. Expand the moment readers must interpret; compress what they only need to know.

Pause and Choose: Earn the Ending

A protagonist suddenly solves the central conflict with an ability never mentioned earlier. What is the strongest revision?

A. Replace the convenient solution with a choice shaped by earlier desire and consequences, or plant the ability and its cost well before the ending.
Stronger choice. This makes the outcome retrospectively logical and keeps character action central. Preparation supports surprise without announcing it.
B. Keep the sudden ability and add an explanation after the conflict is solved.
Weaker choice. A late explanation does not create prior causality. The solution still arrives from outside the narrative system rather than from established choices and consequences.

Retry: If your first choice is weaker, close it and select the other choice before continuing. Compare both explanations so you can name why the stronger answer works.

Step 5 — Earn the Ending

  1. List the ending’s decisive action or realization.
  2. Trace which earlier desires, images, promises, and choices prepare it.
  3. Add preparation without announcing the answer.
  4. Remove coincidences or late devices that replace character choice.
  5. Test whether the ending is both surprising and retrospectively logical.

Revision Order

Motivation → causality → scene order → point of view → pacing → sentence craft → conventions. Do not polish a scene that the structural revision may remove.
