Checkpoint: Comparative Universal Themes

Put the lesson together through analysis, decision-making, writing, and purposeful revision.

Purpose

Show that you can understand the lesson concept, apply it in original writing, and explain your craft decisions. Complete every numbered item and submit your responses with your Checkpoint work.

New Source: Two Ways to Tell the Truth

Scene A: After missing the final shot, Imani tells her teammates that she ignored the coach’s signal. She expects anger, but the captain asks what the team can learn before tomorrow.

Scene B: During a concert, Luis hears his instrument slip out of tune. Afterward, he could blame the old strings, but he tells the director he skipped the tuning check and offers to arrive early for rehearsal.

To compare means to examine meaningful similarities and differences. A thematic treatment is the distinct way a text develops a shared theme through character, conflict, setting, and consequence.

Part 1: Analyze the Source

  1. State one universal theme shared by both scenes. Use one detail from each scene to support your answer.
  2. How does each setting change the way the shared theme is developed? Explain a meaningful difference, not merely a surface detail.

Use this response pattern: Make a precise claim, cite the relevant detail, and explain how the detail proves the claim.

Part 2: Choose A or B

3. Which comparison identifies a shared theme and analyzes a meaningful difference in treatment?

A. Both scenes have young people and mistakes, so they are basically the same.
B. Both scenes develop the idea that responsibility begins with honest ownership, but the team setting emphasizes shared learning while the concert setting emphasizes personal preparation.

Record A or B in your submission and explain your choice in 2–3 sentences. The answer is not revealed here because this item is part of your graded written work.

Part 3: Create and Apply

4. Write two connected micro-scenes of 200–250 words each that develop the same universal theme in different ways.

  1. Scene 1: Establish a character, setting, conflict, choice, and consequence.
  2. Scene 2: Use a different character or setting and develop the same theme through a contrasting choice or consequence.
  3. Synthesis paragraph: State the shared theme and explain how one craft choice in each scene creates a different thematic treatment.

Plan before drafting: Decide what the reader should understand, how each section will build that understanding, and which details will do the most work.

Part 4: Revise With Purpose

5. Strengthen one contrast between the scenes—such as point of view, setting, conflict, or consequence—without changing the shared theme.

Do more than correct spelling. Revise the organization, meaning, evidence, or craft so the writing becomes clearer and more effective.
