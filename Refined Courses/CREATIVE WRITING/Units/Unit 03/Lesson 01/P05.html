The Repaired Lantern

Each winter, the Mercado family hung paper lanterns in the courtyard of their apartment building. Mercado means “market” in Spanish, but here it is the family’s surname. The oldest lantern carried a repaired seam and belonged to Alma’s late grandfather.

This year the landlord prohibited decorations in shared spaces, citing a new safety inspection. Alma’s mother moved the lanterns inside without argument. “A home can remember quietly,” she said.

Alma disagreed. She created a petition and described the ban as an attempt to erase the families who had lived there longest. Newer tenants signed, but several older residents did not. Mr. Cho from 4B explained that the courtyard decorations made it difficult for his wife, who used a wheelchair, to pass during crowded gatherings.

Alma measured the walkway. He was right. The lantern tables narrowed the accessible route—the unobstructed path needed by people using mobility devices—to less than half its required width.

At the inspection meeting, the landlord presented photographs of blocked exits. Alma presented a different plan: lanterns suspended above the courtyard, a clear route below, and written permission for decorations meeting specific safety rules. The inspector approved a trial.

On the first night, the repaired lantern hung higher than it ever had. Alma worried that raising it had made the tradition less authentic. Then Mrs. Cho crossed beneath it and asked whose lantern it had been.

Alma told her.

The lantern moved in the cold air, its repaired seam opening and closing like a small mouth learning a new story.

Part A — Theme Evidence

  1. Write a 120–150 word objective summary.
  2. Identify the subject and central value conflict.
  3. Trace Alma’s changing understanding.
  4. Explain how Mr. Cho complicates her initial claim.
  5. Map the causal chain from measurement to trial.
  6. Trace the lantern motif through three contexts.
  7. Analyze the mother’s statement as subtext.
  8. Explain the final image without reducing it to one fixed symbol.
  9. Write two plausible qualified themes.
  10. Select the stronger theme and support it with three details.

Part B — Specificity and Revision

  1. Explain how the story preserves cultural and accessibility-specific details.
  2. Identify one interpretation the evidence does not support.
  3. Use context to define authentic as Alma uses it.
  4. Revise one absolute claim into a qualified claim.
  5. Rewrite the ending so the same theme is earned through different action.

Part C — Original Thematic Narrative

  1. Complete the four-column Visual Theme Bridge for an original conflict.
  2. Write a polished 600–750 word narrative grounded in specific context, using motif, contradiction, choice, and consequence to develop a qualified theme.
  3. Write a 170–220 word rationale quoting four choices and explaining how you preserved specificity while creating broader resonance.
