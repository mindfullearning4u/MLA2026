Worked Example: Three Synthesis Diagnoses

Example 1 — Repair Causality

Draft problem: Leena wants to expose a forged map. In the next scene, the curator suddenly confesses.
Step 1: Name the broken link: Leena makes no choice that causes the confession.
Step 2: Add pressure: the exhibit opens in ten minutes.
Step 3: Give Leena an evidence-based action: she projects the map’s mismatched watermarks during rehearsal.
Step 4: Make the response earned: the curator stops the projector and reveals knowledge only the forger should possess.
Result: Her choice causes the reversal; the confession is no longer coincidence.

Example 2 — Integrate Viewpoint and Theme

Draft problem: A third-person narrator tells readers that both rivals are afraid, destroying uncertainty.
Step 1: Set the controlling effect: readers should question whether pride or fear drives the conflict.
Step 2: Limit access to Mateo’s viewpoint.
Step 3: Show Priya through observable evidence—her precise notes, delayed replies, and hand covering a cracked medal.
Step 4: Let Mateo misread those details as arrogance; later, the medal reveals shared fear of failure.
Result: Limited access produces tension and supports the theme that certainty about others can be a form of pride.

Example 3 — Transform Setup into Payoff

Opening setup: Every morning, Jo resets a stopped station clock to 8:12, the minute her sister left town.
Weak ending: Jo watches another train depart.
Step 1: Identify the repeated object and its current meaning: the clock preserves grief as ritual.
Step 2: Force a choice: Jo can miss an interview to reset it or board her train.
Step 3: Transform, not erase, the pattern: she leaves the clock stopped and writes the correct time on her ticket.
Result: The payoff answers the opening image; memory remains, but it no longer controls her future.

Choose A or B — Which Ending Completes the System?

Opening setup: Every morning, Jo resets a stopped station clock to 8:12, the minute her sister left town.

Choice A: Jo watches another train leave and the narrator announces that she has learned to move on.
Weaker response.

Not the strongest choice. The ending repeats the situation and explains the theme, but Jo makes no consequential choice and the clock is not transformed.

Choice B: Jo boards her own train, leaves the clock stopped, and writes the correct time on her ticket.
Stronger response.

Correct. The action pays off the clock motif, shows agency, transforms the opening ritual, and preserves memory without allowing it to control the future.

Retry: Close your first choice and open the other. Compare both explanations before continuing.

Your Turn — Test a counterreading

A reader might argue that leaving the clock stopped means Jo remains trapped in grief. The ticket’s correct time is stronger evidence: she carries the memory while choosing forward movement. The interpretation accounts for both details.
