Lesson: The Fiction Integration Protocol

The Core Principle

A draft is integrated when every major element earns its place by supporting the same developing effect. Do not ask only, “Is the character interesting?” Ask, “How does this character’s desire activate conflict, shape structure, control what the viewpoint notices, and reveal the theme?”

Seven-Step Integration Protocol

  1. Name the controlling effect: Write the exact feeling, question, or insight the reader should carry.
  2. Trace the throughline: Summarize desire → opposition → escalating choices → irreversible consequence.
  3. Map access: Record what each character and the reader know, misread, or discover.
  4. Test causality: Replace “and then” between scenes with “therefore” or “but.” Repair any break.
  5. Track pattern: Mark repeated image, object, phrase, or setting detail and explain how its meaning changes.
  6. Test the ending: Verify that it pays off earlier choices and transforms—not merely repeats—the opening.
  7. Revise in order: Global structure first; scene purpose second; paragraph and sentence craft last.

Pause and Choose: Repair the Broken Causal Arrow

The ending reveals a hidden record, but the protagonist makes no decision that causes the discovery. Which global revision best repairs choice → consequence?

A. Describe the hidden record in more vivid language during the ending.
Not the best choice. Vivid wording changes presentation but leaves the discovery accidental and the protagonist passive.
B. Plant an earlier clue, force the protagonist to risk trust by following it, and make that decision uncover the record.
✓ Correct—the consequence now grows from agency. Setup, costly choice, and discovery become one causal sequence, allowing the ending to pay off earlier evidence.

Retry: If your first choice is weaker, close it and select the other choice before continuing. Compare both explanations so you can name why the stronger answer works.

Visual Diagnostic: The Story System

DESIRE → PRESSURE → CHOICE → CONSEQUENCE → NEW MEANING
Viewpoint controls what is seen   |   Pattern builds theme   |   Setting changes the cost

If one arrow is weak, diagnose that link instead of polishing random sentences.

Three Learning Routes

  • Visual: Color-code each element and draw arrows showing influence.
  • Verbal/auditory: Explain the story aloud as a chain of “because…therefore…but…”
  • Hands-on: Put scenes on cards; remove or reorder one and observe what meaning changes.

Pause and Choose: Test an Integrated Interpretation

A locked-window motif appears when the protagonist avoids conflict, cracks during a costly confession, and stands open in the final scene. Which analysis accounts for the full pattern and a counterreading?

A. “The window symbolizes freedom because open windows always mean freedom.”
Not the best choice. This imports a fixed symbol and ignores how the image changes with avoidance, confession, and consequence.
B. “The changing window tracks increasing openness: although it may first signal physical confinement, its shifts align most fully with the protagonist’s movement from avoidance to accountable disclosure.”
✓ Correct—the interpretation tests the whole system. It follows recurrence and variation, considers another plausible reading, and explains why the character arc and consequences support the stronger claim.

Retry: If your first choice is weaker, close it and select the other choice before continuing. Compare both explanations so you can name why the stronger answer works.

Evidence-Chain Sentence Frame

Because the writer uses [specific detail or pattern], the reader can infer [meaning]; this advances [craft function] and contributes to [controlling effect or theme]. Test a counterreading, then explain why your interpretation accounts for more evidence.

Pause and Try — Diagnose Before You Polish

Draft symptom: The ending is beautifully written, but readers say it feels unearned. The protagonist has not made a decision that causes the final event.

Your turn: Use the Story System to identify the broken arrow. Choose a global revision before proposing any sentence-level change.

Check your thinking with the teacher model

The broken arrow is choice → consequence. I would add an earlier decision that risks something important and directly produces the final event. Then I would revise the ending so it pays off that decision.

Why this comes first: Local polish cannot repair missing causality. Diagnostic reading locates the structural problem so revision effort serves the controlling effect.
