Worked Example: Three Rhetorical Craft Decisions

Example 1 — Personal Scene With Evidence

A student writes to the transit board about a missing late bus.

  1. Audience: board members controlling routes.
  2. Purpose: support a three-month pilot.
  3. Pathos: one scene shows a custodian waiting after midnight.
  4. Logos: missed-shift data reveals a pattern.
  5. Ethos: the writer acknowledges cost constraints.
  6. Kairos: budget hearing occurs Friday.
  7. Craft: scene opens attention; concise comparison moves toward action.

Example 2 — Revise Manipulative Emotion

Weak: “Only a heartless person could close the garden where children dream.”

  1. Problem: attacks audience character and substitutes emotion for evidence.
  2. Revision: describe one class measuring soil recovery.
  3. Add evidence: participation and maintenance costs.
  4. Acknowledge the competing need for parking.
  5. Purposeful pathos: show what use looks like.
  6. Ethical effect: invite judgment rather than shame disagreement.

Example 3 — Same Evidence, Different Audience

A story about water restrictions addresses residents and engineers.

  1. Residents need recognizable household stakes and clear choices.
  2. Engineers need measurement, uncertainty, and system constraints.
  3. Both versions preserve the same facts.
  4. Resident craft: close focalized scene and plain diction.
  5. Engineer craft: causal structure and precise quantities.
  6. Alignment changes form, not truth.

Choose A or B — Which Appeal Is Better Aligned?

Purpose: Persuade the library board to test later evening hours.

Pause and Choose: A or B

Which appeal is better aligned to a library board considering later evening hours?

A. The board clearly does not care about students, and everyone will suffer unless it keeps the library open late immediately.
Weaker response. This accusation relies on exaggerated emotion, weakens the writer’s credibility, and ignores the board’s practical concerns.
B. A four-week evening-hours pilot would serve students whose work schedules limit daytime access; attendance counts and staffing costs could then guide a permanent decision.
Stronger response. This proposal combines audience-aware evidence, a reasonable action, measurable results, and respectful tone, strengthening logos and ethos while acknowledging student needs.

Retry: Close your first choice and open the other. Compare both explanations before continuing.

Your Turn — Identify the appeals, then check

The human scene is pathos; verified counts are logos; acknowledging constraints builds ethos; proposing a limited pilot now uses kairos. Together they create rhetorical alignment rather than manipulation.
