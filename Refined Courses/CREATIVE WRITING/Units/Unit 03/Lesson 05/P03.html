Direct Teaching: Aligning Purpose, Audience, and Craft

Seven-Step Rhetorical Craft Method

  1. Name the audience precisely.
  2. State the purpose as an action: reassure, challenge, expose, invite, or persuade.
  3. Define the intended effect.
  4. Identify the situation, constraints, and timing.
  5. Select ethical appeals and evidence.
  6. Choose craft elements that carry those appeals.
  7. Audit alignment and unintended effects.

Pause and Choose: Align Appeal with Audience

A student asks the library board to test later evening hours. Which opening coordinates ethos, logos, and pathos for that audience?

A. “Adults clearly do not care whether teenagers have a safe place to study.”
Not the best choice. The accusation stereotypes the audience, offers no evidence, and makes cooperation less likely. Emotional force does not substitute for a defensible appeal.
B. “At closing, twelve students moved unfinished work to the unlit bus stop. Usage logs show this pattern on most weekdays; we request a thirty-day trial that accounts for staffing limits.”
✓ Correct—the appeals reinforce one purpose. The scene clarifies human stakes, the data supports reasoning, and the fair acknowledgment plus limited request builds credibility with the board.

Retry: If your first choice is weaker, close it and select the other choice before continuing. Compare both explanations so you can name why the stronger answer works.

Purpose Is Not Topic

Topic: school lunch. Weak purpose: “to talk about lunch.” Precise purpose: “to help families see how a shortened lunch period affects students with mobility needs and support a schedule revision.”

Ethical Persuasion

  • Represent opposing evidence fairly.
  • Do not fabricate urgency or certainty.
  • Let emotional detail clarify stakes rather than replace reasoning.
  • Anticipate audiences without stereotyping them.
  • Give readers enough context for informed judgment.

Pause and Choose: Use Urgency Ethically

The board meets next month, but no immediate closure is planned. Which sentence uses kairos without fabricating a crisis?

A. “Unless you approve this tonight, students will permanently lose their chance to succeed.”
Not the best choice. It invents a deadline and an absolute consequence. False urgency weakens credibility and prevents informed judgment.
B. “A trial approved at next month’s meeting could begin before winter’s shorter daylight hours increase the need for a safe evening study space.”
✓ Correct—the timing is genuine and relevant. The sentence connects the actual decision window to a foreseeable condition without exaggerating certainty or forcing agreement.

Retry: If your first choice is weaker, close it and select the other choice before continuing. Compare both explanations so you can name why the stronger answer works.

Common Failures

  • Choosing techniques before defining purpose.
  • Calling any sad detail pathos without explaining its function.
  • Treating ethos, logos, and pathos as separate boxes rather than interacting appeals.
  • Using a generalized “everyone” audience.
  • Confusing persuasion with guaranteed agreement.

Pause and Try — Build an Ethical Appeal

Situation: A student wants the library board to extend evening hours. The draft begins by accusing adults of not caring about teenagers.

Your turn: Name the audience and purpose. Then replace the accusation with one ethical appeal that uses a purposeful craft choice.

Check your thinking with the teacher model

The audience is the library board; the purpose is to win approval for a trial extension. I would open with a brief scene of students leaving at closing time, add reliable usage data, acknowledge staffing limits, and request a thirty-day pilot.

Why this works: The scene creates measured pathos, the data supplies logos, and the fair acknowledgment builds ethos. The form and tone respect the audience instead of manipulating it.
