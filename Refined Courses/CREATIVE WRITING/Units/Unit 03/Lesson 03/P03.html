Direct Teaching: Transforming Sources Responsibly

Eight-Step Adaptation Method

  1. Identify the exact source and version.
  2. Research its cultural, historical, and—when relevant—sacred context.
  3. Map structural DNA: roles, desire, prohibition, turn, consequence.
  4. Name what your audience must recognize.
  5. Choose at least two meaningful transformations.
  6. Write a new controlling effect or thematic question.
  7. Set an ethical boundary and attribution plan.
  8. Audit the draft for transformation, respect, and independent value.

Pause and Choose: Transform Meaning, Not Cosmetics

A source follows a gifted person who ignores warnings, crosses a boundary, and loses the gift. Which adaptation makes a meaningful transformation?

A. Rename the character, move the same events to a modern city, and preserve the original consequence.
Not the best choice. New names and scenery are cosmetic. The roles, pressure, decision, consequence, and thematic question remain essentially copied.
B. Make the character a researcher who releases a powerful model despite community warnings, shifting the consequence from personal loss to public harm.
✓ Correct—the transformation changes stakes and meaning. The recognizable warning-boundary pattern remains, but power, affected people, consequence, and the question of responsibility are newly constructed.

Retry: If your first choice is weaker, close it and select the other choice before continuing. Compare both explanations so you can name why the stronger answer works.

Source Responsibility

  • Use a public-domain literary source or a source you have permission to adapt.
  • For living religious or cultural traditions, research community perspectives and avoid presenting sacred material as decorative fantasy.
  • Do not combine distinct cultures into a generic “mythic” setting.
  • Credit the exact version used.
  • If context cannot be researched responsibly, choose a different source.

Pause and Choose: Set a Responsible Source Boundary

A writer finds a sacred story from a living tradition but cannot access reliable community context. Which decision best follows source responsibility?

A. Keep its most striking images, remove the cultural names, and call the setting “mythic.”
Not the best choice. Removing names does not remove cultural meaning. It strips context while retaining sacred material as decoration and prevents accurate attribution.
B. Choose a documented public-domain literary source that can be researched and credited responsibly.
✓ Correct—the boundary protects both craft and context. A source with verifiable history and clear permissions lets the writer transform meaning while acknowledging the exact version and avoiding unsupported cultural claims.

Common Failures

  • Plot copying with cosmetic updates.
  • Using an allusion readers cannot recognize or understand.
  • Claiming one version is the universal original.
  • Removing cultural context while keeping exotic imagery.
  • Explaining connections instead of building them into action.

Pause and Try: Map the Source DNA

Source pattern: A gifted person ignores repeated warnings, crosses a boundary, and loses the gift.

Before opening the model, identify the conflict, turning point, and consequence. Then propose one transformation that changes the meaning—not merely the names.

Check your thinking

Conflict: desire or pride versus a protective limit. Turn: the character knowingly crosses the boundary. Consequence: the gift is lost. One strong transformation could place the pattern in a data-science lab where a researcher releases a powerful model despite community warnings; the new context changes who bears the consequence and raises a question about public responsibility.
