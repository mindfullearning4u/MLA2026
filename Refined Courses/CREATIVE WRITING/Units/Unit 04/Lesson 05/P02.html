Notebook Title

Lesson 5: Connotation, Denotation, and Style

Vocabulary

  • Denotation: A word's direct dictionary meaning.
  • Connotation: The feeling, attitude, or association attached to a word.
  • Style: The recognizable way a writer uses language.
  • Tone: The writer's attitude toward the subject.
  • Mood: The feeling created for the reader.
  • Precision: Choosing the exact word that fits the intended meaning and effect.

Step 1: Start with Denotation

Denotation is the basic meaning. The words thin, slender, and scrawny all connect to being small in width. If you stop there, you miss the style effect.

Denotation: All three words can describe a narrow or slight body or object.

Step 2: Add Connotation

Slender often feels graceful or delicate. Thin is more neutral. Scrawny often feels negative, weak, or unhealthy. Same general denotation, different connotation.

Poetry use: A poet writing a gentle line may choose slender branch. A poet writing a harsh line may choose scrawny branch.

Step 3: Connect Connotation to Style

Style is built from repeated choices. If a poem uses words like slender, hushed, and silver, the style may feel delicate and quiet. If it uses scrawny, cracked, and grit, the style may feel harsher and rougher.

Self-Paced Learning Path

If you need remedial support: Label each word as positive, negative, or neutral before explaining style.

If you are ready for standard work: Choose the best word for a poem line and explain its connotation.

If you are ready for accelerated work: Explain how three diction choices work together to create a consistent style.
