Checkpoint: Figurative Language and Mood

Put the lesson together through analysis, decision-making, writing, and purposeful revision.

Purpose

Show that you can understand the lesson concept, apply it in original writing, and explain your craft decisions. Complete every numbered item and submit your responses with your Checkpoint work.

New Source: Platform at Dawn

The train station yawns beneath a bruised-blue sky. A single lamp keeps watch while rain stitches silver lines across the tracks. My suitcase waits beside me like a question I cannot answer.

Figurative language creates meaning beyond literal wording. Mood is the emotional atmosphere a reader experiences.

Part 1: Analyze the Source

  1. Identify two examples of figurative language. Explain what each comparison or image suggests beyond its literal meaning.
  2. What mood does the passage create? Cite two words or phrases and explain how their connotations build that mood.

Use this response pattern: Make a precise claim, cite the relevant word, line, or image, and explain how it proves the claim.

Part 2: Choose A or B

3. Which response analyzes how figurative language creates mood rather than merely summarizing details?

A. The station is quiet and it is raining. The speaker has a suitcase.
B. The personified station “yawns” while the lamp “keeps watch,” creating a lonely, uncertain mood as the speaker faces departure.

Record A or B in your submission and explain your choice in 2–3 sentences. The answer is not revealed here because this item is part of your graded written work.

Part 3: Create and Apply

4. Write a 14–18 line original poem that creates one intentional mood.

  1. Opening image: Place the reader in a specific moment or setting.
  2. Development: Use at least three purposeful examples of figurative language, including two different techniques.
  3. Turn: Shift the speaker’s understanding, emotion, or focus.
  4. Close: End with an image that deepens the mood instead of explaining it.

After the poem, identify your three figurative choices and explain how each contributes to the mood.

Plan before drafting: Decide what the reader should experience, how each movement will build that effect, and which words and images will do the most work.

Part 4: Revise With Purpose

5. Replace one predictable comparison with a fresher image. Explain how the new wording changes or sharpens the mood.

Do more than correct spelling. Revise the organization, meaning, evidence, or craft so the writing becomes clearer and more effective.
