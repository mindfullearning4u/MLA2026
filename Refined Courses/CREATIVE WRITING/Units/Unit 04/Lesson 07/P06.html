Checkpoint: Integrated Poem Development

Put the lesson together through analysis, decision-making, writing, and purposeful revision.

Purpose

Show that you can understand the lesson concept, apply it in original writing, and explain your craft decisions. Complete every numbered item and submit your responses with your Checkpoint work.

New Source: Weather Station

The rooftop gauge collects rain
in careful increments.
Below, my father packs the kitchen
into boxes labeled KEEP.
The needle records pressure falling.
Neither of us names the storm.

Integration means making imagery, figurative language, diction, sound, structure, ambiguity, and theme work as one design rather than as separate decorations.

Part 1: Analyze the Source

  1. How do the weather instruments and household actions work together to develop meaning? Cite two connections.
  2. How does the final line use ambiguity and restraint to deepen the emotional effect?

Use this response pattern: Make a precise claim, cite the relevant word, line, or image, and explain how it proves the claim.

Part 2: Choose A or B

3. Which plan creates an integrated poem rather than a checklist of devices?

A. Add one example of every poetic device even if the devices do not relate to one another.
B. Choose a small set of connected craft moves and repeat, vary, or transform them so each supports the poem’s central tension.

Record A or B in your submission and explain your choice in 2–3 sentences. The answer is not revealed here because this item is part of your graded written work.

Part 3: Create and Apply

4. Write a polished 24–32 line poem in which multiple craft elements operate together.

  1. Opening: Establish a precise situation, speaker, and controlling image.
  2. Development: Build a connected image or sound pattern using deliberate diction and figurative language.
  3. Tension: Let the literal situation carry an implied emotional or thematic conflict.
  4. Turn: Transform an earlier image, sound, or word.
  5. Close: Create a resonant ending that preserves purposeful complexity.

Add a 175–225 word craft reflection explaining how at least four elements—imagery, figurative language, diction, sound, structure, ambiguity, or theme—work together.

Plan before drafting: Decide what the reader should experience, how each movement will build that effect, and which words and images will do the most work.

Part 4: Revise With Purpose

5. Complete one structural revision and one line-level revision. Identify both changes and explain how they improve unity and effect.

Do more than correct spelling. Revise the organization, meaning, evidence, or craft so the writing becomes clearer and more effective.
