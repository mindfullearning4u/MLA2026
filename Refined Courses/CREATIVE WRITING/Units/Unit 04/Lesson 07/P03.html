Lesson: The Integrated Poem Blueprint

Nine-Step Process

  1. Name the controlling effect.
  2. Select a concrete generative image.
  3. List literal details before symbolism.
  4. Build two related image or lexical fields.
  5. Choose speaker, audience, and situation.
  6. Plan a three-part architecture: establish, complicate, transform.
  7. Use sound and lineation to control movement.
  8. Create a turn caused by discovery, contrast, or re-seeing.
  9. Revise globally, then locally, testing every choice for coherence.

Visual Blueprint

IMAGE → PATTERN → COUNTERPRESSURE → TURN → TRANSFORMED IMAGE
Diction + sound + lineation shape every stage.

Pause and Choose: Build a Transforming Image

A poem begins with a seed sealed in a paper envelope. Which plan creates a coherent three-part architecture?

A. “Describe the seed, switch to an unrelated storm, and end by describing the same seed in the same way.”
Weaker choice. The storm has no clear link to the generative image, and the ending does not transform the seed. Events occur, but the poem lacks a developing pattern and payoff.
B. “Establish the unopened seed, complicate it with the speaker’s fear of planting, then end with the torn envelope beside new soil.”
Stronger choice. The same image passes through pressure and change. The torn envelope and soil return to the opening materials while showing that the speaker’s relationship to possibility has shifted.

Retry: If your first choice is weaker, close it and select the other choice before continuing. Compare both explanations so you can name why the stronger answer works.

Learning Routes

Color-code patterns; perform the poem aloud; arrange lines on cards and test alternate architectures.

Pause and Choose: Revise Globally Before Locally

A draft contains vivid lines, but its ending introduces a new image that does not connect to anything earlier. What should the writer do first?

A. Rework the ending so it transforms an established image or intentionally completes a pattern.
Stronger choice. This is a global revision because it repairs architecture and coherence. Once the ending performs its structural job, the writer can polish individual words and sounds.
B. Keep the ending and replace three ordinary verbs with more unusual synonyms.
Weaker choice. Local word changes cannot solve an architectural problem. Polishing sentences before repairing the poem’s design may make disconnected material sound smoother without making it meaningful.

Retry: If your first choice is weaker, close it and select the other choice before continuing. Compare both explanations so you can name why the stronger answer works.
