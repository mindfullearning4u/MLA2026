Vocabulary and Definitions

Notebook Task: Record every core term and its complete meaning in your notebook. Submit these vocabulary notes with your Independent Work as Notebook Evidence. Use the examples to see how each term works in context. Reference glossary terms do not need to be copied.
TermMeaningExample
paraphraseA restatement preserving meaning in genuinely new wording and structure.“Night covered the field” can become “Darkness settled across the open land.”
interpretationAn evidence-based explanation of what a text suggests or means.The repeated locked windows may suggest the speaker feels trapped.
inferenceA conclusion formed from textual evidence and reasoning.A trembling hand and unfinished sentence support an inference of anxiety.
literal propositionA basic claim directly stated or logically contained in a line.“The train left at dawn” literally states a departure time.
syntactic inversionAn unusual word order used for emphasis, rhythm, or form.“Into the rain ran Maya” places the setting before the action.
referentThe person, object, or idea a pronoun or phrase identifies.In “Lena gave it back,” context must show what “it” names.
context clueNearby information that helps determine meaning.“Brittle, snapping twigs” helps readers infer that brittle means easily broken.
semantic relationshipA meaning connection such as cause, contrast, condition, or comparison.“Although” signals a contrast between two ideas.
patchwritingA near-copy that changes only a few words.Replacing two adjectives while keeping the source structure is patchwriting.
distortionA change that misrepresents the original meaning.Removing “might” turns a possibility into an unsupported certainty.
evidence chainDetail → literal meaning → inference → interpretation.A repeated empty chair supports a chain from absence to possible grief.
qualifierA word limiting certainty, scope, or intensity.“May suggest” accurately limits an interpretive claim.

Make the Vocabulary Work

Which statement is an accurate interpretation rather than a distortion?

A. “The repeated empty chair may suggest that the speaker is grieving an absence.”

Best choice. The claim names evidence and uses the qualifier “may,” leaving room for uncertainty.

B. “The empty chair proves that the speaker’s parent died.”

Not the best choice. The detail supports absence, but it does not prove who is missing or why. The claim distorts evidence into certainty.
