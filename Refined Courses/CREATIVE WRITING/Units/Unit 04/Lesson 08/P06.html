Put the lesson together through analysis, decision-making, writing, and purposeful revision.

Purpose

Show that you can understand the lesson concept, apply it in original writing, and explain your craft decisions. Complete every numbered item and submit your responses with your Checkpoint work.

New Source: The Names We Carry

A city replaces the names of its streets after every flood. An older resident remembers all the names; a child knows only the newest ones. On the morning another storm arrives, they begin marking the forgotten names in chalk, but the rain is already falling.

Poetic synthesis combines interpretation and original craft so figurative language, mood, layered meaning, word history, connotation, style, model-based learning, and structure reinforce one another.

Part 1: Analyze the Source

  1. Identify two possible meanings of the changing street names. Explain how the resident and child could create contrasting perspectives.
  2. Choose three Unit 4 concepts and explain how they could work together at the poem’s turn rather than appearing as separate requirements.

Use this response pattern: Make a precise claim, cite the relevant word, line, or image, and explain how it proves the claim.

Part 2: Choose A or B

3. Which plan demonstrates authentic poetic synthesis?

A. Mention each Unit 4 vocabulary term in the poem so readers can see that every skill is present.
B. Embed the skills in a unified pattern of images, diction, sound, contrasts, and transformations that develops the poem’s meaning.

Record A or B in your submission and explain your choice in 2–3 sentences. The answer is not revealed here because this item is part of your graded written work.

Part 3: Create and Apply

4. Create a polished 32–45 line original poem or sequence that synthesizes Unit 4 learning.

  1. Opening movement: Establish a speaker, concrete situation, mood, and controlling image.
  2. Development: Build layers through purposeful figurative language, diction, connotation, and a repeated image or sound pattern.
  3. Interpretive tension: Allow at least two defensible meanings to emerge.
  4. Turn: Transform an earlier word, image, or structural pattern so the speaker’s understanding changes.
  5. Closing movement: Synthesize the poem’s central tension in a resonant ending without explaining the message.

Then write a 250–300 word craft commentary analyzing how at least five Unit 4 concepts work together. If you used a poetic model, name it and explain the structural influence without copying.

Plan before drafting: Decide what the reader should experience, how each movement will build that effect, and which words and images will do the most work.

Part 4: Revise With Purpose

5. Complete three purposeful revisions: one structural, one image or diction revision, and one sound or line-level revision. Briefly explain the effect of each.

Do more than correct spelling. Revise the organization, meaning, evidence, or craft so the writing becomes clearer and more effective.
