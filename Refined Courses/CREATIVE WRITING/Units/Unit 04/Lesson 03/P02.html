Vocabulary and Definitions

Notebook Task: Record every core term and its complete meaning in your notebook. Submit these vocabulary notes with your Independent Work as Notebook Evidence. Use the examples to see how each term works in context. Reference glossary terms do not need to be copied.
TermMeaningExample
layered meaningMeaning operating at literal, implied, symbolic, or structural levels.A locked door can be literal while also suggesting emotional separation.
productive ambiguityDeliberate openness that supports multiple evidence-based readings.“She kept the second key” may suggest hope or reluctance; both readings need evidence.
vaguenessInsufficient specificity that prevents a defensible interpretation.“Something happened somehow” provides no concrete clue to analyze.
inferenceA conclusion formed from evidence and reasoning.Repeated glances at a clock may suggest urgency, though they do not prove its cause.
subtextMeaning suggested beneath what is directly stated.“You remembered,” spoken while packing, may imply hurt beneath the literal words.
symbolic possibilityA plausible abstract meaning carried by a concrete detail.An unwatered plant may suggest a neglected relationship when other details reinforce it.
referentThe person, object, or idea to which a word or pronoun refers.A writer clarifies whether “it” refers to the letter or the promise.
syntaxThe arrangement of words and phrases in a sentence.A sudden fragment can slow the reader and emphasize a turning point.
enjambmentA poetic sentence continuing beyond a line break without final punctuation.The thought runs into the next line, delaying completion and building tension.
caesuraA strong pause within a poetic line.A dash in the middle of a line creates a noticeable pause.
paradoxA seeming contradiction that reveals a possible truth.“The silence was loud” conveys emotional pressure through contradiction.
evidence coverageHow fully an interpretation accounts for important details.A strong reading explains both the open window and the locked door.

Make the Vocabulary Work

Which sentence creates productive ambiguity rather than vagueness?

A. “She placed the unopened letter beside the key, then turned off the porch light.”

Best choice. Concrete details allow more than one defensible reading: she may be ending hope, protecting a secret, or postponing a decision. The uncertainty is purposeful because readers can test each interpretation against evidence.

B. “Somehow, she felt a thing about what happened.”

Not the best choice. This is vague because it withholds the specific details readers would need to form and defend an interpretation.
