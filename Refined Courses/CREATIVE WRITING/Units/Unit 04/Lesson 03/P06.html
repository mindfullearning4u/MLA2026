Checkpoint: Poetic Layers of Meaning and Ambiguity

Put the lesson together through analysis, decision-making, writing, and purposeful revision.

Purpose

Show that you can understand the lesson concept, apply it in original writing, and explain your craft decisions. Complete every numbered item and submit your responses with your Checkpoint work.

New Source: The Key

I keep the key
to a door that is gone.
Some nights it opens
the house behind my eyes;
some mornings
it is only cold metal.

Ambiguity allows more than one reasonable interpretation. Layered meaning develops when an image works literally and symbolically at the same time.

Part 1: Analyze the Source

  1. Explain two reasonable meanings of the key. Cite a line that supports each interpretation.
  2. How does the contrast between “the house behind my eyes” and “only cold metal” deepen the poem rather than resolve it?

Use this response pattern: Make a precise claim, cite the relevant word, line, or image, and explain how it proves the claim.

Part 2: Choose A or B

3. Which interpretation respects the poem’s ambiguity while remaining evidence-based?

A. The key can only be literal because the poem names a physical object.
B. The key can be a physical keepsake and a symbol of memory, belonging, or loss because the surrounding images support more than one reading.

Record A or B in your submission and explain your choice in 2–3 sentences. The answer is not revealed here because this item is part of your graded written work.

Part 3: Create and Apply

4. Write a 16–22 line poem centered on one ordinary object that develops at least two defensible meanings.

  1. Opening: Present the object through precise sensory detail.
  2. Development: Place it in two different contexts so its significance changes.
  3. Turn: Introduce tension between the literal object and what it may symbolize.
  4. Close: Preserve purposeful ambiguity while leaving enough evidence for interpretation.

After the poem, explain two possible readings and cite one line supporting each. Do not declare that only one reading is correct.

Plan before drafting: Decide what the reader should experience, how each movement will build that effect, and which words and images will do the most work.

Part 4: Revise With Purpose

5. Revise one line that explains the symbol too directly. Replace the explanation with an image, action, or contrast that invites interpretation.

Do more than correct spelling. Revise the organization, meaning, evidence, or craft so the writing becomes clearer and more effective.
