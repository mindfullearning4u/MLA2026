Notebook Title

Lesson 4: Word Origins and Poetic Diction

Vocabulary

  • Diction: A writer's word choice.
  • Denotation: A word's direct dictionary meaning.
  • Connotation: The feeling or association a word carries.
  • Root: A word part that carries core meaning.
  • Prefix: A word part added to the beginning of a word.
  • Suffix: A word part added to the end of a word.
  • Etymology: The history or origin of a word.

Step 1: Begin with Meaning

Poets need accurate words before they can make beautiful lines. Start with denotation. For example, glow, glare, and gleam all involve light, but they do not feel the same.

Glow: soft steady light. Glare: harsh bright light. Gleam: brief or polished light.

Step 2: Add Connotation

If the poem should feel gentle, glow may fit best. If the poem should feel uncomfortable, glare may fit better. If the poem should feel quick and precise, gleam may be strongest.

Line: The hospital lights ____ above us.

Best choice for discomfort: glared. The word suggests harsh brightness and pressure.

Step 3: Use Word Parts to Infer Meaning

Word parts can help you understand or choose precise language. The prefix sub- often means under. The root luc or lum connects to light. The suffix -less means without. If a line describes a room as lightless, the word means without light and may suggest emptiness, fear, or loss depending on context.

Self-Paced Learning Path

If you need remedial support: Make a three-column chart: word, denotation, connotation.

If you are ready for standard work: Choose the best word for a line and explain the mood it creates.

If you are ready for accelerated work: Revise a line twice to create two different moods using different diction.
