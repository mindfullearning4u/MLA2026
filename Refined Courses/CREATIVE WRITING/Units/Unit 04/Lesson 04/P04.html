Worked Example: Three Diction Decisions

Example 1 — Precision

Draft: “The fog went across the field.” Candidates: moved, drifted, prowled. Prowled adds stealth and threat through connotation and hard consonants. Revision: “Fog prowled the field.” The choice creates unease.

Pause and Choose: A or B

Which revision uses diction most deliberately to create unease?

A. The fog went slowly across the field.
Weaker response. “Slowly” changes speed but leaves the action general and does not create a strong association.
B. Fog prowled the field.
Stronger response. “Prowled” is precise and carries connotations of stealth and threat, so one verb changes both movement and mood.

Retry: Close your first choice and open the other. Compare both explanations before continuing.

Example 2 — Origin and Pattern

“Luminous” derives from Latin lumen, light. In a poem using lamp, ember, and dawn, “luminous” joins the light lexical field while sounding elevated. “Bright” is accurate but less patterned.

Example 3 — Register and Voice

A child speaker says, “Grandma’s room smelled medicinal.” The word is precise but mismatched to voice. “Like the sharp cabinet at the doctor’s” preserves sensory accuracy and believable register.

Revision Rule

Choose the word whose meaning, association, sound, register, and pattern serve the poem—not the longest or rarest word.

Your Turn Before Independent Work

Pause and try: Revise “The leaves moved across the sidewalk” with one precise verb that creates a chosen mood. Then explain the verb’s connotation.

Check your thinking — open after you try
Teacher model: For unease: “Leaves skittered across the sidewalk.” “Skittered” suggests quick, irregular movement, making the ordinary leaves feel nervous or unpredictable.
