Lesson: The Six-Lens Diction Protocol

Six Lenses

  1. Denotation: What does the word name precisely?
  2. Origin: What root or history adds a useful association?
  3. Connotation: What emotion or cultural association travels with it?
  4. Register: Does it fit the speaker and situation?
  5. Sound: Do its vowels, consonants, and stress reinforce the effect?
  6. Pattern: Does it strengthen or disrupt the poem’s lexical field?

Pause and Choose: Diction with Purpose

A poet describes a person moving through an abandoned station. Which sentence uses diction whose connotation, sound, and register best create quiet unease?

A. “The traveler walked across the empty platform.”
Weaker choice. “Walked” gives the basic action, but its neutral connotation and ordinary sound do little to build unease. It names movement without shaping the reader’s emotional response.
B. “The traveler drifted across the hollow platform.”
Stronger choice. “Drifted” suggests aimless, nearly ghostlike motion, while “hollow” adds emptiness through both meaning and sound. Together, the words create unease without stating the feeling directly.

Retry: If your first choice is weaker, close it and select the other choice before continuing. Compare both explanations so you can name why the stronger answer works.

Learning Routes

  • Visual: color-code lexical fields.
  • Auditory: read choices aloud and compare sound.
  • Hands-on: rank word cards from weakest to strongest connotation.

Pause and Choose: Use Word Origins Responsibly

The word disaster developed from roots connected with an unfavorable star. Which explanation uses that origin accurately in a poem analysis?

A. “Because of its origin, disaster can only mean a harmful event caused by stars.”
Weaker choice. This commits the etymological fallacy. A word’s history can enrich an interpretation, but it does not erase the word’s accepted present-day meaning.
B. “The modern meaning is catastrophe, while the older star association may deepen a poem’s suggestion of fate.”
Stronger choice. This begins with current meaning, then treats the origin as a possible association supported by context. It adds a layer of interpretation without turning word history into a false definition.

Retry: If your first choice is weaker, close it and select the other choice before continuing. Compare both explanations so you can name why the stronger answer works.

Caution

Etymology informs current meaning; it does not replace it. Avoid the etymological fallacy—the mistaken belief that a word’s earliest meaning is its only correct meaning.
