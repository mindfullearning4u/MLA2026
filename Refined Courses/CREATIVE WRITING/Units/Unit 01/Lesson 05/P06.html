Checkpoint: Inference and Writer Choices

Your Purpose

Independently analyze a new source and apply inference and writer choices in organized original writing.

Complete all four parts. A score of at least 80% is required.

New Source: “The Spare Key”

Nolan found the spare key beneath the flowerpot where it had always been. The porch light was off, but a fresh grocery receipt lay folded under the mat. He read the time twice, returned the receipt exactly where it was, and knocked instead of unlocking the door.

Term support: An inference is a conclusion supported by evidence and reasoning. It is not a guess presented as fact.

1. Analyze the Source

  1. Make one supported inference about Nolan’s relationship to the house. Cite two details and explain the connection.
  2. Identify one tempting assumption the passage does not support. Explain what additional evidence would be needed.

2. Choose and Defend

Record A or B, then defend your choice in 3–4 sentences using source evidence and a lesson concept. Explain why the other response is weaker.

A. Nolan is definitely estranged from his parents because he refuses to use the key.
B. Nolan’s choice to knock may suggest that his sense of belonging or permission has changed, although the reason remains unstated.

No answer is revealed before submission. Your choice and reasoning are graded.

3. Create and Organize

Task: Write a 300–400 word scene in which a character’s relationship has changed, but readers must infer the change from actions, objects, setting, and dialogue.

Required organization:

  1. Opening—use an immediate action or object to establish the situation and create a question.
  2. Development—plant at least three connected clues; do not explain the relationship directly.
  3. Turning point—make the character choose an action that changes or confirms the reader’s inference.
  4. Closing—show an immediate consequence and end with a detail that sharpens the implied meaning.

4. Revise and Explain

Ask whether each major inference has at least two supporting details. Add, remove, or revise one clue, then explain how the change guides readers without overexplaining.

Before You Submit

  • Both analysis questions use exact source evidence.
  • Your A/B defense applies the lesson concept and addresses both choices.
  • Your original writing follows the required organization and demonstrates the lesson skill.
  • Your revision is meaningful and your explanation identifies its effect.
  • Your work is original, complete, and carefully proofread.
