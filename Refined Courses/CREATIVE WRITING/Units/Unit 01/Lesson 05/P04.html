Worked Example: Three Ways to Guide an Inference

Watch the Reasoning, Including Its Limits

Each example moves from observations to a calibrated claim and then to a complete original transfer. Notice when I reject my first confident explanation. Good reading is not guessing the most dramatic story; good writing makes a particular inference possible without making every detail certain.

Example 1 — Converging Clues Need a Rival

Model: “Ari placed the visitor badge in the kitchen drawer. Before bed, he checked the latch twice and moved the spare pillow from the sofa to the closet.” A latch is a fastening device that keeps a door or container closed.

1. Observe: Ari puts away a badge, checks a latch twice, and moves a spare pillow. “He hides evidence of a visitor” is already an interpretation, not a camera-recorded fact.

2. Connect: The badge and spare pillow may be associated with a visitor. Putting both out of sight can suggest removing traces of a visit; the repeated check may suggest concern about security.

3. Compare: Concealment is possible, but ordinary bedtime tidying and checking a door also explain all three actions. The passage gives no explicit secrecy or fear. I should not call concealment certain or automatically stronger.

4. Model analysis: The three actions can make a reader wonder whether Ari wants a visit to leave no visible traces. Yet routine tidying remains a plausible rival. The limited context supports a question about his motive more strongly than one definite answer.

5. Transfer and sharpen: I want a new scene to suggest concern about recognition. I need a more discriminating clue—one that ordinary tidying explains less well.

Original transfer: “Before the exhibition opened, Ren turned her name card toward the wall. When footsteps entered the gallery, she straightened every other card but left hers backward. She stayed beside the painting until the visitors passed.”

Why it works: Turning only her own card and leaving it reversed while correcting the others converge on selective concealment. Staying beside the painting complicates complete withdrawal. We still do not know why she wants her name less visible.

Example 2 — A Private Action Complicates a Public Response

Model: “The coach congratulated Mina for breaking the record. She smiled for the photograph, then wrote the old time back onto the locker-room board after everyone left.”

1. Observe: Mina is congratulated, smiles for a photograph, and later restores the old time. Her emotion is not directly named.

2. Connect: Public celebration and private restoration pull in different directions. The later action complicates a simple story of uncomplicated pride.

3. Compare: Unease about the new record is plausible. So are loyalty to a former record holder or a practical correction. The passage does not establish cheating, guilt, or even discomfort as a confirmed fact.

4. Model analysis: The sequence lets us first see an expected celebratory response, then revises that impression through a private action. Mina’s restoration of the old time suggests a reservation or other concern, but the reason remains open.

5. Transfer: Keep the principle—public acceptance followed by a complicating private action—but change situation, object, and wording.

Original transfer: “At the club meeting, Jo accepted the chairperson’s badge and thanked everyone. After the room emptied, she slid the badge into its box. She left the agenda open at the page headed NEXT YEAR and took the box with her.”

Why it works: Acceptance followed by putting the badge away can suggest mixed feelings; keeping the agenda open and taking the box complicate a definite refusal. A writer could add ‘Jo had decided to resign’ to settle the outcome, but that would remove the current interpretive question.

Example 3 — Sequence Invites Cause, but Does Not Prove It

Model A: “Nora deleted the message. Then the storm alert sounded.”

Model B: “The storm alert sounded. Then Nora deleted the message.”

1. Observe: Both versions contain deletion and an alert. Only the order changes.

2. Connect: In B, readers may initially treat deletion as a response to the alert. In A, deletion occurs before this alert and initially seems separate from it.

3. Compare: Nora could be reacting to the alert, or two unrelated events could occur close together. Neither version identifies the message contents. We cannot claim she is concealing a weather warning.

4. Model analysis: Putting the alert first increases the invitation to connect the events causally. The sequence changes a reader’s hypothesis, not the facts supplied about Nora’s motive.

5. Original transfer, Version A: “Suri closed the rehearsal schedule on her phone. Across the room, the audition results appeared on the screen. She kept the phone in her hand.”

Original transfer, Version B: “Across the room, the audition results appeared on the screen. Suri closed the rehearsal schedule on her phone. She kept the phone in her hand.”

Why it works: B invites us to connect closing the schedule with the results; A initially separates those events. The unchanged final action leaves open whether Suri will reopen the schedule. Neither version establishes whether she was selected.

Check your thinking

For Suri, “Her name was absent, so she canceled every rehearsal” supplies an outcome and cause. Without it, the observable actions still make sense and support a question about her response. Removing all reference to the results instead might weaken the evidence too much. Judge clarity and support, not simply how much information is withheld.
