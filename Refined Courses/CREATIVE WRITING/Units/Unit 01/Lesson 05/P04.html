Worked Example: Three Inference Architectures

Example 1 — Converging Evidence

Model: “Ari placed the visitor badge in the kitchen drawer. Before bed, he checked the latch twice and moved the spare pillow from the sofa to the closet.”

  1. Observations: badge hidden, latch rechecked, spare pillow removed.
  2. Convergence: all three actions concern a recent or expected visitor and a desire to erase or prevent that presence.
  3. Rival claims: Ari fears someone’s return; Ari wants to conceal that someone visited.
  4. Evaluation: concealment is stronger because hiding the badge and pillow changes visible evidence, while checking the latch also permits fear.
  5. Calibrated analysis: The actions strongly suggest Ari is concealing or repudiating a visit, although the exact relationship and cause remain unknown.
  6. Writer transfer: Use three actions aimed at removing traces rather than explicitly naming secrecy.

Example 2 — Contradictory Clue

Model: “The coach congratulated Mina for breaking the record. She smiled for the photograph, then wrote the old time back onto the locker-room board after everyone left.”

  1. Obvious reading: Mina should be proud of the record.
  2. Contradiction: restoring the old time resists public celebration.
  3. Possible inferences: guilt, loyalty to the previous holder, disbelief, or rejection of the circumstances.
  4. Evidence limit: the passage establishes discomfort but cannot identify its cause.
  5. Model analysis: The contradiction between public smile and private restoration makes unease certain but motive ambiguous. Naming guilt as fact would overstate the evidence.
  6. Writer transfer: Pair a socially expected response with a private action that quietly opposes it.

Example 3 — Sequence Changes the Inference

Model A: “Nora deleted the message. Then the storm alert sounded.”

Model B: “The storm alert sounded. Then Nora deleted the message.”

  1. Same details: deletion and alert.
  2. Different sequence: In A, deletion initially appears independent; in B, the alert may motivate it.
  3. Inference effect: B invites a causal relationship, perhaps an attempt to prevent travel or conceal warning.
  4. Limit: neither version proves the message concerned the storm.
  5. Model analysis: Sequence creates implied causality without adding facts. Placing the alert first strengthens—but does not establish—the inference that it prompts deletion.
  6. Writer transfer: Reorder two neutral actions and compare the causal stories readers construct.

Synthesis

Strong inference writing does not merely scatter clues. It designs their convergence, contradiction, and sequence, while analysis matches the strength of the claim to the strength of the evidence.

Common Failure and Revision

Unsupported: “Mina cheated and feels guilty.”

Revised: “Mina’s private restoration of the old record contradicts her public smile, establishing discomfort while leaving its cause unresolved.”
