Lesson: Give Readers Evidence, Not Guesswork

Start Here — Read Slowly, Then Decide

Imagine you are helping a reader cross a stream. Your details are the stepping-stones. Too few, and the reader must leap without support. Too many explanations, and there is no thinking left to do. Our aim is a clear route with a meaningful question still open.

We will use five moves: observe → connect → compare → calibrate → design. “Calibrate” means adjust the strength of your claim to the strength of your evidence.

Step 1 — Separate the Fact from the Story You Add

Read: “Rae set out two cups. Each time footsteps passed the door, she looked up.” First, I name only what a camera could record: two cups, passing footsteps, and repeated looking. Next I connect the actions: she may be expecting someone. I cannot yet say she is waiting for her father to apologize. That adds a relationship and an event.

ObservationSupported inferenceSpeculation
Rae sets out two cups and repeatedly looks toward the door.Rae may be waiting for company.Her father will arrive to apologize tonight.

Teacher thinking: The second cup alone has several explanations. Repeated attention to footsteps makes expected company more plausible. I use both clues rather than treating one object as proof.

Pause and Try

“Rae looks up when footsteps pass” and “Rae fears abandonment”: which is an observation? What would the other claim need?

Pause: say or jot your reasoning before opening the explanation. This is optional, ungraded practice—not a submission.

Check your thinking

Looking up is stated. Fear of abandonment is a specific interpretation that needs additional evidence; the two cups and looking may support expectation, but not that exact fear.

Step 2 — Build an Evidence Ladder, Not a Clue Count

MoveQuestion to askExample
CollectWhat is actually stated?Two cups; repeated looking; footsteps.
RankWhich details are relevant to this particular claim?Looking up at each passing person is more relevant to waiting than the cup’s color would be.
ConnectWhy do these details matter together?A place prepared for another person plus attention to arrivals supports expectation.
CheckDoes a detail challenge my reading?If Rae then puts both cups into a display cabinet, another explanation becomes stronger.

Evidence convergence means different relevant clues point toward the same conclusion. Repeating the same quotation three times is not three independent clues. Evidence hierarchy is your ranking of evidence for a particular claim; the last detail is not automatically the strongest.

Pause and Choose: Which Inference Holds Up?

Model: “At every rehearsal, Kit placed two scripts on the desk. Tonight he placed one, checked the empty chair, and left the second script inside his bag.”

Which claim connects the strongest evidence without inventing a backstory?

A. Kit may be uncertain whether a partner will join him, because the usual pair of scripts changes while he checks the empty chair.
✓ Correct—better supported. This response connects the repeated routine, the changed number of scripts, and Kit’s attention to the chair. “May” calibrates the claim because the partner’s identity and reason for absence remain unknown.
B. Kit’s partner has quit permanently after an argument, because there is only one script on the desk.
Not the best inference. This answer adds quitting, permanence, and an argument. It also ignores Kit checking the chair. Keep the supported idea of a changed expectation, but remove the invented history.

Retry: If your first choice is weaker, close it and select the other choice before continuing. Compare both explanations so you can name why the stronger answer works.

Step 3 — Compare a Rival and Calibrate the Claim

For Kit, a rival reading is that he is simply organizing materials. That explains keeping a script in a bag, but it accounts less well for checking the empty chair after a repeated two-script routine. Uncertainty about another person is a stronger inference—not a certainty.

Claim strengthUse it whenExample
Establishes / statesThe text directly supplies the fact.The text establishes that one script remains in the bag.
Suggests / strongly suggestsSeveral relevant clues support a conclusion.The changed routine suggests an altered expectation.
May suggestThe evidence supports a possibility but leaves alternatives.Kit may be unsure whether a partner will arrive.
Does not establishA needed fact is absent.The passage does not establish an argument.

A rival must be plausible, not deliberately silly. Ask which reading explains more evidence with fewer added facts. Sometimes two readings remain similarly supported. State that limit instead of forcing a winner.

Pause and Choose: Calibrate the Claim

Evidence: Kit places one script on the desk, leaves another in his bag, and checks an empty chair.

Which sentence matches the strength of the evidence?

A. The passage establishes that Kit’s partner will not attend rehearsal.
Too certain. The actions create an expectation and uncertainty, but the passage never states whether anyone will arrive.
B. The changed routine strongly suggests that Kit is uncertain about another person’s arrival.
✓ Correct—well calibrated. “Strongly suggests” recognizes that several clues converge while preserving the missing fact of whether a partner will actually arrive.

Retry: If your first choice is weaker, close it and select the other choice before continuing. Compare both explanations so you can name why the stronger answer works.

Step 4 — Use Four Writer Choices Deliberately

ChoiceWhat you controlReader effect to test
SelectionInclude the unused script rather than an unrelated wall color.Does the clue support the intended question?
SequenceShow the usual pair before the changed action.Does the reader notice the break?
OmissionLeave the partner’s history unstated.Is the situation clear even though the cause is uncertain?
ContradictionHave Kit say “Nothing has changed” after moving one script.Does speech conflict with action and invite scrutiny?

A contradiction is an actual conflict between statements or details. A surprising action may merely complicate a reading; it does not automatically prove dishonesty. Likewise, one event happening before another can invite a causal inference, but sequence alone does not prove cause.

Pause and Try

Compare “The bell rang. Sol hid the note” with “Sol hid the note. The bell rang.” What changes—and what is still unknown?

Pause: say or jot your reasoning before opening the explanation. This is optional, ungraded practice—not a submission.

Check your thinking

Bell first may make hiding seem like a response to it. Hiding first initially presents the action without that trigger. Neither order proves why Sol hides the note or what it says.

Step 5 — Design, Test, and Refine an Original Scene

Let me build a short scene. My intended inference is that a student hesitates to give up a responsibility. I need actions readers can connect, not a sentence announcing hesitation.

  1. Select two converging clues: a returned equipment box and a hand still holding its label.
  2. Add one complicating clue: the student leaves the sign-out card unsigned.
  3. Arrange the clues: show the return before the unfinished paperwork.
  4. Leave a precise unknown: why the responsibility is changing.
  5. Read as a skeptical reader: do the actions support hesitation, or am I relying on history only I know?

Complete model: “Jules carried the camera case back to the media room. He set it on the return shelf but kept his fingers around the name label. The sign-out card lay beneath his pen. When the lights flickered off for closing, he placed the pen beside the unsigned line.”

Reasoning: Returning the case points toward ending the responsibility; holding the label and leaving the line unsigned complicate that ending. The situation is understandable, but the reason is open.

Refinement: A weak ending, “He was uncertain because he did not want to quit,” would settle what the actions already suggest. The unsigned line preserves inference. Do not remove every explanation from every story—remove one only when purposeful details can do that work.

Ambiguity Is Not Confusion

Intentional ambiguity: the reader knows who does what, and two interpretations have concrete support. Confusion: unclear actions or missing context prevent any supported interpretation.

Overdetermination, as used in this lesson: so much explicit explanation that an intended inference becomes unnecessary. “More clues” is not automatically bad. Ask whether a detail adds a new relationship or only repeats an already obvious conclusion.

Use this flexible reasoning frame: The details ___ and ___ suggest ___ because ___. A plausible alternative is ___. The text does not establish ___. Changing ___ would shift the reader’s inference by ___.
