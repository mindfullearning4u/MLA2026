Worked Example: Three Synthesis Decisions

Example 1 — Aligning Voice, Inference, and Audience

Draft: “Obviously, Aunt Sol had hidden the deed because she feared the family would sell the orchard.”

  1. Goal: let readers suspect protection without certainty.
  2. Diagnosis: “Obviously” and the explicit motive eliminate inference; the voice sounds analytical rather than dramatic.
  3. Revision: “Aunt Sol filed every tax receipt by year. The deed she folded into the flour tin beneath a layer of sugar.”
  4. Evidence: orderly records contrast with secret placement.
  5. Warrant: the contradiction makes concealment deliberate while leaving motive open.
  6. Tradeoff: clarity of motive decreases; reader participation increases.
  7. Success criterion: readers infer intentional concealment but propose more than one plausible reason.

Example 2 — Using Feedback Without Losing Voice

Writer goal: a bilingual narrator should sound intimate and slightly withholding.

Feedback: one reader asks for all phrases translated; another cannot identify who speaks in paragraph two.

  1. Sort feedback: full translation is preference/access data; speaker confusion is a verifiable comprehension problem.
  2. Revision choice: preserve untranslated terms supported by context, but add one concrete speaker tag in paragraph two.
  3. Rationale: the revision repairs reference without flattening the narrator’s linguistic voice.
  4. Tradeoff: added tag slightly reduces ambiguity but protects the intended intimacy.
  5. Success criterion: readers identify speakers while still experiencing cultural and emotional meaning through context.

Example 3 — Revising Structure by Hypothesis

Original order: fire alarm → character hides photograph → reader learns photograph shows missing sibling.

Hypothesis: revealing the sibling before the alarm will shift the scene from mystery to dread.

  1. Version A effect: hiding first invites broad questions about the photograph.
  2. Version B effect: early recognition makes every later action emotionally specific.
  3. Counterevidence: Version B may overdetermine the character’s motive.
  4. Decision: reveal only a matching birthmark before the alarm; delay the relationship.
  5. Warrant: partial evidence narrows interpretation without closing it.
  6. Success criterion: readers sense personal danger before learning the exact relationship.

Complete Model Rationale

The three decisions integrate writer goal, craft analysis, inference control, feedback interpretation, revision hypothesis, tradeoff, and measurable success. Synthesis means the decisions support one another rather than appearing as separate checklist items.

Common Failure and Revision

Weak reflection: “I added details and fixed grammar because feedback helped.”

Strong reflection: “Readers consistently misidentified the speaker in paragraph two. I added one action tag rather than translating the dialogue, which repaired reference while preserving the narrator’s bilingual register.”
