Worked Example: Three Complete Synthesis Decisions

How to Use These Models

Read each original before the explanation. Ask what should stay and what should change. These are short fictional models, so you will study three. The examples show decisions, not formulas that every story must obey.

Example 1 — Suggest Concealment without Announcing the Motive

Original: “Aunt Sol hid the orchard deed because she wanted to protect it from the family. She was secretive but loving.” A deed is a document recording property ownership; an orchard is land planted with fruit trees.

Goal: readers should suspect deliberate concealment, with protection as one possible motive rather than a settled fact.

  1. Diagnose: “because” supplies the motive; “secretive but loving” labels character. Neither lets readers weigh clues.
  2. Design two interacting clues: orderly records establish a pattern; hiding one document breaks it.
  3. Complete revision: “Aunt Sol filed every tax receipt by year. The orchard deed she folded into the flour tin beneath a layer of sugar. When my cousin asked for the folder, she brushed the white dust from her hands.”
  4. Explain: the contrast between filing and hiding makes the unusual placement seem deliberate. The dust links her later action to the hiding without proving what she intends.
  5. Test the limit: protection, fear of a sale, and self-interest remain possible. The text does not yet establish love as the only motive.

Model rationale: “I replaced motive labels with an orderly pattern and a concealed exception. The receipt/deed contrast invites suspicion through action, and the dust connects the actions. This asks readers to infer more and gives them less certainty about motive. I would ask what seems deliberate and which alternative reason remains possible.”

Example 2 — Repair Reference without Flattening Voice

Complete original: “Abuela lifted the flood-marked chair. ‘The river keeps receipts,’ she said. I brought the paint. ‘Leave that line,’ she said. She set the brush down.” Abuela means grandmother in Spanish; the dialogue is supplied in English. The flood mark is a line left by high water.

Goal: preserve the grandmother’s familiar expression while making it clear that the narrator decides not to paint over the mark.

Supplied feedback: “I can use the flood mark to understand the river’s ‘receipts,’ but I cannot tell who sets down the brush. Would naming the person there help?”

  1. Sort: the reader reports a specific reference problem, not a demand to remove the family expression.
  2. Choose the smallest useful change: replace the final “She” with “I.” Do not translate away a metaphor already supported by context.
  3. Complete revision: “Abuela lifted the flood-marked chair. ‘The river keeps receipts,’ she said. I brought the paint. ‘Leave that line,’ she said. I set the brush down.”
  4. Explain the effect: the final first-person action shows the narrator responding to the request. The river’s “receipts” can still suggest traces of the past.
  5. Check the cost: the actor becomes explicit, but the narrator’s full reasons remain unstated.

Model response to feedback: “You are asking who puts down the brush, not asking me to remove Abuela’s expression. I changed the last pronoun to ‘I’ so the narrator’s choice is visible. That reduces reference uncertainty while leaving the metaphor and motives open. Can you now locate the narrator’s response and quote the word that identifies the actor?”

Example 3 — Test What a Change in Sequence Actually Does

Version A: “The fire alarm sounded. Eli slid a photograph into his sleeve. In the stairwell, he opened his hand: the picture showed his missing sister beside the locked laboratory door.” A laboratory is a place for scientific work. The scene supplies no cause for the alarm.

Goal: let readers feel personal stakes during the concealment, without establishing who caused the alarm.

  1. Map information: Version A introduces alarm and hiding before identifying the person in the photograph.
  2. Predict: moving the photograph’s identity earlier may make the hiding feel personally significant immediately.
  3. Version B: “Eli studied the photograph of his missing sister beside the locked laboratory door. The fire alarm sounded. He slid the photograph into his sleeve and joined the line for the stairs.”
  4. Compare: B gives readers the connection before the alarm; A delays recognition. B shifts some attention from “What is hidden?” to “What will happen to this evidence?”
  5. Test counterclaims: neither version proves Eli caused the alarm or knows where his sister is. The photograph may be old.

Model decision: “I choose B because the reader knows the personal connection when Eli hides the photograph. Earlier identification gives up A’s delayed reveal but makes the concealment more specific. I would ask what the reader knows before the alarm and what remains uncertain afterward. The answer should distinguish the sister connection from unproved claims about the alarm.”
