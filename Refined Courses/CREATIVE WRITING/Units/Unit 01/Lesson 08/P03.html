Direct Teaching: The Integrated Writing Cycle

Stage 1 — Define the Contract

Name the audience, purpose, intended reader effect, and boundaries of knowledge. A draft cannot be evaluated well until the writer states what it is trying to accomplish.

Stage 2 — Design Voice and Tone

Select diction, syntax, detail, perspective, and register that create a coherent voice. Track tone shifts deliberately. Ask whether the language fits both the speaker and the audience without erasing linguistic identity.

Stage 3 — Build Inference Architecture

Decide what readers should know, infer, question, and misunderstand at each point. Arrange converging clues, counterevidence, omission, and sequence so uncertainty is calibrated rather than accidental.

Stage 4 — Analyze the Draft

Write a craft claim. Select precise and representative evidence. State the warrant. Test counterevidence. Compare the draft with a mentor-text principle without copying surface features.

Stage 5 — Request and Interpret Feedback

Tell reviewers the intended effect and ask focused questions. Sort feedback into: comprehension evidence, reader-effect evidence, preference, and actionable suggestion. Repair misunderstandings and preserve writer ownership.

Stage 6 — Revise by Hypothesis

Do not “make it better” generally. Predict a result: “If I delay the photograph’s description, suspicion will rise before recognition.” Revise, compare versions, and judge the outcome against a success criterion.

Stage 7 — Defend and Reflect

Explain the final decision with a claim–evidence–warrant chain, acknowledge a tradeoff or counterevidence, and identify the next growth target. Reflection should analyze process, not merely report effort.
