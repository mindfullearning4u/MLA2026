Inventory of Small Losses

On the final Saturday before the store closed, Niko’s mother gave every customer a numbered card. “For the auction,” she explained, although no auctioneer had arrived and the shelves still held the same dented kettles, seed packets, and jars of screws they had sold for thirty-two years.

Niko wrote numbers on objects no one had requested: the brass bell above the door, the stool his grandfather repaired with blue wire, the ledger containing names instead of account numbers. His mother removed each card after he walked away.

At noon, Mrs. Alvarez brought in a toaster that had not worked since 2009. “Your father said he would find the part,” she told Niko’s mother.

“He was an optimist,” his mother said.

Mrs. Alvarez set the toaster beside the register. “Then keep it until he proves us wrong.”

Niko waited for his mother to refuse. Instead, she wrote 47 on a card and tied it to the cord.

By four, former customers had returned a lamp, a window fan, two clocks, and a red bicycle missing its chain. Each object carried a promise his father had made before the hospital, before the winter when the CLOSED sign stayed in the window for six weeks.

“These are not inventory,” Niko said.

His mother opened the ledger. Beside every returned object, she wrote a name. “No,” she said. “They are not.”

At sunset, Niko took the card from the bell and rang it once. The sound was smaller than he remembered, but people on the sidewalk stopped. His mother turned the sign from OPEN to CLOSED, then unlocked the door.

Part A — Integrated Analysis

  1. Write a 110–140 word accurate paraphrase.
  2. State the passage’s audience contract and intended effect.
  3. Analyze how the numbered-card pattern changes meaning.
  4. Explain how the mother’s tone differs in her two short replies.
  5. Make a calibrated inference about the father using four converging details.
  6. Identify counterevidence or uncertainty that limits your claim.
  7. Analyze the returned objects as compression and community evidence.
  8. Explain the final contradiction: turning the sign to CLOSED and unlocking the door.

Part B — Evidence and Revision

  1. Write a craft claim about structure, select three representative details, and state the warrant.
  2. Identify one isolated detail that would be insufficient evidence by itself.
  3. Propose two interpretations of the final bell and evaluate which is stronger.
  4. Add one sentence that would overdetermine the father’s fate. Explain why it weakens the piece.
  5. Propose a revision that sharpens the intended effect without resolving the central uncertainty.
  6. Write a revision hypothesis, tradeoff, and measurable success criterion.

Part C — Original Synthesis

  1. Write a polished 350–450 word scene for an adult or young-adult audience. It must include a distinctive voice, controlled tone shift, meaningful object pattern, evidence-based inference, counterevidence, and an ending that changes the pattern.
  2. Write a 120–160 word author’s note stating audience, purpose, intended effect, and three major craft decisions.
  3. Create a focused feedback request with three questions, including one access or clarity question.
  4. Write a revision rationale explaining one change you made, the evidence behind it, a tradeoff, and a success criterion.
