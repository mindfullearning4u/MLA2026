Direct Teaching: From Evidence to Revision

The Six-Step Reasoning Method

  1. Define the intended effect: name what the passage is trying to make the reader notice, feel, infer, or anticipate.
  2. Make a focused claim: identify the choice and its function.
  3. Select evidence: use exact words plus a broader pattern or structural feature.
  4. State the warrant: explain the mechanism connecting choice to effect.
  5. Test counterevidence: locate details that weaken or complicate the claim.
  6. Decide and verify: revise, compare versions, and judge which better serves the intended effect.

Evidence Quality Test

Strong evidence is relevant to the precise claim, sufficient across more than one detail when needed, representative of the passage, and precise enough for another reader to verify. A vivid quotation may still be weak if it is isolated or unrelated to the claim.

Write the Warrant

Claim: The syntax accelerates the escape.

Evidence: Three coordinated verbs—“grabbed, turned, ran”—appear without description.

Warrant: Removing pauses between actions compresses decision and motion, so the reader processes events at the speed of the character’s reaction.

The warrant names the mechanism. Without it, the quotation is decoration rather than proof.

Counterevidence and Qualification

A passage may mostly create speed but pause for one long reflective sentence. Do not ignore that counterevidence. Revise “The scene is fast” to “The scene accelerates during the escape, then deliberately pauses at the threshold.” Qualification makes the claim more accurate, not weaker.

Causation Requires Comparison

To claim that a choice causes an effect, compare alternatives. If replacing fragments with one complex sentence reduces urgency, that contrast supports a causal claim. Avoid assuming every reader reaction comes from a single device; effects often arise from interacting choices and context.

Turn Analysis Into Revision

  1. Identify the current evidence pattern.
  2. Predict what one revision will change.
  3. Draft the revision.
  4. Compare original and revised versions using the intended effect.
  5. Keep, reject, or refine the change with a written rationale.

Reasoning Frame

The passage aims to ____. The choice ____ supports that effect, as shown by ____ and ____. These details matter because ____. However, ____ complicates the claim, so a more accurate conclusion is ____. Revising ____ to ____ would likely ____; success should be judged by ____.
