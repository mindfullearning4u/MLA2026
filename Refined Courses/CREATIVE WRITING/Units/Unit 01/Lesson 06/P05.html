Independent Work: “The Quiet Frequency”

Directions

Read the complete passage and answer all 16 questions in complete sentences. Use precise, representative evidence. Submit these responses with Page 2 vocabulary as Notebook Evidence.

The Quiet Frequency

Every evening at 8:15, Lena’s father tuned the workshop radio between stations. Static filled the room while he sharpened tools, labeled jars, and checked the lock on the back window. He called the noise “weather from far away.”

After he died, Lena left the dial where his hand had placed it. For three months she worked in silence around the radio, restoring the wooden boats he had never finished. She replaced split hulls and faded paint but refused to repair their missing names.

In April, a voice surfaced through the static: numbers, then coordinates, then her father’s call sign. Lena dropped the brush. The voice repeated once and vanished beneath a weather report.

She recorded the next six evenings. At 8:15 there was only static. On the seventh, the call sign returned, followed by a date two years old.

Her cousin Malik listened to the recording. “It could be a delayed relay,” he said. “Or interference.”

“He stopped transmitting four years ago.”

Malik studied the boats lined across the bench. “Then why are you still listening?”

Lena turned the radio off. The sudden quiet made the workshop seem larger, every unfinished hull separated from the next.

The following evening, she arrived at 8:14 carrying a soldering kit. She removed the radio’s back panel, replaced one corroded connection, and tuned to the licensed rescue frequency her father had actually used. At 8:15, a coast-guard dispatcher read the ordinary marine forecast.

Lena wrote the date on the first restored boat. She did not give it a name.

Part A — Claims and Evidence

  1. Write a 100–130 word accurate paraphrase.
  2. State the passage’s likely intended emotional effect.
  3. Make a focused claim about the repeated 8:15 pattern.
  4. Select two precise details and one structural pattern that support the claim.
  5. Write the warrant connecting those details to the effect.

Part B — Quality and Counterevidence

  1. Rank the three strongest pieces of evidence for Lena’s grief-related avoidance. Explain the ranking criteria.
  2. Identify counterevidence that complicates a claim that Lena simply wants to hear her father.
  3. Explain how Malik’s explanation changes the evidentiary status of the voice.
  4. Evaluate whether the repaired radio resolves the mystery. Distinguish fact from inference.
  5. Analyze the final unnamed boat as representative evidence. Does it support change, continued uncertainty, or both?

Part C — Revision Reasoning

  1. Evaluate the causal claim: “Repairing the radio causes Lena to accept her father’s death.” What supports and limits it?
  2. Add one sentence that would overexplain the ending, then explain why it weakens the passage.
  3. Propose a different revision that strengthens the intended effect without resolving every ambiguity.
  4. Write a complete revision rationale with predicted reader effect and a success criterion.

Part D — Craft Transfer

  1. Write a 180–220 word original scene built around a repeated behavior, one piece of uncertain evidence, counterevidence, and a final action that changes the character’s relationship to uncertainty. Do not copy the radio, workshop, boats, or bereavement situation.
  2. Present a claim–evidence–warrant analysis of your scene, address one counterevidence detail, and justify one revision you made.
