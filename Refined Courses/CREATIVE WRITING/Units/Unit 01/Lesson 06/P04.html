Worked Example: Three Evidence-Based Decisions

Example 1 — Testing a Pacing Claim

Original: “The elevator stopped. The lights blinked. A cable struck the shaft, once, then again. Mara counted to five before she breathed.”

  1. Intended effect: controlled panic.
  2. Claim: fragments create urgency while the final measured action shows attempted control.
  3. Evidence: three brief event units; repeated impact; deliberate “counted to five.”
  4. Warrant: compressed syntax accelerates external danger, but the slower final clause shifts attention to Mara regulating herself.
  5. Counterevidence: “once, then again” slightly slows the sound, so the passage is not uniformly fast.
  6. Qualified analysis: Syntax alternates shock with controlled delay, producing panic that Mara actively contains.
  7. Revision test: Combining every action into one sentence weakens the jolting rhythm; retain the fragments.

Example 2 — Challenging a Symbol Claim

Passage: “Theo carried the cracked compass through three cities. At the border, he traded it for bread without looking back.”

  1. Initial claim: The compass symbolizes permanent attachment to home.
  2. Supporting evidence: he keeps it through repeated travel.
  3. Counterevidence: he ultimately exchanges it for an immediate need and does not look back.
  4. Evaluation: “permanent attachment” is too absolute.
  5. Revised claim: The compass represents a connection he values until survival requires release.
  6. Warrant: duration gives the object emotional weight; exchange establishes a limit to that attachment.
  7. Revision decision: Adding “sadly” would overexplain the conflict; the action already supplies evidence.

Example 3 — Using Feedback as Evidence

Draft: “The courtroom emptied. Inez stacked every paper except the unsigned statement, which she folded into her sleeve.”

Feedback: Reader A infers concealment; Reader B thinks the paper is simply important.

  1. Intended effect: concealment with uncertain motive.
  2. Evidence in draft: selective stacking, “unsigned,” and hidden placement in a sleeve.
  3. Diagnosis: both readers noticed importance, but only one reached concealment; the sleeve clue may be too subtle for consistent effect.
  4. Revision: “She waited until the bailiff turned away before folding the unsigned statement into her sleeve.”
  5. Rationale: the added timing provides behavioral evidence of secrecy without naming motive.
  6. Success criterion: new readers should infer concealment while disagreeing reasonably about why.

Synthesis

Each example makes a claim, uses more than one kind of evidence, states the warrant, addresses counterevidence or reader data, and makes a revision decision tied to a measurable intended effect.

Common Failure and Revision

Weak: “The short sentences are effective because they are dramatic.”

Strong: “The fragments isolate each mechanical failure, forcing the reader to process danger in separate jolts; the later counting clause then shows Mara imposing control.”
