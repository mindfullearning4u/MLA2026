Worked Example: Three Evidence-Based Decisions

Watch a Writer Think, Test, and Decide

Each example shows the complete draft, an initial claim, exact evidence, a warrant, a challenge, and a visible revision comparison. Notice that I sometimes repair the claim rather than rewrite the scene. Better reasoning does not always require more words.

Example 1 — Test a Pacing Claim

Original: “The elevator stopped. The lights blinked. A cable struck the shaft, once, then again. Mara counted to five before she breathed.” The shaft is the vertical space in which the elevator travels.

1. Set a goal: suggest danger interrupted by Mara’s attempt at control.

2. Name the evidence accurately: “The elevator stopped” and “The lights blinked” are short complete sentences, not fragments. “Once, then again” spaces the impacts; “counted to five” names deliberate delay.

3. State a warrant: separate sentence boundaries present mechanical failures as distinct beats. The counting shifts attention from external events to Mara regulating her response. This may create alternating shock and delay, not uniform speed.

4. Test the first claim: “The fragments make everything fast” fails twice: the grammar label is wrong, and counting is counterevidence to “everything fast.” I narrow the claim before revising.

5. Compare an actual alternative: “The elevator stopped, and the lights blinked. A cable struck the shaft, once, then again. Mara counted to five before she breathed.”

6. Decide: Only the first boundary changes. The combined version links the failures more smoothly; the original isolates them. I would retain the original for distinct shocks, while acknowledging that some readers may experience its stops as slower. The trade-off is a more segmented rhythm.

Success check: Ask where a reader notices separate failures and where the scene pauses. Do not ask the leading question “Wasn’t the original scarier?”

Example 2 — Revise the Claim Before Revising the Symbol

Original: “Theo carried the cracked compass through three cities. At the border, he traded it for bread without looking back.” A compass indicates direction; a border separates places or territories.

1. Initial claim: “The compass proves permanent attachment to home.” That sounds literary, but where does the text identify home? It does not.

2. Evidence: carrying it through three cities suggests continued usefulness or value. Trading it for bread shows a changed priority. “Without looking back” complicates a claim of visibly reluctant release.

3. Warrant: duration establishes that the object has stayed with Theo; exchanging it for food sets a limit on keeping it. Emotional attachment is possible, but practical usefulness is a plausible alternative.

4. Qualified claim: “The retained object becomes exchangeable when an immediate need takes priority.” This fits both sentences without inventing the compass’s origin or Theo’s feelings.

5. Test a revision for a chosen goal: Suppose I want to make the cost of parting more visible. Original: “At the border, he traded it for bread without looking back.” Revision: “At the border, he traded it for bread, then traced the empty circle it had worn into his pocket.”

6. Compare and decide: The new tactile action draws attention to absence after the exchange. I would use it for lingering awareness; the trade-off is losing the original’s brisk departure. It still does not identify home or prove regret.

Success check: Ask what the final action makes a reader notice after the trade. If the goal were decisive release instead, the original might serve it better.

Example 3 — Evaluate Feedback without Treating It as a Vote

Original: “The courtroom emptied. Inez stacked every paper except the unsigned statement, which she folded into her sleeve.” A bailiff is a courtroom officer; an unsigned statement lacks a signature.

Supplied feedback: Reader A infers concealment. Reader B notices that the paper is important but is unsure about secrecy. These are two observations about reader response, not proof of how all readers react.

1. Set a goal: make concealment more noticeable while leaving its motive open.

2. Locate evidence and warrant: separating one paper from the stack and placing it in a sleeve make it less visible. The selection marks importance; the placement may suggest hiding.

3. Diagnose cautiously: Reader B’s response suggests that secrecy may need a stronger cue for this reader. I do not diagnose the reader as wrong or claim that two comments establish a universal failure.

4. Complete revised version: “The courtroom emptied. Inez stacked every paper except the unsigned statement. She waited until the bailiff turned away before folding it into her sleeve.”

5. Explain the mechanism: timing the action to another person looking away connects concealment with avoiding observation. The added beat clarifies behavior without naming the motive.

6. Name the trade-off: the revision makes secrecy less subtle and adds length. It changes both timing detail and sentence structure, so I cannot attribute the whole effect to punctuation alone.

Success check: Ask a fresh reader what Inez appears to keep from view and what remains unknown. A successful response identifies concealment but does not need to agree on fear, guilt, or protection. If no reader is available, describe this as a proposed check, not a completed test.
