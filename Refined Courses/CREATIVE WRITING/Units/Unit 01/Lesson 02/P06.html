Independent Work: Analyze, Reshape, Explain

Purpose and Directions

Apply the lesson skills by identifying how voice, tone, and purpose develop, choosing the stronger analysis, reshaping one scene, and explaining your craft decisions. Complete each numbered task and submit it with your vocabulary notes in Notebook Evidence Submission.

Quality matters more than length: use precise evidence and purposeful choices.

Draft for Workshop: “The Message”

At 6:14, the phone lit beneath the stack of unopened mail. Can we talk?

Rina read the message twice. The kitchen clock clicked with the confidence of something that had never made a mistake. She placed the phone face down, then turned it over again. Outside, the neighbor’s sprinkler swept silver arcs across an empty lawn.

She typed Sure, erased it, and typed About what? The second question looked too sharp. She erased that too. When the clock reached 6:16, Rina carried the phone to the window as if better light might change the words.

1. Notice and Plan

Complete the organizer with brief notes, then explain in two or three sentences what Rina does and what the details reasonably suggest.

Exact detailCraft choiceEffect on tone or purpose
   

2. Choose the Stronger Analysis

Record A or B, then explain in two or three sentences why your choice better connects evidence, craft, and effect. The answer is not revealed on this page.

A. “The tone is nervous because Rina is nervous and does not answer the message.”
B. “The repeated typing and erasing, the impatient clock, and the comparison about better light create a hesitant, self-conscious tone because each choice shows delay without explaining the message’s mystery.”

3. Apply the Craft in Your Writing

Choose hopeful anticipation or comic embarrassment. Write one goal sentence naming your audience, purpose, and intended tone. Then reshape the scene in six to eight sentences.

Keep the central event: Rina receives “Can we talk?”, considers a reply, and has not sent it by the end. Change at least two coordinated choices, such as diction, syntax, imagery, or selected details. Do not solve the mystery or replace it with a different plot.

Revise one sentence from your new scene. Explain how its diction, syntax, or detail better creates the intended tone for your audience.
