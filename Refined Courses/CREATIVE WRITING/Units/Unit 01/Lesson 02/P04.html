Worked Example: One Event, Three Purposes

Shared Event

A student spills water on a shirt immediately before an important interview. Each model changes voice and tone through several coordinated craft choices.

Example 1 — Humorous

Model: “Naturally, the water chose the exact moment before my interview to leap from the cup and introduce itself to my shirt. I stared at the spreading stain. It had excellent timing and no professional boundaries.”

  1. Purpose: Make embarrassment entertaining.
  2. Voice: Witty and self-aware.
  3. Evidence: Personification (“chose,” “introduce itself”), mock-formal diction (“professional boundaries”), and dry understatement.
  4. Effect: The narrator controls the embarrassment by joking about it.

Model analysis: The humorous tone comes from treating the water as an inconsiderate coworker. The formal phrase “professional boundaries” contrasts with the ordinary accident, while the short final sentence sharpens the joke.

Example 2 — Suspenseful

Model: “The cup tipped. Water crossed the table toward the folder marked FINAL INTERVIEW. I caught the folder first. The stain on my shirt could wait; the footsteps outside the door could not.”

  1. Purpose: Create urgency before an approaching encounter.
  2. Voice: Controlled but pressured.
  3. Evidence: Short sentences, a concrete threat to the folder, and the contrast between waiting and approaching footsteps.
  4. Effect: The reader prioritizes the danger with the narrator and anticipates the door opening.

Model analysis: The suspense depends on pacing and withheld information, not on saying “I was nervous.” The final semicolon connects two pressures and produces a deliberate escalation.

Example 3 — Reflective

Model: “The water darkened my shirt exactly where a medal used to hang during school ceremonies. I had spent years preparing to look qualified. Watching the stain spread, I wondered whether the interview might reveal something preparation could not cover.”

  1. Purpose: Connect the accident to identity and uncertainty.
  2. Voice: Introspective and candid.
  3. Evidence: Symbolic comparison, longer syntax, abstract reflection, and the contrast between appearance and revelation.
  4. Effect: The accident becomes a prompt for self-examination.

Model analysis: The reflective tone develops because the narrator interprets the stain rather than reacting only to it. The final sentence broadens a physical problem into a question about identity.

Comparative Reasoning

The event remains the same, but the purpose changes what the narrator notices. Humor notices absurdity, suspense notices immediate threat, and reflection notices symbolic meaning. Strong tone revision changes the entire pattern of diction, syntax, detail, and thought—not merely one adjective.
