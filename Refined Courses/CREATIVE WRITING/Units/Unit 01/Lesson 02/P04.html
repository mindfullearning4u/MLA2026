Worked Example: One Event, Three Purposes

Watch the Decisions, Not Just the Finished Answer

Shared event: A student spills water on a shirt immediately before an important interview. We will keep that event recognizable while changing how the narrator presents it.

Follow the same cycle in each model: goal → plan two choices → draft → explain → test a revision. Notice that craft decisions serve the goal; we are not adding decorative language simply to sound impressive.

Example 1 — Make Embarrassment Humorous

1. Set the goal. I want fellow students to laugh with a self-aware narrator. I am not trying to make the interview seem unimportant.

2. Plan the choices. I will personify the water as an inconsiderate guest and contrast a small accident with official-sounding language. This is mock-formal diction: exaggerated formality for comic effect.

3. Write the complete model. “Naturally, the water chose the exact moment before my interview to leap from the cup and introduce itself to my shirt. I stared at the spreading stain. It had excellent timing and no professional boundaries.”

4. Explain the pattern. “Chose” and “introduce itself” make the spill seem deliberately rude. “Professional boundaries” treats water as if it could follow workplace rules. The short final sentence delivers the joke after the longer setup. The voice is witty and self-aware; the tone toward the accident is wryly frustrated.

5. Test a weaker revision. “I was really embarrassed and the water was funny” merely names reactions. It removes the contrast that lets readers experience humor. I would keep the personification and cut that explanation.

Example 3 — Turn an Accident into Reflection

1. Set the goal. For a reflective personal narrative, I want readers to consider the difference between looking prepared and being prepared. Reflective means thoughtfully examining meaning.

2. Plan the choices. I will connect the stain to a remembered medal and let a longer final sentence follow the narrator’s questioning thought.

3. Write the complete model. “The water darkened my shirt exactly where a medal used to hang during school ceremonies. I had spent years preparing to look qualified. Watching the stain spread, I wondered whether the interview might reveal something preparation could not cover.”

4. Explain the pattern. The medal connects appearance with public recognition. “Look qualified” raises doubt about the difference between appearance and ability. “I wondered whether” qualifies the thought rather than announcing a certainty. The voice is introspective—examining its own thoughts—and the tone is thoughtful and uncertain.

5. Test a revision. “The interview would prove I was a failure” goes further than the model’s evidence and removes its openness. I would keep the question rather than force a conclusion the narrator has not earned.

Compare the Craft Decisions

PurposeTwo choices doing the workReader invitation
HumorPersonification + mock-formal dictionLaugh with the narrator’s embarrassment.
UrgencyApproaching footsteps + compressed syntaxAnticipate what happens when the door opens.
ReflectionMedal comparison + qualified thoughtQuestion what preparation can and cannot control.

Model craft rationale: I kept the spill before the interview but changed the narrator’s focus from rude water to approaching footsteps. That concrete sound and the short opening sentence create pressure because the narrator has little time to respond. The revision serves suspense rather than humor without needing a new central event.

Check your thinking

Compare your revision with its goal. A useful explanation names your exact new phrase and what it changes for a reader. For example, adding “at the handle” after “footsteps” could make the arrival feel closer. It would not help the reflective model unless that model’s purpose also changed. You do not need to submit this practice.
