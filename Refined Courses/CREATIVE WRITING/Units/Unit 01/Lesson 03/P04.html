Worked Example: Notice, Explain, Revise

Watch the Teacher’s Decisions

We will use three short models, not three unrelated lists of devices. In each, I will establish the task, choose evidence, explain how two choices work together, and test a revision. Read the model answer only after following the reasoning that produces it.

Example 1 — Diction and Character

Text: “The dog crept toward the porch, stopping whenever the screen door sighed.”

1. Set the task. Explain how the description makes the approach uncertain. Then make it welcoming while keeping a dog approaching a porch and hearing a door.

2. Select evidence. I choose “crept” and “stopping whenever.” Both describe movement. I also notice “sighed,” but I will not claim that this alone proves danger.

3. Think through the connection. “Crept” suggests care or caution in this context. Repeated stopping reinforces hesitation. The door’s human-like “sigh” makes the ordinary sound seem significant to the dog.

4. Complete model analysis. The cautious connotation of “crept” is reinforced by the selected detail of repeated stopping. Personifying the door as something that “sighed” makes the setting seem responsive, so the approach feels uncertain. The sentence suggests hesitation without establishing its cause.

5. Write and defend the revision. “The dog trotted toward the porch and lifted its nose as the screen door whispered open.” “Trotted” gives the movement more ease, and lifting the nose suggests curiosity instead of stopping. “Whispered” keeps a soft sound. Together the diction and action invite a gentler reading, although context could still change that interpretation.

Example 2 — Imagery, Detail, and Information Order

Text: “Orange dust clung to the sill, and every footprint ended before the locked room.” A sill is the ledge at the bottom of a window.

1. Set the task. Explain how two setting details create a question. Do not solve the mystery.

2. Select evidence. I mark “orange dust” and the footprints ending before the locked room. The color creates visual imagery. The footprints are a selected trace of someone’s movement.

3. Test my first claim. I might write “The house has been empty for years.” But dust alone cannot prove how long it has been there, and footprints complicate a simple claim of emptiness. I will qualify the interpretation.

4. Complete model analysis. The visual imagery of “orange dust” suggests neglect, while the footprints indicate that someone has moved through the space at some point. Placing the “locked room” at the end directs attention to a boundary. These details invite a question about access rather than establish who was there or when.

5. Revise for reassurance. “Orange dust clung to the sill, but fresh footprints led to an open room where two cups steamed.” The contrasting conjunction “but,” the open room, and the warm sensory detail counter the initial neglect. This changes the setting details deliberately; it is not merely a synonym swap.

Example 3 — Syntax and the Timing of Discovery

Text: “One step. Then another. Then the door. Behind it, the radio continued playing to an empty room.”

1. Set the task. Explain how the sentence pattern controls the approach and discovery. Then create a more continuous movement without changing the empty room or playing radio.

2. Name the actual pattern. The first three units are fragments: incomplete sentences. “Then” repeats. The final sentence gives more information and includes a subject and verb.

3. Explain the effect. Each stop isolates a stage of the approach. The longer final sentence releases the discovery, but the radio playing without a visible listener leaves a question. An empty room is not proof of danger; the arrangement invites uncertainty.

4. Complete model analysis. The repeated fragments make readers attend to each step separately, slowing the approach in this passage. The structural decision to reveal the empty room last postpones the discovery. The radio’s continuing sound then makes that absence noticeable, sustaining uncertainty instead of fully resolving it.

5. Revise and compare. “I crossed to the door and opened it on a radio still playing in an empty room.” Linked actions replace the isolated stages, so the movement feels more continuous. The important facts stay, but the syntax reduces the stop-and-start approach. This revision changes pacing; it does not guarantee that the room now feels safe.
