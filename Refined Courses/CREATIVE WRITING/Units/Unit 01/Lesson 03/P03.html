Lesson: From Evidence to a Useful Craft Explanation

Start with What the Words Do

Imagine a classmate says, “Your paragraph has imagery.” That may be accurate, but it does not tell you what to preserve or improve. Today we will turn a label into useful reasoning. Read a short passage first for its situation. Only then choose the terms that help explain how it works.

Our goal is not to find a device in every sentence. It is to make a claim a reader can test against the text. We will move from evidence → accurate term → specific effect → purpose, then use that explanation to revise.

Step 1 — Separate What Happens from What You Infer

Read: “The dog crept toward the porch, stopping whenever the screen door sighed.” What happens? A dog approaches and repeatedly stops when the door makes a sound. What might we infer? The dog seems cautious. We do not know that someone has hurt it or that the house is dangerous. Keep that limit in mind: a convincing explanation does not need an invented backstory.

Step 2 — Quote Small; Name Precisely

EvidenceAccurate termWhy it fits
“crept”DictionThe writer selected a verb for slow or careful movement.
“crept” suggests caution hereConnotationThe association is supported by repeated stopping.
“the screen door sighed”Personification; figurative languageA nonhuman object is described with a human-like action.
The dog stops repeatedlySelected detailThis action supports our interpretation of caution.

Notice that one phrase can support more than one term. “Sighed” is diction and personification. Use the label that makes your reasoning clearer; do not stack labels to sound academic. Personification does not mean the door is literally alive.

Pause and Try

Would “The dog crept toward the porch, trying not to wake the baby” support exactly the same inference as the original?

Pause: say or jot your answer before opening the explanation. This is optional, ungraded practice, not a submission.

Check your thinking

No. “Crept” still denotes careful movement, but the added context supports consideration for the sleeping baby rather than fear of the door. Connotation depends on the surrounding evidence; a word is not a fixed code for one emotion.

Step 3 — Explain the Connection with Because

Now move past “This is personification.” Try: The door’s “sigh” makes the setting seem almost responsive to the dog. Because the dog stops at that sound, the description makes an ordinary approach feel uncertain. The second sentence connects two choices. It does not merely repeat the definition of personification.

Ask yourself: What specifically does the reader notice, question, anticipate, or infer? “It makes readers interested” is too general. “It makes readers anticipate what is behind the door” identifies an expectation the text supports. Say “may,” “suggests,” or “invites” when describing interpretations—not every reader reacts identically.

Pause and Choose: Evidence, Term, and Effect

Text: “The clock swallowed the last minute. I kept my hand on the unopened envelope.”

Which explanation would help a writer understand what to preserve? Read both complete responses, choose A or B, and then open your choice.

A. This is imagery, diction, syntax, and detail, so the writing is very powerful.
Not the best explanation. This answer stacks labels without showing where each one appears or what it does. “Powerful” is too vague to guide revision. A useful analysis selects relevant evidence, names the choice accurately, and explains a specific effect.
B. “Swallowed” treats time as something consuming the moment; the unopened envelope keeps information withheld, creating pressure.
✓ Correct—stronger explanation. This response quotes exact evidence, identifies figurative language and a selected detail, and explains how they work together. It stays within the evidence: pressure is supported, but the envelope’s contents remain unknown.

Reasoning chain: evidence → accurate term → specific effect → careful limit.

Step 4 — Distinguish Syntax from Structure

Syntax concerns how words and sentences are arranged. Structure concerns how parts of a passage are organized. They can overlap, but they are not interchangeable.

VersionWhat I noticeEffect I can justify
“I lifted the lid. Slowly.”The fragment isolates one adverb.The pause draws attention to hesitation; the short unit can slow this moment.
“I lifted the lid and saw the letter and reached for it before anyone could stop me.”Linked clauses accumulate in one sentence.The continuous chain can quicken this action rather than relax it.
A passage reveals the letter’s contents only in its final paragraph.Information is withheld until the end.The structure sustains a question across the passage.

A paragraph break is not automatically a sentence fragment. Before naming a fragment, check whether the sentence lacks a complete grammatical structure. Before saying “fast” or “slow,” explain what the arrangement makes you experience.

Pause and Choose: Syntax or Structure?

Observation: A story withholds the letter’s contents until its final paragraph.

Which explanation uses the most precise craft term and reasoning?

A. The syntax creates suspense because the letter comes at the end.
Not the best explanation. Syntax concerns the arrangement of words and sentences. This observation concerns where information appears across the whole passage, so the answer uses the wrong level of analysis.
B. The structure sustains suspense by delaying essential information until the final paragraph.
✓ Correct—precise reasoning. This answer identifies the passage-level organization as structure and explains how withholding information maintains a question for the reader.

Step 5 — Use Your Analysis to Revise

Start with “The corridor was quiet.” Suppose we want a welcoming rather than threatening atmosphere. First choose a target. Then coordinate two choices: “Warm light rested along the corridor, and someone had left a chair beside the open door.”

“Warm” and “rested” suggest comfort; the available chair and open door imply welcome. These choices reinforce each other. Changing only “quiet” to “nice” would name an attitude without giving the reader a concrete experience. Keep the central situation—a quiet corridor—while changing how it is presented.

Model rationale: I replaced the general adjective with visual imagery of warm light and selected the detail of an open door. Together they invite a reassuring interpretation because the space seems prepared for a visitor rather than closed against one.

Pause and Try

Change “The corridor was quiet” to suggest unease. Add one sensory detail and one action or object. Keep the corridor quiet.

Pause: say or jot your answer before opening the explanation. This is optional, ungraded practice, not a submission.

Check your thinking

One possibility: “Cold light trembled along the corridor; every door had been fastened from the outside.” The visual detail and unusual fastening may create unease without adding a loud event. “Trembled” personifies the light. Other versions can work: explain the effect your own evidence supports, and avoid claiming this is the only correct answer.

Step 6 — Test Your Terminology and Your Claim

  1. Point to it: Can I quote the exact evidence?
  2. Check the label: Does this term describe the choice, or am I guessing?
  3. Explain the relationship: How do my two choices reinforce or complicate each other?
  4. Respect the limit: Have I invented a fact the text does not establish?
  5. Read the revision: Does it create the intended effect through language, not just announce an emotion?

Common Mistakes — Repair the Explanation

Weak moveUseful repair
“It has lots of devices.”Choose two relevant choices, quote them, and explain their relationship.
“A long sentence always slows the pace.”Inspect this sentence’s clauses, punctuation, and action before naming an effect.
“The word means danger in every passage.”Use denotation to check meaning and context to justify an association.
“The author wants every reader to feel scared.”Describe a likely purpose with evidence and allow for different responses.
“I corrected spelling, so I changed the style.”Editing fixes mechanics; revision changes meaning or effect. Explain an actual craft change.
