Worked Example: Three Complete Communication Repairs

Follow the Decision, Not Just the Finished Answer

In each model, watch me identify the goal, locate exact evidence, check an assumption, and choose language that preserves the writer’s thinking. The examples move from academic directions to voice feedback to a difficult workshop exchange. All drafts and comments needed for the analysis appear here.

Example 1 — Clarify an Academic Direction

Complete miniature draft: “You know how stations empty after midnight. At noon, I stepped onto the platform and found every bench vacant.” A platform is the area beside a train track where passengers wait.

Original task: “Analyze how the narrator’s assumption about the audience shapes the effect of the time contrast.” The task asks for a relationship, not a list of details.

  1. Find the reasoning demand: the reader must connect a claim of shared knowledge, two times, and an effect.
  2. Define the obstacle: an audience assumption is something the narrator treats the reader as already knowing. Here “You know” directly makes that move.
  3. Expose the sequence: first locate the assumption; then compare expectation with the scene; finally explain the effect using both details.

Complete revised direction: “First, quote the phrase that treats the reader as sharing knowledge about stations. Next, compare the expected time of emptiness with the actual time. Finally, use both details to explain how the difference may make the scene seem unusual.”

Model student response: “‘You know’ invites the reader to accept empty stations after midnight as familiar. The scene then presents vacant benches at noon. That contrast may make ordinary emptiness seem out of place, but it does not prove why the station is empty.”

Confirmation check: “Which step asks you to explain the relationship rather than only quote?” Answer: the final step. I preserved inference and evidence; I did not replace analysis with “What time is it?”

Example 2 — Discuss Voice without Ranking Language

Complete draft: “My auntie said, ‘That river got a long memory.’ She lifted the flood-marked chair onto the porch. I set the new paint beside it and left the old waterline showing.” Writer’s goal: preserve the auntie’s familiar spoken phrasing while making the physical action clear. A waterline is a mark left by water; the phrase about memory gives the river a human quality.

Weak comment: “Fix the grammar so the auntie sounds educated.”

  1. Identify the assumption: the reviewer equates one language convention with intelligence and ignores the stated voice goal.
  2. Locate an actual question: the narrator brings new paint but leaves the mark. That action is observable; the narrator’s entire motive is not established.
  3. Check the intended effect: ask whether the visible mark should echo the auntie’s phrase. Do not assign a dialect or cultural identity from one sentence.
  4. Offer options: add a gesture around the mark for clarity, or keep the compressed action for subtlety. Each should preserve the quoted voice.

Complete replacement feedback: “Your goal is to keep the auntie’s familiar phrasing. ‘Long memory’ and the visible waterline both direct my attention to a past flood. Am I right that leaving the mark is meant to echo her words? If you want that connection clearer, you could have the narrator brush paint around the mark; that adds a visible action but makes the connection more explicit. Keeping the current ending is subtler, though some readers may need to infer more. Which fits your goal?”

Visible test revision: “I brushed new paint around the old waterline and left the mark showing.” This tests the action, not the auntie’s intelligence. The original might still be preferable if the writer wants restraint.

Example 3 — Repair an Exchange about Ambiguity

Complete draft: “Dani set a packed bag beside the door. On the table, her brother’s unfinished birthday card lay open. She reached for the handle, then folded the card.” Goal: show reasons to leave and stay, but leave the decision open.

Initial exchange: Reviewer: “The ending is unclear. Make her walk out.” Writer: “I don’t want readers to know whether she leaves.” Reviewer: “I’m only trying to help.”

  1. Name the mismatch: the advice decides the outcome instead of clarifying the competing reasons.
  2. Take responsibility without blame: replace defense of good intentions with an acknowledgment of the mismatch.
  3. Ground the response: the packed bag suggests leaving; the card may suggest a commitment, but its timing is unknown.
  4. Offer two testable options: a time cue makes the obligation explicit; a hesitation gesture draws attention more subtly.

Complete repaired exchange:

Reviewer: “I treated uncertainty as a flaw. You want the decision open but the competing reasons visible—is that right?”
Writer: “Yes. The card should make staying a real possibility.”
Reviewer: “‘Packed bag’ makes leaving clear to me. Could we add ‘For tomorrow’ on the card, or let Dani touch it before reaching for the handle? The time cue makes the nearby commitment clearer but limits the timing; the gesture is subtler but may leave more inference to the reader. Which serves your goal?”
Writer: “I’ll test the time cue and keep the last action unresolved.”

Complete revised draft: “Dani set a packed bag beside the door. On the table, her brother’s unfinished birthday card lay open, ‘For tomorrow’ written across its cover. She reached for the handle, then folded the card.”

Why it works: the reviewer supplies evidence and choices; the writer makes the decision. The new cue supports a near-term commitment without proving Dani stays. A useful next check is “What supports each possible choice?” rather than “Now you know she stays, right?”
