Worked Example: Three Communication Repairs

Example 1 — Clarifying a Complex Prompt

Original direction: “Interrogate the narrator’s positionality and its destabilization of the implied audience.”

  1. Problem: essential concepts are undefined and the action is hidden inside abstract nouns.
  2. Preserved rigor: students still must analyze narrator, audience, and change.
  3. Revised direction: “First, identify what the narrator assumes about the audience. Next, quote one place where that assumption changes or fails. Finally, explain how the change affects the reader’s trust.”
  4. Why it works: the revision exposes the thinking sequence, names evidence, and preserves analysis.
  5. Confirmation check: “Which step asks you to use a quotation?”

Example 2 — Feedback on Linguistic Voice

Draft line: “My auntie been knowing the river don’t forgive.”

Weak feedback: “Fix the grammar so it sounds educated.”

  1. Problems: the comment attacks identity, assumes error, and ignores voice and context.
  2. Goal question: “Is this phrasing part of the auntie’s established voice or the narrator’s general style?”
  3. Evidence-based response: “The aspect in ‘been knowing’ gives the knowledge a long duration, and the sentence sounds proverbial. If that is intentional, preserve it consistently. If the narrator—not the auntie—is speaking, check whether the surrounding voice establishes the same pattern.”
  4. Ownership: the writer decides based on voice consistency and purpose.

Example 3 — Repairing Misunderstood Feedback

Reviewer: “The ending is unclear.” Writer: “I want it ambiguous.”

  1. Name the gap: “I may be confusing intentional ambiguity with missing evidence.”
  2. Paraphrase: “I understand that the reader should not know whether Dani leaves.”
  3. Focused question: “Should readers understand why staying and leaving are equally possible?”
  4. Evidence: “The packed bag supports leaving, but I do not yet see a detail that makes staying plausible.”
  5. Options: add a reason to stay, or remove one leaving clue to rebalance uncertainty.
  6. Tradeoff: adding evidence clarifies the conflict; removing evidence increases openness.
  7. Confirmation: writer selects the option that matches the intended degree of ambiguity.

Synthesis

All three examples preserve intellectual demand, use exact evidence, identify assumptions, offer choices, and return the final decision to the writer.

Common Failure and Revision

Vague: “Make this clearer.”

Specific and inclusive: “I understand the character’s action, but the pronoun in sentence four could refer to either sibling. If the ambiguity is unintentional, repeat the intended name; if intentional, add a detail showing why both possibilities matter.”
