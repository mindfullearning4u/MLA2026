Worked Example: Making the Thinking Visible

Read Like an Apprentice Writer

We will examine three short drafts. For each, first read the passage without the explanation. Ask yourself, “Which words made me react?” Then compare your thinking with mine. You do not have to produce my exact interpretation; you do need a clear link between the words and your response.

In Example 1, we will also follow the writer’s decision through a complete revision cycle. In Examples 2 and 3, notice how the writer’s purpose changes which advice is useful.

Example 1 — Narrative Image and Pacing

Draft: “The last bell rang. The hallway swallowed everyone at once. Mira stayed beside the dented locker, counting to ten before she touched the envelope.”

Task: Give feedback that helps the writer strengthen tension.

Teacher think-aloud: Notice that the hallway cannot literally swallow people. “Swallowed” turns the space into an active force. Now compare “everyone at once” with Mira counting to ten. The crowd moves quickly; Mira delays. I notice her hesitation because the two speeds are placed beside each other. I will not invent the contents of the envelope to explain it.

  1. Locate evidence: “the hallway swallowed everyone” and Mira’s delayed touch.
  2. Name choices: personification, contrast, and slowed action.
  3. Reason through the effect: The rushing hallway makes Mira’s stillness noticeable; counting delays the envelope reveal and creates tension.
  4. Choose a revision priority: The tension is already working. If the writer wants to make the hesitation more physical, one sensory cue could be a useful experiment—not an automatic requirement.

Model feedback: “The personification in ‘the hallway swallowed everyone’ makes the crowd feel forceful, while Mira’s counting slows the moment and builds tension. You might add one sound or touch detail from the envelope so the reader feels the hesitation physically while its cause stays uncertain.”

Why it works: The response uses two pieces of evidence, explains their relationship, and proposes a focused option without deciding what the envelope contains.

Example 1 Continued — Feedback Becomes a Revision

Annotation map: words → craft → reader effect
Exact evidenceCraft choiceReasoned effect
“the hallway swallowed everyone”PersonificationThe movement seems forceful because the hallway is described as if it could consume the crowd.
“everyone at once” / “counting to ten”Contrast and pacingThe crowd’s speed highlights Mira’s delay, so attention shifts to her hesitation.

1. The writer sets the goal: “I want the reader to feel Mira’s hesitation without learning what the envelope contains.”

2. The reader offers feedback: The model above suggests one sound or touch detail. It explains why that might support the goal.

3. The writer decides: “I will test a touch detail. I will not add the envelope’s contents, because that would remove the uncertainty I want.” Notice that the writer selects and adapts an option.

Before and after: only the final sentence changes
Original sentenceRevised sentence
Mira stayed beside the dented locker, counting to ten before she touched the envelope.Mira stayed beside the dented locker, counting to ten before she touched the envelope; its rough edge scraped her thumb, and she loosened her grip.

4. Read the complete revised draft: “The last bell rang. The hallway swallowed everyone at once. Mira stayed beside the dented locker, counting to ten before she touched the envelope; its rough edge scraped her thumb, and she loosened her grip.”

5. Compare the effect: Read both versions aloud, or track the actions silently. The revision adds two small actions—scraping and loosening—before the scene moves on. These extend the pause and make the contact physical. We still do not know the envelope’s contents. That matches the stated goal.

6. Judge the trade-off: More detail is not always better. The revision is longer and may feel slower than necessary. If the writer wants a sharper, faster ending, the original could be preferable. A strong revision explanation names the intended effect and the evidence of change; it does not simply say “I added description.”

Pause and try: Which explanation is stronger: “My revision is longer” or “The touch detail extends the hesitation without revealing the envelope’s contents”? Why?

Check your thinking — open after you try

The second is stronger because it connects a specific change to the writer’s goal and the reader’s experience. Length alone does not prove improvement.

Example 2 — Poetic Image and Ambiguity

Draft:

Morning stitched gold
along the cracked sill—
a bright repair
that vanished when I moved.

Word help: A sill is the ledge at the bottom of a window. Ambiguity means more than one plausible interpretation; here, the apparent repair may feel hopeful, temporary, or both.

Task: Respond to the image without flattening its ambiguity.

Teacher think-aloud: I first picture sunlight on a damaged window ledge. Then I notice that “stitched” belongs to sewing and repair, not literal sunlight. That metaphor lets light seem helpful. But “vanished” prevents me from treating the repair as permanent. I should hold both pieces of evidence together instead of announcing that the poem is simply happy.

  1. Locate evidence: “stitched gold,” “cracked,” “repair,” and “vanished.”
  2. Name choices: metaphor, contrast, and an image that changes.
  3. Consider more than one meaning: The light may suggest hope, but its disappearance may also suggest that the repair is temporary or imagined.
  4. Protect productive ambiguity: Feedback should help sustain the tension rather than force one explanation.

Model feedback: “The metaphor ‘stitched gold’ makes the light seem capable of repairing damage, but ‘vanished’ makes that repair uncertain. The contrast leaves me deciding whether the speaker feels hopeful or unconvinced. You might echo either ‘stitch’ or ‘crack’ in the next image so the tension continues without explaining it away.”

Why it works: The response acknowledges multiple defensible interpretations and bases each one on diction—specific word choices.

Pause and try: Would “End by saying the speaker feels hopeful” preserve this draft’s uncertainty? Explain before checking.

Check your thinking — open after you try

Not necessarily. It would select one interpretation and could erase the tension between “repair” and “vanished.” A better first move is to ask whether the writer wants certainty or uncertainty. The suggestion to echo an image is an option, not a demand to add another line.

Example 3 — Dialogue, Action, and Subtext

Draft: “‘Fine,’ Mateo said. He folded the permission slip in half, then in half again, pressing each crease with his thumbnail. ‘Tell her I signed it.’”

Task: Help the writer clarify character emotion without naming it directly.

Teacher think-aloud: Start with the word “Fine.” By itself, it might communicate agreement. Now read the repeated folding and pressure of the thumbnail. Those actions complicate the word. This is subtext: a meaning implied beneath the speech. I can support discomfort, but I cannot prove its cause. Before requesting a clearer emotion, I would ask, “Should readers recognize one feeling here, or remain uncertain?”

  1. Locate evidence: “Fine,” repeated folding, pressure from the thumbnail, and “Tell her.”
  2. Name choices: dialogue-action contrast, repetition, and an indirect request.
  3. Infer carefully: Mateo may be angry, reluctant, or anxious; the draft supports tension but not one certain emotion.
  4. Suggest a discriminating detail: One additional behavior or tonal cue could narrow the reader’s inference.

Model feedback: “Mateo says ‘Fine,’ but the repeated folding and hard creases contradict the word and create subtext. I can tell he is tense, though I cannot yet distinguish anger from worry. You might add one precise vocal or physical detail that points toward the emotion you intend while keeping it unstated.”

Why it works: The feedback distinguishes evidence from inference and asks for precision without rewriting Mateo’s reaction. A vocal cue means a detail about how the words sound, such as a whisper or a broken phrase. If uncertainty is the author’s goal, the existing actions may already be enough.

Pause and try: Choose a safe claim: “Mateo is definitely angry with his mother” or “Mateo’s actions suggest tension despite his word ‘Fine.’” What evidence supports your choice?

Check your thinking — open after you try

The second stays within the evidence. Repeated folding and firm creases suggest tension. The draft neither identifies “her” as his mother nor proves anger. Good feedback does not turn a possible interpretation into a fact.

Comparison: Weak vs. Useful

Weak: “Make all three pieces more descriptive.”

Useful: Each model above identifies a different craft problem—sensory tension, sustained ambiguity, or emotional subtext. Effective feedback responds to the specific purpose and evidence of a draft rather than applying the same generic advice to every genre.

Your Turn Before Independent Work

New practice line: “Ari placed two cups on the table, then returned one to the cupboard when the doorbell rang.”

Pause and try: Write two or three sentences: quote an action, name a craft choice, explain a possible effect, and offer one optional revision tied to a goal. Do not invent the visitor’s identity.

Check your thinking — open after you try

One possible response: “Returning one cup just as the doorbell rings interrupts the preparation and creates uncertainty about Ari’s expectations. If you want the change of mind to feel more tense, you might pause Ari’s hand above the cup before it is removed.” The action supplies evidence; interrupted action names a choice; uncertainty explains an effect; and the last sentence offers a purposeful option.

Ready to apply: On Page 5, use this process with a new passage and your own scene. Your ideas should be your own; use the models for the reasoning process, not sentences to copy.
