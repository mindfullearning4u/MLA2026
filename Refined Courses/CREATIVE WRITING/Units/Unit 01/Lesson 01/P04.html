Worked Example: Making the Thinking Visible

Example 1 — Narrative Image and Pacing

Draft: “The last bell rang. The hallway swallowed everyone at once. Mira stayed beside the dented locker, counting to ten before she touched the envelope.”

Task: Give feedback that helps the writer strengthen tension.

  1. Locate evidence: “the hallway swallowed everyone” and Mira’s delayed touch.
  2. Name choices: personification, contrast, and slowed action.
  3. Reason through the effect: The rushing hallway makes Mira’s stillness noticeable; counting delays the envelope reveal and creates tension.
  4. Choose a revision priority: The tension is working, but the reader lacks a sensory cue connecting Mira to the envelope.

Model feedback: “The personification in ‘the hallway swallowed everyone’ makes the crowd feel forceful, while Mira’s counting slows the moment and builds tension. You might add one sound or touch detail from the envelope so the reader experiences why opening it feels difficult.”

Why it works: The response uses two pieces of evidence, explains their relationship, and proposes a focused option without deciding what the envelope contains.

Example 2 — Poetic Image and Ambiguity

Draft: “Morning stitched gold / along the cracked sill— / a bright repair / that vanished when I moved.”

Task: Respond to the image without flattening its ambiguity.

  1. Locate evidence: “stitched gold,” “cracked,” “repair,” and “vanished.”
  2. Name choices: metaphor, contrast, and an image that changes.
  3. Consider more than one meaning: The light may suggest hope, but its disappearance may also suggest that the repair is temporary or imagined.
  4. Protect productive ambiguity: Feedback should help sustain the tension rather than force one explanation.

Model feedback: “The metaphor ‘stitched gold’ makes the light seem capable of repairing damage, but ‘vanished’ makes that repair uncertain. The contrast leaves me deciding whether the speaker feels hopeful or unconvinced. You might echo either ‘stitch’ or ‘crack’ in the next image so the tension continues without explaining it away.”

Why it works: The response acknowledges multiple defensible interpretations and bases each one on diction.

Example 3 — Dialogue, Action, and Subtext

Draft: “‘Fine,’ Mateo said. He folded the permission slip in half, then in half again, pressing each crease with his thumbnail. ‘Tell her I signed it.’”

Task: Help the writer clarify character emotion without naming it directly.

  1. Locate evidence: “Fine,” repeated folding, pressure from the thumbnail, and “Tell her.”
  2. Name choices: dialogue-action contrast, repetition, and indirect speech.
  3. Infer carefully: Mateo may be angry, reluctant, or anxious; the draft supports tension but not one certain emotion.
  4. Suggest a discriminating detail: One additional behavior or tonal cue could narrow the reader’s inference.

Model feedback: “Mateo says ‘Fine,’ but the repeated folding and hard creases contradict the word and create subtext. I can tell he is tense, though I cannot yet distinguish anger from worry. You might add one precise vocal or physical detail that points toward the emotion you intend while keeping it unstated.”

Why it works: The feedback distinguishes evidence from inference and asks for precision without rewriting Mateo’s reaction.

Comparison: Weak vs. Useful

Weak: “Make all three pieces more descriptive.”

Useful: Each model above identifies a different craft problem—sensory tension, sustained ambiguity, or emotional subtext. Effective feedback responds to the specific purpose and evidence of a draft rather than applying the same generic advice to every genre.
