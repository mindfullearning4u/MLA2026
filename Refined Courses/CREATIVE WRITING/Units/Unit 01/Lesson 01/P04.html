P04 Worked Example

Study each example step by step. These examples show how to move from a vague response to useful workshop feedback.

Example 1: Responding to an Image

Student draft line: "The last bell rang, and the hallway swallowed everyone at once."

Step 1: Point to the exact craft choice. The phrase "hallway swallowed everyone" is personification.

Step 2: Explain the effect. It makes the hallway feel powerful and crowded.

Step 3: Suggest a next move. The writer could add one sound or movement detail to extend the image.

Workshop feedback: "I noticed the personification in 'the hallway swallowed everyone.' This creates a crowded, active feeling. You might add one sound detail so the reader can hear the rush of students too."

Example 2: Responding to a Character Action

Student draft line: "Jonah erased his answer three times, even though the page was already blank."

Step 1: Notice the action. Jonah keeps erasing when there is nothing left to erase.

Step 2: Connect action to possible meaning. The action suggests uncertainty, fear, or pressure without naming the emotion directly.

Step 3: Give a focused suggestion. The writer could add what Jonah hears or sees to show why he feels pressure.

Workshop feedback: "I noticed Jonah's repeated erasing. This shows stress through action instead of explanation. You might add one surrounding detail, such as a clock or another student's movement, to increase the pressure in the scene."

Example 3: Responding to Voice

Student draft line: "Obviously, I had a plan. It was just the kind of plan that works better if nobody asks questions."

Step 1: Identify the voice. The narrator sounds humorous and defensive.

Step 2: Explain what the voice does. It makes the reader curious and creates a playful tone.

Step 3: Suggest a next move. The writer could keep the same voice in the next few sentences so the tone stays consistent.

Workshop feedback: "I noticed the narrator's humorous, defensive voice. This creates a playful tone and makes me want to know what the plan is. You might keep that same voice in the next action so the narrator stays consistent."

Common Mistake

Incorrect: "Make it more interesting."

Why this is weak: The writer does not know what "interesting" means, where to revise, or what craft move to try.

Correct: "The opening action is clear, but the setting is still general. You might add one visual detail and one sound detail so the reader can enter the scene more fully."

Why this works: The comment identifies a specific area, explains the need, and gives a concrete revision direction.
