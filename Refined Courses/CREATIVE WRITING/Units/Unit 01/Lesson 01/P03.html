Direct Teaching: From Writer’s Notebook to Useful Feedback

Begin with Noticing, Not Judging

Let’s begin as writers before we respond as readers. Imagine noticing a person turn an old key over in a pocket. I do not yet have a plot. I have an action worth exploring. Instead of deciding that my idea is too ordinary, I record what I can observe and ask a question about it.

Here is a model notebook entry. Read down the left column to follow my process. Read across to see both the writing and the reason behind my choice.

Your notebook can include unfinished trials and crossed-out ideas. That is evidence of thinking, not failure. Choose a safe or invented object and jot an observation, a “What if?” question, and a possible writing goal. Keep these as practice notes you may use on Page 5.

Workshop Norms: Protect the Writer and Study the Draft

A workshop is not a vote on whether a writer is talented. It is a conversation about what a draft currently does. We can question a sentence without judging the person who wrote it. The writer owns the final decision.

  1. Ask permission and purpose: “What would you like me to notice?” The writer may choose an invented piece and does not owe readers private background information.
  2. Listen without interrupting: Paraphrase the goal: “You want tension, but you want the reason to remain uncertain.” Ask whether you understood correctly.
  3. Discuss the text, not the person: Say what words or actions led to your response. Do not assume a narrator’s life is the author’s life.
  4. Offer, do not take over: Suggest a test. The writer can accept it, adapt it, or decline it with a reason.

Teacher reminder: If you work alone, use the supplied draft and practice both roles. You do not need a classmate to complete this lesson.

Knowledge Check: Protect the Writer

Situation: A writer says, “I want the reader to feel uncertain about whether Mara will open the door.” Which response follows the workshop norms and gives the writer something useful to test?

A. “Mara should open the door immediately because waiting makes the scene boring.”

B. “The pause before Mara reaches the handle creates uncertainty. If you want that uncertainty to last longer, you might add one sound from behind the door before she decides.”

Choose A or B before opening the explanation. Look for four parts: evidence, craft choice, reader effect, and an optional revision connected to the writer’s goal.

Check your choice and reasoning

B is stronger. It points to the pause, explains how the pause affects the reader, and offers a small experiment that supports the writer’s stated purpose. A is weaker because it replaces the writer’s goal with the reader’s preferred plot and calls the scene boring without citing evidence.

The Four-Step Feedback Process

Read the draft once for the situation before hunting for techniques. Then read again with a pencil or a note beside you. The sequence below keeps your response anchored to the writing.

Feedback route: follow the numbered steps
StepQuestion to askUseful sentence opening
1. Locate evidenceWhich exact words or action caused my response?“In the words…”
2. Name the craft choiceWhat is the writer doing with those words?“The repeated action / image / dialogue…”
3. Explain the reader effectWhat does it make me infer, feel, notice, or expect—and why?“This makes me… because…”
4. Offer a focused optionWhat could the writer test to support the stated goal?“If you want…, you might try…”

The steps are not four disconnected labels. Build a chain: words in the draft → a craft choice → a reasoned reader response → a purposeful revision option. A writer cannot act on “make it better.” A writer can test “delay the reply with one physical action.”

Watch Me Read a Single Sentence

Sentence: “Her key stopped an inch from the lock.”

First, I locate evidence. I underline “stopped an inch.” I can point to those words; I am not yet making a claim about the character’s past.

Next, I name the choice. The writer pauses an action just before it is complete. This controls pacing—how fast the reader experiences the event. A tiny distance gets its own moment, so we wait with the character.

Now, I explain the effect. I wonder why she hesitates because she is close enough to unlock the door but does not finish. Notice the word “because”: it connects my response to evidence. I cannot responsibly announce that someone dangerous is inside; the sentence does not establish that.

Finally, I consider purpose. Suppose the writer wants hesitation without revealing its cause. I might say: “The pause in ‘stopped an inch’ slows the moment and makes me expect a difficult choice. You might add one touch detail, such as the key pressing into her palm, to make the hesitation physical while keeping its cause uncertain.”

I have offered a possible test, not a required rewrite. Another reader may suggest a sound. The writer should compare each option with the intended effect.

Pause and Choose: Evidence or Assumption

Compare the two explanations before selecting an answer. Feedback appears immediately after your choice. Read why one interpretation is better supported and why the other goes too far. You can retry; this practice does not count toward your course grade.

Take the strategy with you: Notice the detail → explain what it may suggest → acknowledge what remains unknown. No additional written submission is required.

Three Craft Choices You Will Meet

Personification: “The hallway swallowed everyone.” A hallway cannot literally swallow. The writer gives it an action associated with a living being, making the crowd’s movement seem forceful. Name the action first; then explain its effect.

Metaphor and contrast: “Morning stitched gold along the cracked sill.” A sill is the ledge at the bottom of a window. Light becomes a kind of stitching. “Cracked” introduces damage, while “stitched” suggests repair. The meaning comes from the relationship between the words, not from calling the line “pretty.”

Subtext: “‘Fine,’ Mateo said, folding the permission slip into smaller squares.” The spoken word suggests agreement, but the repeated action may suggest discomfort. Subtext is this implied meaning beneath the speech. We still need context before deciding whether he is angry, worried, or reluctant.

Symbolism without guessing: An object can suggest more than its practical use when the surrounding actions or memories support that meaning. A key might suggest access in one story and an unwanted responsibility in another. Never assume that an object has one fixed meaning in every text.

Knowledge Check: Explain the Craft Choice

Sentence: “Fine,” Mateo said, folding the permission slip into smaller squares.

Which analysis shows the stronger evidence-to-reasoning chain?

A. “The contrast between Mateo’s word and his repeated folding may suggest discomfort because his action does not match the calm agreement of ‘Fine.’ The exact emotion remains uncertain.”

B. “Mateo is angry with his parents and plans to refuse permission because folding paper always symbolizes rebellion.”

Choose A or B. Test each answer by asking: What does the sentence actually show? What can reasonably be inferred? What remains unknown?

Check your choice and reasoning

A is stronger. It uses both the spoken word and the physical action, explains the contrast, and keeps the inference appropriately qualified. B is weaker because it invents parents, a plan, and a fixed symbolic meaning that the sentence does not establish.

Helpful Uncertainty or Simple Confusion?

Sometimes a writer deliberately leaves more than one interpretation possible. That is ambiguity. Before asking the writer to explain everything, ask: Do I understand what is happening but wonder what it means? Or am I unable to follow who is speaking or acting?

Decide what kind of clarification is needed
Reader experienceUseful response
I understand the action but can support two emotions.Ask about the intended effect. Preserve uncertainty if it serves the writer’s goal.
I cannot tell which character said the line.Suggest clarifying the speaker before adding an explanation of feelings.

Rigor means considering alternatives and defending a choice with evidence. It does not mean inventing a complicated interpretation that the passage cannot support.

Compare Feedback Before You Give It

From judgment to usable feedback
Weak responseMore useful responseWhy it helps
“I liked it.”“The pause before the key turns makes me anticipate a difficult choice.”Names a specific strength and its effect.
“Make her confess.”“I cannot tell what she wants in this exchange. Could one action make her immediate goal clearer?”Identifies a reader difficulty without choosing the plot for the author.
“Add more description.”“If you want the pause to feel longer, you might add one touch detail before she turns the key.”Offers a small, testable change tied to purpose.

A response to a strength does not always need a correction. Explain what is working so the author knows what to preserve. When a task asks for revision advice, add a focused option instead of inventing a flaw.

Check your thinking — open after you try

One possible response: “Jon’s smile seems to conflict with hiding the invitation, so I wonder whether he is pretending to welcome it. If you want that conflict to be clearer, you might add a small change in his expression after the invitation disappears.” This preserves the author’s choice of motive. It is not the only valid answer.

Retry: If your first choice is weaker, close it and select the other choice before continuing. Compare both explanations so you can name why the stronger answer works.

If you got stuck: Return to the exact words. Name what you can see before interpreting it. Use the craft glossary if a label is unfamiliar, and try the sentence opening “This makes me wonder… because…”.
