Direct Teaching: Evidence-Based Workshop Feedback

The Purpose of Workshop

A workshop is not a vote on whether a draft is “good.” It is a disciplined reading conversation. The responder reports what the draft currently does, supports that observation with exact evidence, and offers a revision possibility. The writer listens, asks questions, and decides what serves the piece.

The Four-Step Feedback Process

  1. Locate evidence: Quote or precisely describe a word, image, action, line, structural move, or omission.
  2. Name the craft choice: Identify what the writer is doing—such as imagery, pacing, dialogue, voice, contrast, or characterization.
  3. Explain the reader effect: State what the choice makes the reader notice, infer, feel, question, or expect. Use a because statement.
  4. Offer a revision possibility: Suggest one testable move while preserving the writer’s control. Phrase it as an option, not a command.

Active Listening Before Responding

  1. Let the writer state the purpose or concern without interruption.
  2. Paraphrase the writer’s goal: “You want the scene to feel tense before the door opens.”
  3. Ask one clarifying question if the purpose is uncertain.
  4. Respond to the draft that exists, not the story you would prefer to write.
  5. Separate craft observations from personal taste.

Three Situations, Three Kinds of Analysis

Narrative: “Her key stopped an inch from the lock.” The delayed action slows pacing and signals hesitation. Useful feedback should examine what is causing the pause rather than simply demand “more detail.”

Poetry: “Morning stitched gold along the cracked sill.” The metaphor joins beauty with damage. Useful feedback can ask whether later images sustain that contrast.

Dialogue: “‘Fine,’ Mateo said, folding the permission slip into smaller squares.” The action contradicts the spoken word and creates subtext. Useful feedback should name that contradiction and consider how it develops character.

Common Mistakes and Corrections

  • Vague praise: “I liked it.” Correction: identify the exact choice and its effect.
  • Personal judgment: “Your character is annoying.” Correction: describe the character’s action or language and the response it creates.
  • Taking control: “Make the sister confess.” Correction: explain what remains unclear and offer an option the writer may test.
  • Editing too early: correcting commas while the conflict is undeveloped. Correction: address meaning and structure before proofreading.
  • Unsupported interpretation: claiming an emotion without evidence. Correction: connect every inference to a quoted or precisely described detail.

Quality Check

Before giving feedback, ask: Did I point to evidence? Did I name the craft choice accurately? Did I explain the effect rather than only label it? Is my suggestion specific, respectful, and optional? Does it support the writer’s stated purpose?
