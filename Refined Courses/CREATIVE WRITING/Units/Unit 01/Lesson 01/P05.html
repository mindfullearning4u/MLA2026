Independent Work: Workshop Analysis and Application

Work in Stages

First understand the situation. Next examine how the writing creates meaning. Then turn that analysis into feedback. Finally, use the process on your own scene. You may consult the lesson models, but write your own interpretations and support them with this passage.

Word investigation — include with Question 4: Look up glaze and swallow in a dictionary. Record the source name, the definition that fits each word in this passage, and a context clue supporting your choice. Explain why another meaning of swallow would not fit. Look up any other unfamiliar passage words before answering. This is vocabulary-in-context work, not an invitation to search for story answers.

Directions

Read the draft carefully. Answer all requirements in complete sentences and cite exact words or actions when requested. Then complete the original writing and feedback task. Submit these responses with your Page 2 vocabulary as Notebook Evidence.

Draft for Workshop: “The Blue Bowl”

Leah found the bowl behind the flour tin, where her grandmother used to hide birthday money. Its blue glaze had faded at the rim, and one hairline crack ran from the base to a painted swallow. She set it on the counter before her brother arrived.

“You kept that?” Daniel asked.

Leah turned the bowl until the crack faced the wall. “It was already here.”

Rain tapped the kitchen window. Daniel opened a drawer, closed it, then opened the next one. He had come for the recipe cards, but neither of them said so. Leah remembered their grandmother measuring sugar without a cup, letting the white grains rise in her palm like a small hill.

Daniel reached toward the bowl and stopped. “Mom said we should divide everything.”

“Then take the cards.” Leah heard how quickly she answered. She placed both hands around the bowl, though it was empty.

Part A — Comprehension and Evidence

  1. State the central situation and the unstated conflict between Leah and Daniel.
  2. Identify two actions that reveal tension. Quote each action and explain what it suggests.
  3. Explain why the bowl is more than a household object in this scene.

Part B — Craft Analysis

  1. After completing the word investigation above, analyze how the crack and painted swallow contribute to meaning or mood. Support your interpretation with this scene; do not assume an image has one universal meaning.
  2. Explain the effect of the rain and the remembered sugar image on the pacing.
  3. Analyze the subtext in “It was already here” or “Then take the cards.” Distinguish what is said from what is implied.
  4. Identify one purposeful ambiguity and explain why a writer might preserve it.

Part C — Evidence-Based Workshop Feedback

  1. Write one feedback comment about characterization using the four-step process.
  2. Write a different feedback comment about pacing, imagery, dialogue, or symbolism.
  3. Write one clarifying question you would ask the writer before suggesting a major revision.
  4. Identify one piece of advice that would take too much control away from the writer; rewrite it as a respectful option.

Part D — Original Application

Plan before drafting: Use a safe or invented object. In your notebook, record an observation, a “What if?” question, and the effect you want a reader to experience. Use those notes to shape Question 12. You are not required to reveal private experiences. After Question 15, add one reflection sentence: “This experiment shows that I am interested in writing about…”. These notes connect your scene to your developing writing identity.

  1. Write an original six-to-eight-sentence scene in which an object carries emotional meaning. Reveal tension through action, dialogue, imagery, or pacing without directly naming the main emotion.
  2. Write a weak, vague response someone might give your scene.
  3. Transform it into useful feedback that quotes or describes evidence, names a craft choice, explains its effect, and offers one focused revision possibility.
  4. Revise one sentence of your scene using that feedback. Explain why the revision is stronger.

Check Your Work Before Submitting

  • Questions 1–7: separate what the text states from what you infer; explain the evidence rather than only naming a device.
  • Questions 8–11: connect feedback to exact words and reader effects; keep revision suggestions optional.
  • Questions 12–15: include the six-to-eight-sentence scene, weak response, improved feedback, original and revised sentence, and an explanation of the change.
  • Include your planning/reflection notes, the Question 4 word investigation, and the eight core Page 2 definitions with Notebook Evidence.

If your feedback says only “add detail,” ask yourself: Which detail, where, and for what effect? If you cannot answer, return to the quoted evidence before submitting.
