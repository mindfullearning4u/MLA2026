Independent Work: Notice, Choose, Write, Revise

Purpose and Directions

Apply the lesson skills by reading like a workshop partner, making one evidence-based craft judgment, writing a focused scene, and revising one purposeful choice. Complete each numbered task and submit it with your vocabulary notes in Notebook Evidence Submission.

Quality matters more than length: make each response specific, supported, and useful.

Draft for Workshop: “The Blue Bowl”

Leah found the bowl behind the flour tin, where her grandmother used to hide birthday money. Its blue glaze had faded at the rim, and one hairline crack ran from the base to a painted swallow. She set it on the counter before her brother arrived.

“You kept that?” Daniel asked.

Leah turned the bowl until the crack faced the wall. “It was already here.”

Rain tapped the kitchen window. Daniel opened a drawer, closed it, then opened the next one. He had come for the recipe cards, but neither of them said so. Leah remembered their grandmother measuring sugar without a cup, letting the white grains rise in her palm like a small hill.

Daniel reached toward the bowl and stopped. “Mom said we should divide everything.”

“Then take the cards.” Leah heard how quickly she answered. She placed both hands around the bowl, though it was empty.

2. Choose the Stronger Workshop Response

Read both responses. Record A or B, then explain in two or three sentences why your choice would be more useful to the writer. Consider evidence, craft language, reader effect, and respect. The answer is not revealed on this page.

A. “I like the bowl because it is interesting. The writer should add more details and make the characters explain what happened.”
B. “When Leah turns the crack toward the wall, the action indirectly shows that she wants to protect or hide something connected to the bowl. That makes the tension feel restrained because the characters avoid naming the conflict. If the writer wants the tension slightly stronger, they might add one precise physical reaction from Daniel before he speaks.”

3. Apply the Craft in Your Writing

Choose a safe or invented object that matters to a character. Write one goal sentence stating what you want the reader to feel or wonder. Then write a focused six-to-eight-sentence scene.

Use at least two purposeful craft choices—such as action, dialogue, sensory detail, pacing, or placement of the object—to suggest emotion without directly naming it. Your scene should have a clear situation and remain your own original work.

4. Revise and Explain

Revise one sentence from your scene so it better supports your goal. Explain how the change should affect the reader. Do not rewrite the entire scene.
