Direct Teaching: Reading Models as a Writer

The Five-Stage Reading Method

  1. Establish the literal situation: state only what the passage directly presents.
  2. Paraphrase accurately: preserve the relationships among important actions and details.
  3. Notice a pattern: locate repeated, contrasted, omitted, or unusually placed elements.
  4. Analyze interaction: explain how at least two choices reinforce or complicate each other.
  5. Infer purpose and transfer: make a defensible claim about reader effect, then express the craft principle in a new context.

Literal Meaning Before Subtext

Subtext must grow from literal evidence. In “She set two plates, then returned one to the cupboard,” the literal action is setting and removing a plate. Loneliness is a reasonable inference; certainty about death, divorce, or abandonment is not. Strong analysis marks the boundary between what the text establishes and what it merely permits.

From Device Hunting to Craft Interaction

Naming imagery or repetition is only the beginning. Ask how choices cooperate. A repeated sound may slow pacing; paired with an omitted explanation, it may also increase uncertainty. The strongest analysis uses a chain:

specific evidence → named choices → relationship between choices → reader effect → likely purpose

Compression, Pattern, and Contrast

Compression selects details that imply a larger history. Pattern trains the reader to notice recurrence or change. Contrast creates tension between conditions, voices, settings, or expectations. A writer may combine all three—for example, one clean place setting beside three dusty ones compresses absence, establishes a visual pattern, and contrasts present care with past neglect.

Test Competing Interpretations

Complex passages often support more than one plausible inference. Rank interpretations by asking: Which claim accounts for the most details? Which requires the fewest invented facts? Which best explains the placement and interaction of the writer’s choices? A strong response may acknowledge an alternative before defending the better-supported claim.

Transfer Without Copying

  1. State the craft principle abstractly: “an ordinary object carries an unstated conflict.”
  2. Change subject, setting, character, and wording.
  3. Recreate the function, not the sentence pattern alone.
  4. Explain how your choices produce a comparable effect.

Weak transfer: replacing a suitcase with a backpack while copying the same situation. Strong transfer: using a still-warm bakery receipt to suggest a character arrived moments too late.

Analysis Frame

The literal situation is ____. The pattern/contrast between “____” and “____” combines ____ with ____. This interaction causes the reader to ____. Although the passage could suggest ____, the stronger interpretation is ____ because ____. A transferable principle is ____.
