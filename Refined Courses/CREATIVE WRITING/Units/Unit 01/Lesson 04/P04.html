Worked Example: Three Complex Craft Models

Example 1 — Compression Through Object Detail

Model: “Mara erased the grocery list but left birthday candles on the board. At six, she turned off the porch light without opening the cake box.”

  1. Literal situation: Mara removes most of a list, leaves one item visible, turns off a light, and does not open a cake box.
  2. Pattern: objects connected with celebration remain unused.
  3. Interaction: the visible candles and unopened cake compress an absent or failed celebration; the time marker and dark porch add finality.
  4. Competing inference: she may have changed plans, but disappointment or an expected person’s absence explains more details.
  5. Model analysis: The unused celebratory objects compress a larger disappointment into two concrete details. Turning off the porch light at six closes the possibility of arrival, so action and object detail work together to create restrained grief.
  6. Transfer: In a new scene, a character deletes every rehearsal reminder except “save aisle seat,” then folds an unused program.

Example 2 — Juxtaposition and Subtext

Model: “The acceptance letter rested beneath the eviction notice. Luis read the first twice, folded both into the same envelope, and told his sister the mail was mostly advertisements.”

  1. Literal situation: Luis receives good and threatening news, conceals both, and minimizes the mail.
  2. Pattern: paired documents and paired acts of folding join opportunity with crisis.
  3. Interaction: juxtaposition makes success inseparable from instability; understatement reveals protective concealment.
  4. Competing inference: shame is possible, but his statement to his sister more strongly suggests protection because he hides both triumph and danger.
  5. Model analysis: Placing acceptance under eviction prevents the achievement from feeling uncomplicated. Luis’s understatement creates subtext: he may be shielding his sister even while he cannot separate hope from the family’s immediate crisis.
  6. Transfer: Pair an award certificate with a bus cancellation to place recognition beside a practical barrier.

Example 3 — Pattern Break and Structural Meaning

Model: “Every Friday, Grandmother labeled one jar and placed it on the top shelf: peaches, beans, tomatoes, pears. In October she wrote no label. She set the empty jar beside the telephone.”

  1. Literal situation: a weekly preserving routine stops; an empty jar is placed near the phone.
  2. Pattern: the sequence of filled, labeled jars establishes order and continuity.
  3. Break: the unlabeled empty jar interrupts both material and linguistic patterns.
  4. Interaction: the empty vessel plus proximity to the phone suggests waiting for news that cannot yet be named.
  5. Model analysis: The list of preserved foods makes the October absence visible. Because the jar is both empty and unlabeled, it represents suspended expectation; placing it beside the telephone directs that uncertainty toward anticipated news.
  6. Transfer: Establish repeated completed forms, then leave one unsigned beside a hospital pager.

What the Three Models Demonstrate

Each analysis begins with literal accuracy, traces a pattern across multiple details, evaluates a plausible alternative, and explains interaction before purpose. Each transfer changes the situation and language while preserving only the craft principle.

Common Failure and Revision

Too shallow: “The empty jar is symbolism and makes the passage sad.”

Revised: “The unlabeled empty jar breaks the established pattern of named, filled jars. Its position beside the telephone makes waiting—not general sadness—the most defensible interpretation.”
