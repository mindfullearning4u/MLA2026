Worked Example: Three Portfolio Decisions

Example 1 — Choosing the Stronger Artifact

Candidate A: a polished 1,800-word story with vivid description but little evidence of revision.

Candidate B: a 1,200-word story whose revision trail shows a confusing flashback reorganized into a controlled nonlinear structure.

  1. Identify the portfolio purpose: demonstrate quality and revision growth.
  2. Compare evidence: A is polished; B demonstrates craft, feedback use, and growth.
  3. Decision: include B if its final quality is strong enough.
  4. Portfolio note: cite the exact reordering and its effect on reader orientation.

Example 2 — Prioritizing Feedback

Feedback: (a) “The climax arrives before the central conflict is clear.” (b) “Change two commas.” (c) “The blue wallpaper detail is memorable.”

  1. Classify: (a) structure, (b) correctness, (c) strength to preserve.
  2. Prioritize the structural problem first.
  3. Revise by planting the conflict earlier and testing whether the climax now feels earned.
  4. Then correct punctuation without removing the effective wallpaper image.

Reasoning: Revision is not accepting every comment equally; it is diagnosing impact.

Example 3 — Publication and Accessibility Audit

Draft portfolio page: “FinalStoryNEW2.docx,” decorative script font, a photograph with no caption or text alternative, and a link labeled “click here.”

  1. Rename the file RiverGlass_Final_AnikaChen.pdf.
  2. Use a readable heading and body font with consistent contrast.
  3. Add concise alternative text describing the meaningful content of the photograph.
  4. Replace “click here” with a descriptive label such as “Read the revision reflection.”
  5. Open every file and link in a clean browser view before release.

Principle: A work is not publication-ready if the audience cannot reliably access or navigate it.

Transfer Checklist

For each artifact, answer: Why this piece? What changed? What proves the improvement? How will the audience encounter it?
