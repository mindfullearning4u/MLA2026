Lesson: From Draft Collection to Coherent Portfolio

Step 1 — Curate with Criteria

  1. List candidate works and the standards each demonstrates.
  2. Score each for craft quality, revision growth, range, and readiness.
  3. Select the strongest set—not merely the newest or longest pieces.
  4. Identify any gap, such as no evidence of dialogue, structure, or feedback-based revision.

Selection Matrix

CriterionEvidence to seek
QualityControlled language, structure, voice, and conventions
GrowthA meaningful draft-to-final change with rationale
RangeDifferent genres, techniques, purposes, or audiences
ReadinessNo unresolved high-impact weakness

Pause and Choose: Curate with Evidence

A writer must choose between a polished recent poem and an older story that shows a major, well-documented revision. Which portfolio decision is strongest?

A. Include only the newest work because recency automatically proves quality and growth.
Weaker choice. Recency is not a portfolio criterion. It may hide stronger evidence of craft range or meaningful development.
B. Evaluate both against quality, growth, range, and readiness, then include the story if its revision trail fills an important evidence gap.
Stronger choice. The decision is criterion-based and purposeful. A coherent portfolio selects artifacts for what they demonstrate, not merely when they were written.

Retry: If your first choice is weaker, close it and select the other choice before continuing. Compare both explanations so you can name why the stronger answer works.

Step 2 — Revise by Impact

Sort feedback into four levels: meaning and purpose → structure and development → sentence craft → correctness and format. Revise in that order. Polishing punctuation before repairing a missing turning point wastes effort.

Step 3 — Build a Revision Trail

For every major change, save three pieces of evidence: Before, After, and Why. The “why” must name the reader problem and the intended improvement.

Pause and Choose: Explain Revision Growth

Which revision-trail note demonstrates meaningful growth?

A. “Before, the conflict arrived on page four. After reader confusion, I moved the failed promise into the opening scene so later choices have a visible cause.”
Stronger choice. It shows before and after, identifies a reader problem, and explains how the structural change improves cause and effect.
B. “I changed many things and made the story much better.”
Weaker choice. The claim is too general to verify. It names neither an artifact nor specific evidence, reasoning, or effect.

Retry: If your first choice is weaker, close it and select the other choice before continuing. Compare both explanations so you can name why the stronger answer works.

Step 4 — Publication Audit

  • Content: complete, accurate, ethically represented.
  • Craft: purposeful language, pacing, form, and voice.
  • Conventions: grammar and mechanics support meaning.
  • Design: readable font, spacing, headings, captions, and consistent order.
  • Accessibility: descriptive links, text alternatives for meaningful images, sufficient contrast, and no meaning conveyed by color alone.
  • Files: clear metadata, stable file type, working links, and logical names.

Step 5 — Write the Portfolio Note

Use the pattern Claim → Artifact → Specific evidence → Growth insight → Next goal. Avoid vague statements such as “I became a better writer.” Show exactly what improved.
