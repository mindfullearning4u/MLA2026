Worked Example: Three Integrated Editorial Cases

Example 1 — Revision Priority

Case: A story’s dialogue is polished, but readers do not understand why the protagonist risks entering a flooded tunnel.

  1. Purpose: create a believable high-stakes choice.
  2. Diagnosis: motivation is missing; dialogue polish is not the main problem.
  3. Priority: plant an earlier obligation connecting the protagonist to someone inside.
  4. Test: ask a new reader to explain the choice before reaching the tunnel scene.
  5. Polish only after motivation and structure work.

Pause and Choose: A or B

Which revision priority best addresses the flooded-tunnel story’s central problem?

A. Polish the dialogue first because clean sentences make every scene easier to understand.
Weaker response. Dialogue polish does not repair the missing motivation that makes the central action unbelievable.
B. Plant an earlier obligation connecting the protagonist to someone inside the tunnel, then test whether a new reader can explain the high-stakes choice.
Stronger response. This priority diagnoses the structural need, adds causal evidence, and includes a reader test before sentence-level polishing.

Retry: Close your first choice and open the other. Compare both explanations before continuing.

Example 2 — Conflicting Feedback

Case: One reader asks for more explanation of a recurring moth image; another says the image is most powerful when unexplained.

  1. Locate agreement: both noticed the image, so it has salience—meaning it attracts attention.
  2. Return to purpose: the writer wants ambiguity, not confusion.
  3. Revise by strengthening the moth’s patterned appearances without adding a direct explanation.
  4. Test whether readers can identify its emotional association while offering different interpretations.
  5. Decision: preserve ambiguity but improve coherence.

Example 3 — Publication Constraint

Case: A spoken-word piece depends on pauses and vocal emphasis, but the publication platform accepts only text and images.

  1. Name the constraint: audio cannot carry the performance.
  2. Identify essential meaning: rhythm and hesitation shape the speaker’s conflict.
  3. Adapt with line breaks, spacing, punctuation, and a short performance note.
  4. Add an accessible text description if a visual rhythm map is included.
  5. Author statement: explain the adaptation without claiming text duplicates live performance.

Synthesis Frame

For any new case, write: Goal → Evidence → Diagnosis → Priority → Revision → Test → Publication check.

Your Turn Before Independent Work

Pause and try: A draft has beautiful imagery but an ending that contradicts the protagonist’s stated goal. Name the first revision priority and a test.

Check your thinking — open after you try
Teacher model: First repair the action-and-goal relationship at the ending. Then ask a reader to explain what the protagonist wants and whether the final choice follows from that goal before polishing imagery.
