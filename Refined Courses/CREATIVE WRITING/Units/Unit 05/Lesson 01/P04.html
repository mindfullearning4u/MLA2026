Worked Example: Three Feedback Decisions

Example 1 — Accept the Problem, Adapt the Solution

Peer: “Cut the flashback; it is confusing.” Evidence shows the time shift is unmarked, but the flashback supplies motive. The writer keeps it, adds a sensory trigger and clear return line, then retests. Clarity improves without losing purpose.

Pause and Choose: A or B

Which response shows the strongest use of peer feedback?

A. The writer cuts the flashback immediately because a peer called it confusing.
Weaker response. This treats feedback as a command and sacrifices an important purpose without diagnosing the actual problem.
B. The writer confirms that the unmarked time shift causes confusion, keeps the flashback because it supplies motive, adds a sensory trigger and return line, and then retests clarity.
Stronger response. This response separates the reader’s valid difficulty from the proposed solution, then tests a revision that protects the writer’s purpose.

Retry: Close your first choice and open the other. Compare both explanations before continuing.

Example 2 — Resolve Conflict

Adult reader: “Explain the ending.” Peer: “Keep it ambiguous.” Criterion: readers should understand the choice but debate its consequence. Revision clarifies what the character does while preserving the uncertain result. Both responses identify different parts of the same problem.

Example 3 — Evaluate Online Advice

Anonymous comment: “Make the narrator likable.” It gives no evidence and conflicts with the goal of portraying self-deception. The writer sets aside the preference but tests whether the narrator’s motive is legible. A concrete remembered detail improves comprehension without making the narrator admirable.

Change-Log Model

Feedback → evidence → criterion → decision → before/after change → observed result. A strong log explains reasoning rather than merely listing edits.

Your Turn Before Independent Work

Pause and try: A peer says, “Make the ending happier.” Explain how you would diagnose the reader need before accepting, adapting, or declining the suggestion.

Check your thinking — open after you try
Teacher model: I would ask which evidence made the ending feel unsatisfying and compare that response with my intended effect. I might strengthen resolution without changing the emotional outcome, then test the revision with a new reader.
