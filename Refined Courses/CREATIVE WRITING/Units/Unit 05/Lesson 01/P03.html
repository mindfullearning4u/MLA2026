Lesson: The Feedback-to-Revision Protocol

Eight-Step Protocol

  1. Restate the writing goal and nonnegotiable purpose.
  2. Record feedback exactly before reacting.
  3. Classify it: response, diagnosis, suggestion, correction, or preference.
  4. Evaluate source credibility and relevant expertise.
  5. Locate textual evidence for the reported problem.
  6. Cluster repeated concerns and resolve conflicts by criterion.
  7. Write a revision hypothesis, test a change, and compare before/after effects.
  8. Accept, adapt, or set aside the advice and document the reason.

Pause and Choose: Diagnose Before Revising

A reader says, “The middle dragged.” Which writer response turns that reaction into a useful revision test?

A. “I will delete the middle because the reader disliked it.”
Weaker choice. The writer treats a response as a complete diagnosis and adopts a solution without finding evidence. The real issue might be repetition, weak stakes, unclear time, or misplaced detail.
B. “I will mark where the goal stalls, test whether repeated explanation slows the pace, and compare a tighter version with the original.”
Stronger choice. This preserves the reader’s valid experience, locates textual evidence, forms a revision hypothesis, and tests an effect before committing to the change.

Retry: If your first choice is weaker, close it and select the other choice before continuing. Compare both explanations so you can name why the stronger answer works.

Learning Routes

  • Visual: color-code feedback categories and repeated patterns.
  • Auditory: explain the revision hypothesis aloud before editing.
  • Hands-on: sort feedback cards into accept, adapt, test, and set aside.

Pause and Choose: Protect Purpose and Voice

Two readers ask for more explanation, but the piece intentionally uses a child narrator who understands less than the audience. Which decision is best?

A. Clarify the surrounding evidence while preserving the narrator’s limited language and perspective.
Stronger choice. This adapts the feedback to the writing goal. Readers receive stronger orientation, but the revision does not erase the deliberate voice that creates the piece’s dramatic tension.
B. Replace the child’s voice with an adult explanation because repeated feedback must always be accepted exactly.
Weaker choice. Repetition makes the concern worth investigating, not the proposed solution automatically correct. This change would violate the piece’s nonnegotiable purpose.

Retry: If your first choice is weaker, close it and select the other choice before continuing. Compare both explanations so you can name why the stronger answer works.

Online Feedback Safety

Do not share private identifying information or unpublished work in public spaces without permission. Evaluate anonymity, expertise, evidence, possible bias, platform incentives, and whether advice attempts to imitate another writer or erase your voice.
