P04 Worked Example

These examples show how integrated scene choices work together.

Worked Example 1: Planning an Integrated Scene

Step-by-step explanation: Follow each modeled step carefully and notice how the thinking moves from the task to evidence, reasoning, and a clear response.

Step 1: Scene purpose: show a narrator deciding whether to tell the truth.

Step 2: Point of view: first person, because the reader should feel the narrator's hesitation.

Step 3: Key element: an unopened envelope.

Step 4: Pacing: slow down the moment before the envelope is opened.

Why it works: Each choice supports hesitation and uncertainty.

Worked Example 2: Drafting the Scene

Step-by-step explanation: Follow each modeled step carefully and notice how the thinking moves from the task to evidence, reasoning, and a clear response.

Model scene: I held the envelope under the kitchen light until the paper warmed against my fingers. My name sat in the center, written in Dad's careful block letters. Across the table, Mom kept washing the same clean spoon. I slid one finger under the flap, then stopped. The glue gave a soft tearing sound. I pressed it closed again.

Step 1: Point of view is first person.

Step 2: The envelope and clean spoon are key elements.

Step 3: The pacing slows the moment with small actions.

Step 4: The reader effect is tension because the narrator almost opens the envelope but does not.

Worked Example 3: Revising for Alignment

Step-by-step explanation: Follow each modeled step carefully and notice how the thinking moves from the task to evidence, reasoning, and a clear response.

Problem: A draft includes a funny narrator, a tragic object, fast action, and a peaceful mood. The effects compete.

Step 1: Choose the main effect: tension.

Step 2: Keep details that create tension.

Step 3: Remove or revise details that create a different effect unless contrast is intentional.

Revision move: Change playful comments into uncertain observations, and slow the turning point.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: "I used point of view, pacing, setting, tone, irony, and an object, so the scene is automatically strong."

This is incorrect because a list of features does not prove the scene works.

Correct: "I used first person, the envelope detail, and slowed pacing to create hesitation before the narrator makes a choice."

This is correct because it explains how the choices work together.

Teachable Explanation: The correct example works because it applies the lesson skill deliberately and explains the reasoning instead of only naming an answer.
