Direct Teaching: Controlling Narrative Time and Pressure

Seven-Step Pace-and-Tension Method

  1. Name the desired outcome: what does the character need now?
  2. Set stakes: what changes if the attempt fails?
  3. Control information: decide what readers know, suspect, and still need.
  4. Choose time mode: scene, summary, pause, or ellipsis.
  5. Shape the beat: use action, reaction, decision, and consequence.
  6. Coordinate style: sentence rhythm, paragraph breaks, diction, and sensory field.
  7. Graph and revise: tension should change, not remain at maximum.

Tension rises through changed uncertainty and stakes—not through constant shouting.
Choose a learning route:
  • Visual: graph each paragraph from 1–5 and label the change.
  • Verbal: read versions aloud and listen for rhythm and pauses.
  • Hands-on: arrange scene cards to test where delay creates the strongest pressure.

Mood and Tone Are Different

Mood is the atmosphere readers experience. Tone is the narrating attitude. A narrator may describe an ominous room with amused, skeptical, or reverent tone. Name each separately and cite the language producing it.

Revision Test

Mark every paragraph S (scene), M (summary), P (pause), or E (ellipsis). Then write its tension level from 1–5. If several paragraphs have identical modes and levels, change information, stakes, or narrative time—not merely punctuation.

Common Failures

  • Equating short sentences with tension in every context.
  • Withholding basic orientation instead of meaningful information.
  • Keeping tension at maximum until it becomes flat.
  • Confusing a frightening mood with a frightened narrator.
  • Expanding routine movement while compressing the decisive moment.
