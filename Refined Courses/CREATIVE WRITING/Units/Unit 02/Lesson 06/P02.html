Notebook Title

Lesson 6: Pace, Tension, Mood, and Tone

Vocabulary

  • Pace: How fast or slow a scene feels to the reader.
  • Tension: The pressure, uncertainty, conflict, or anticipation that makes the reader keep reading.
  • Mood: The emotional atmosphere of a scene.
  • Tone: The narrator's attitude toward the subject, situation, character, or reader.
  • Delay: A pacing technique that makes the reader wait for information or outcome.
  • Compression: Moving quickly through less important time or action.
  • Expansion: Slowing down an important moment with detail, action, thought, or imagery.

Step 1: Decide What Needs to Be Fast or Slow

Not every part of a scene deserves the same amount of space. Writers often compress routine action and expand important action.

Compressed: After school, I walked home, made a sandwich, and waited for the call.

Expanded: The phone lit up. I reached for it, stopped, and watched my name reflect faintly in the black screen.

The walk home is routine, so it moves quickly. The phone moment matters, so it slows down.

Step 2: Build Tension with Waiting and Stakes

Tension grows when the reader understands that something could happen and that the outcome matters.

Weak tension: I opened the door.

Stronger tension: I opened the door slowly because the last text had said, Do not go inside yet.

The stronger version gives a reason to worry. The reader waits to learn what is behind the door and why the warning matters.

Step 3: Shape Mood and Tone

Mood is the atmosphere the reader feels. Tone is the narrator's attitude. You can shape both with diction, sensory details, sentence length, figurative language, and what the narrator notices.

Uneasy mood: The hallway light clicked twice, then held steady, too bright against the closed doors.

Playful tone: The hallway light made one heroic attempt at drama before deciding to behave like a normal light.

The event is similar, but the word choice changes the effect.
