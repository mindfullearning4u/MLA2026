Three, Then Two

The radio observatory closed to visitors at sunset. Nadiya stayed because the receiver had captured the same narrow signal three nights in a row. An observatory is a place equipped to study the sky; this one used enormous dishes to collect radio waves rather than visible light.

Her supervisor called the signal interference from a nearby machine. Nadiya agreed in the report, but she left the recording open on her screen.

At 8:17, the signal returned: three pulses, a pause, then two. Wind pushed grit against the control-room windows. Nadiya checked the maintenance log. Every local transmitter had been shut down.

She called her supervisor. Voicemail.

The signal repeated. Three. Pause. Two.

Nadiya slowed the playback and heard a faint echo after each pulse. The echo arrived later each time. Something was changing distance—or the dish was turning without authorization.

Outside, the largest dish moved one degree east. Its motor-status light remained dark.

Nadiya reached for the emergency cutoff. If she stopped the dish, she would lose the signal. If the motor had failed, waiting could strip the gears and destroy the instrument. The next sequence began before her hand touched the switch.

Three pulses. Pause. Three.

She cut the power. The dish continued turning.

Part A — Time and Tension

  1. Write a 120–150 word objective summary.
  2. Identify Nadiya’s goal, stakes, and central uncertainty.
  3. Label each paragraph S, M, P, or E.
  4. Graph the tension of each paragraph from 1–5 and explain three changes.
  5. Identify the decision point and explain why it receives expanded attention.

Part B — Information and Style

  1. List what readers know, suspect, and still need to learn after paragraph six.
  2. Explain how the repeated numbers change function.
  3. Analyze how paragraph length affects pace.
  4. Identify the sensory field and explain the mood it creates.
  5. Distinguish the passage’s mood from its restrained tone.

Part C — Vocabulary and Revision

  1. Use context to determine and write a definition for interference as used in radio observation.
  2. Rewrite the first two paragraphs as one concise summary and explain the effect.
  3. Expand the voicemail moment without adding new plot; justify three choices.
  4. Move one reveal earlier and explain how suspense changes.
  5. Write an alternate final sentence that lowers tension without resolving the mystery.

Part D — Original Narrative

  1. Plan a scene with a clear goal, stakes, knowledge gap, tension curve, and deliberate shifts among scene, summary, pause, and ellipsis.
  2. Write a polished 550–650 word scene with at least four meaningful changes in tension and a controlled sensory field.
  3. Write a 160–200 word rationale quoting four choices and explaining pace, information control, mood, and tone.
