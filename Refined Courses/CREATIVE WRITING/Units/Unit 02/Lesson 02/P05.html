The Borrowed Window

I told the landlord the crack had been there when we moved in. He stood in our kitchen with his clipboard pressed to his chest and studied the window as if it might answer for itself.

“It was smaller,” he said.

I looked toward the hallway. My brother’s bedroom door was closed. Behind it, the music had stopped.

The crack began at the lower corner and climbed through the glass in three pale branches. On cold mornings, Luis traced them with his finger and named them after rivers from our grandfather’s maps. Amazon. Orinoco. Magdalena. Yesterday he had called the longest one escape route.

“Temperature change,” I said. I had read online that old glass sometimes broke without impact.

The landlord crouched. Near the frame, a circle no wider than a coin interrupted the first branch. He touched it with his pen.

“Temperature,” he repeated.

From the hallway came the soft scrape of Luis moving something heavy. I began explaining the radiator, the draft, the week of freezing rain. Each fact was true. Together, they sounded arranged.

The landlord wrote a figure on his clipboard and turned the page before I could read it.

“I’ll return tomorrow,” he said.

After the door closed, Luis emerged carrying our grandfather’s wooden practice bow. The rubber tip was missing.

“Did he believe you?” he asked.

I studied the coin-sized circle in the glass. “He believes the window is broken.”

That night, snow entered through the crack and melted on the sill. I placed a bowl beneath it, then moved the bowl to the floor outside Luis’s room.

Part A — POV Map

  1. Write a 110–140 word accurate summary.
  2. Identify the narrator, grammatical person, and focalization.
  3. Create an access map: direct perception, memory, reported knowledge, inference, and blind spot.
  4. Identify three places where the narrator’s language signals uncertainty or self-protection.
  5. What does the reader infer that the narrator never states directly?

Part B — Distance and Reliability

  1. Analyze the psychic distance in the first paragraph.
  2. Explain how the river-name memory changes distance and characterization.
  3. Why does “Each fact was true. Together, they sounded arranged” complicate reliability?
  4. Distinguish what the narrator knows about the landlord’s belief from what is only inferred.
  5. Analyze the final bowl movement. What can and cannot be concluded?
  6. Identify one detail that acts as counterevidence to the narrator’s temperature explanation.

Part C — POV Revision

  1. Find one sentence that would become a POV violation if it directly named another character’s thought. Explain.
  2. Rewrite one paragraph in third-person limited while preserving the same focalizer and distance.
  3. Rewrite the landlord’s inspection from his limited viewpoint in 100–130 words. Do not reveal the siblings’ private conversation.
  4. Compare the two focalizations: what new irony or uncertainty appears?

Part D — Original Narrative

  1. Plan a POV system for a 400–500 word scene: narrator, focalizer, access boundaries, distance shifts, reliability strategy, and withheld fact.
  2. Write the polished scene. Include one mistaken inference, one observable clue that challenges it, and one controlled distance shift.
  3. Write a 120–160 word POV rationale explaining four choices, one limitation, and why another POV would produce a weaker effect.
