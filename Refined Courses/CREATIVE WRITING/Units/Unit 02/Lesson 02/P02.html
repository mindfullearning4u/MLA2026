Notebook Title

Lesson 2: Clearly Established Point of View

Vocabulary

  • Point of view: The perspective from which a story is told.
  • Narrator: The voice or speaker who tells the story.
  • First person: A point of view that uses I, me, my, we, or our because the narrator is inside the story.
  • Third person limited: A point of view that uses he, she, they, or names while staying close to one character's thoughts and experiences.
  • Third person omniscient: A point of view that uses he, she, they, or names and can reveal more than one character's thoughts or knowledge.
  • Narrative distance: How close or far the narration feels from a character's thoughts and emotions.

Step 1: Identify Who Is Telling the Story

Before you can analyze or write point of view, ask one basic question: Who is telling this story?

First person example: I held the envelope under my jacket and hoped no one noticed.

The narrator is a character in the story. The reader only knows what this character knows, sees, remembers, or guesses.

Third person limited example: Eli held the envelope under his jacket and hoped no one noticed.

The narrator is outside the story, but the reader is still close to Eli's thoughts.

Third person omniscient example: Eli held the envelope under his jacket, unaware that Mara had already seen the seal.

The narrator knows something Eli does not know. That wider knowledge changes the reader's experience.

Step 2: Know What the Narrator Can and Cannot Know

A clear point of view has boundaries. The narrator cannot know everything unless the chosen point of view allows it.

First person boundary: I can report my own thoughts, but I cannot directly know another character's thoughts unless they say or show them.

Third person limited boundary: The narrator can reveal one focus character's inner thoughts, but should not suddenly enter another character's mind.

Third person omniscient boundary: The narrator may reveal multiple characters' thoughts, but the shifts must be controlled and clear so the reader does not feel lost.

Step 3: Connect Point of View to Voice and Tone

Point of view affects voice because the narrator's position changes the sound of the story. A first person narrator can sound personal, defensive, funny, uncertain, or honest. Third person limited can feel close to a character while still giving the writer more control over sentence style. Third person omniscient can feel wider, more distant, or more dramatic.

Same event, different effect:

First person: I did not mean to lie. I only needed one more minute before everyone knew.

Third person limited: Nora did not mean to lie. She only needed one more minute before everyone knew.

The first version feels like Nora is speaking directly. The third person version gives a little distance while still staying close to Nora's thoughts.
