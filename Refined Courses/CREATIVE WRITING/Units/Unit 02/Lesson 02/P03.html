Direct Teaching: Controlling Point of View

The Six-Part POV Control Method

  1. Choose the narrative position: first person, third limited, or omniscient.
  2. Name the focalizer: whose perceptions organize this scene?
  3. Map access: list what the narrator can observe, remember, infer, and know directly.
  4. Set distance: decide when narration moves close to immediate thought or farther into summary.
  5. Control bias and reliability: determine what the narrator misreads, withholds, or cannot understand.
  6. Audit every sentence: verify that information has a legitimate source within the chosen POV.

Pronouns Do Not Determine Depth

“She entered the room” and “She entered the room; of course they had started without her” are both third person. The second moves closer because the narration carries the character’s judgment. Point of view includes grammatical person, but focalization and psychic distance determine the reader’s actual intimacy.

Build an Access Map

For the focal character, separate five categories:

  • Direct perception: what the character sees, hears, smells, touches, or tastes.
  • Memory: information genuinely known from the past.
  • Reported knowledge: what another source has communicated.
  • Inference: conclusions that must remain visibly uncertain.
  • Blind spots: facts unavailable or distorted by bias.

Use the map three ways:
  1. Visual: highlight known details blue, inferences gold, and blind spots red.
  2. Verbal: read each sentence aloud and complete “The focalizer knows this because…”
  3. Hands-on: sort details into three labeled columns before drafting.

Control Psychic Distance

Far: “Mina spent the winter avoiding the station.” Middle: “Mina crossed the street whenever the station clock appeared.” Close: “Not that clock again—four minutes slow and still accusing her.”

Writers shift distance to balance context, action, and emotional immediacy. The shift must remain inside the same consciousness unless a clearly signaled structure permits otherwise.

Reliability Is Evidence-Based

An unreliable narrator is not simply dishonest. Limited memory, fear, self-protection, misunderstanding, youth, or bias can shape the account. Give readers a basis for evaluating reliability through contradiction between statement and action, gaps, other testimony, or details the narrator notices but misinterprets.

Repair POV Violations

Violation: “I watched Theo close the letter. He knew I had discovered the truth.”

Observable revision: “I watched Theo close the letter. He folded it twice more after the crease was sharp.”

Inferential revision: “The extra folds made me think he knew.”

The revision either supplies observable evidence or marks the thought as the narrator’s inference.

POV Audit Frame

Who perceives this? How could the narrator know it? Is it observation, memory, report, or inference? What distance does the sentence create? Does the narrator’s interpretation match the evidence? What should remain outside the narrator’s knowledge?
