P03 Notebook Task - Part 2

Continue by learning how to avoid accidental point-of-view shifts.

Step 4: Keep the Point of View Stable

A point-of-view shift happens when the narration changes perspective in a way the reader cannot follow. Some advanced writers shift point of view on purpose, but in this lesson you are learning to establish one clear point of view first.

Confusing shift: I watched Mara open the locker. She felt nervous because she knew I had seen the note.

The first sentence is first person because of I. The second sentence suddenly reports Mara's feelings and knowledge. Unless Mara has told the narrator, a first person narrator cannot directly know that.

Clear first person revision: I watched Mara open the locker. Her hands shook, and she glanced at me before sliding the note into her sleeve.

This version keeps first person. The narrator observes Mara's actions instead of claiming to know her thoughts.

Step 5: Use Evidence from the Draft

When checking point of view, do not guess. Look for evidence in the words.

Pronoun evidence: I, me, my, we, and our usually signal first person.

Thought evidence: A sentence that reveals a thought, fear, hope, memory, or private knowledge tells you whose mind the narration enters.

Distance evidence: If the narrator reports only outside actions, the narration feels more distant. If the narrator reports thoughts and feelings, the narration feels closer.

Notebook Task Part 2

Part A: Copy this draft: I saw Devon hide the key. He was terrified that I would tell everyone.

Part B: Explain why the point of view is confusing.

Part C: Revise the draft so it stays in first person. Show Devon's fear through action, dialogue, or description instead of directly naming his private thought.

Part D: Write one sentence explaining how your revision keeps the narrator's knowledge realistic.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: A writer thinks point of view only means choosing I or she.

This is incorrect because point of view also controls knowledge, distance, voice, and reader trust.

Correct: A writer chooses a narrator, keeps that narrator's knowledge consistent, and uses that perspective to shape voice and tone.

Strong point of view helps the reader know whose experience they are following and what information is reliable.

Need help? Ask your TOR if your narrator reports another character's private thoughts without a reason the narrator could know them.
