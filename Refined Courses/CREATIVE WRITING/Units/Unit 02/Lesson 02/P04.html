P04 Worked Example

Study how each point-of-view choice changes what the reader knows and feels.

Example 1: First Person

Model: I kept the receipt folded in my palm until the ink blurred against my skin.

Step 1: Identify the narrator. The pronoun I shows that the narrator is a character inside the story.

Step 2: Identify what the narrator can know. The narrator can know their own action and physical feeling.

Step 3: Identify the effect. First person makes the moment feel personal and close. The reader is inside the narrator's experience.

Example 2: Third Person Limited

Model: Tessa kept the receipt folded in her palm until the ink blurred against her skin. She wished she had thrown it away before anyone asked questions.

Step 1: Identify the narrator. The narrator is outside the story and uses Tessa's name and she.

Step 2: Identify whose mind the narration enters. The sentence reveals Tessa's wish, so the narration is close to Tessa.

Step 3: Identify the effect. Third person limited creates closeness to one character while giving the writer a little distance from that character's voice.

Example 3: Fixing an Accidental Shift

Confusing draft: I watched Tessa fold the receipt. She wished she had thrown it away.

Step 1: Identify the starting point of view. The first sentence is first person because of I.

Step 2: Find the problem. The second sentence enters Tessa's mind, which the first person narrator cannot directly know.

Step 3: Revise using observable evidence. I watched Tessa fold the receipt. She looked toward the trash can, then tucked the paper deeper into her sleeve.

Explanation: The revision keeps first person because the narrator reports what can be observed.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: In first person, the narrator says what every character secretly feels.

This is incorrect because first person is limited to the narrator's own thoughts, observations, memories, and guesses.

Correct: In first person, the narrator can infer another character's feelings from action, dialogue, facial expression, or context, but should not directly report private thoughts as facts.
