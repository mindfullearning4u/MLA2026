Worked Example: Three Point-of-View Decisions

Example 1 — First-Person Reliability

Model: “I returned the library key on Monday. At least, I remember the brass tray and Mrs. Cole’s red sleeve. The key in my desk on Tuesday must have been the spare.”

  1. Position: first person.
  2. Access: personal memory and current discovery.
  3. Reliability clues: “At least,” incomplete visual fragments, and the unsupported word “must.”
  4. Reader advantage: readers can question the explanation while the narrator defends it.
  5. Model analysis: The narrator’s certainty exceeds the evidence. Fragmentary memory and rationalization create unreliability without proving intentional deception.
  6. Revision option: remove “must” to make the narrator more honest about uncertainty; keep it to emphasize self-protection.

Example 2 — Third-Limited Distance

Far draft: “Elias attended the hearing and became nervous when the photograph appeared.”

Close revision: “The photograph filled the screen. Wrong date in the corner. Wrong coat on his brother. Elias pressed his thumbnail beneath the table until the skin blanched.”

  1. Focalizer: Elias.
  2. Distance change: summary becomes immediate perception and bodily response.
  3. Interior control: fragments echo his rapid noticing without directly labeling fear.
  4. Boundary: the narration does not enter the lawyer’s or audience’s thoughts.
  5. Tradeoff: context decreases while intensity and uncertainty increase.
  6. Success criterion: readers should infer alarm and identify its visual trigger.

Example 3 — Repairing Head-Hopping

Confusing draft: “Mara smiled at Dev, hoping he believed her. Dev recognized the lie immediately but pretended to relax. She wondered why his shoulders remained tense.”

  1. Starting focalizer: Mara, through “hoping.”
  2. Violation: sentence two directly enters Dev’s knowledge.
  3. Limited revision: “Mara smiled at Dev, hoping he believed her. He leaned back, but his hands stayed locked around the cup. Why were his shoulders still tense?”
  4. Free indirect effect: the final question carries Mara’s thought while remaining third person.
  5. Dramatic irony option: If the reader must know Dev recognizes the lie, separate his viewpoint into a clearly marked later scene.
  6. Rationale: the revision preserves uncertainty inside Mara’s access and turns Dev’s body language into evidence.

Synthesis

Each example distinguishes direct knowledge from inference, identifies distance and reliability, and explains the tradeoff of a revision. Clear POV is not rigid pronoun consistency; it is disciplined control of access.

Common Failure and Revision

Weak: “Use first person because it is more personal.”

Stronger: “First person limits the reader to Ana’s incomplete memory, allowing her confident explanation to conflict with details the reader can evaluate independently.”
