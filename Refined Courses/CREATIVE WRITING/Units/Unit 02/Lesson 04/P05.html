Independent Work: “Two Shadows”

Read the complete story. Answer all 18 numbered tasks using precise evidence. Submit these responses with Page 2 vocabulary as Notebook Evidence.

Two Shadows

Every Thursday, Inez unlocked the museum’s west gallery and turned on the lights beneath the model ships. Her father had built the smallest one, a fishing boat no longer than her hand. Its paper sail carried a brown water stain shaped like a coastline.

The museum planned to replace the gallery with an interactive screen. The director called the cases “static interpretation,” meaning displays that presented information without changing or responding to visitors. Inez understood the criticism. Children pressed their hands to the glass, looked for thirty seconds, and moved on.

During inventory, she found a penciled note beneath her father’s boat: PORT AZUL, 1978—NOT A RESCUE. The label above the case described the boat as one used by volunteers after the hurricane. Inez had repeated that account during tours for years.

She searched the accession file—the museum’s official record of how an object was obtained. It contained a newspaper photograph of the boat returning with six families aboard. On the back, someone had written, Returned too late for the people at North Point.

Inez asked her aunt, who had survived the storm. “Your father carried people,” she said. “He also chose which inlet to enter first.” Her aunt would not say whether another choice had been possible.

At the final planning meeting, Inez did not argue that the old label was false. She argued that it was complete in only one direction. She proposed keeping three ships beside the new screen. Visitors could rotate each model and hear conflicting accounts from passengers, builders, and people who had waited on shore.

The director approved one case. On reopening day, the little boat remained beneath glass, but its sail cast two shadows because Inez had added a second lamp. A child listened to both recordings, then asked which one was true.

“Begin with what each person could see,” Inez said.

Part A — Literal and Structural Meaning

  1. Write a 120–150 word objective summary.
  2. Identify Inez’s initial goal, resistance, turning point, and consequence.
  3. Explain how the museum setting limits and enables her choices.
  4. Trace the causal chain from the penciled note to the redesigned exhibit.
  5. Explain the literal meaning of “complete in only one direction.”

Part B — Layers and Evidence

  1. Trace the boat through three contexts and explain how its meaning changes.
  2. Analyze the water stain as a possible symbol; state one reasonable limit on that interpretation.
  3. Explain the subtext of the aunt’s final sentence.
  4. Identify the central ambiguity and explain why it is purposeful rather than confusing.
  5. Analyze the two shadows as image, structure, and implication.

Part C — Style and Vocabulary

  1. Use context to determine and write a definition for inlet, then explain why that word matters to the conflict.
  2. Compare the connotations of rescue, returned, and waited.
  3. Analyze how the short final dialogue controls tone.
  4. Rewrite the reopening paragraph with a single long sentence; explain how pacing changes.
  5. Identify one example of understatement and explain its effect.

Part D — Original Application

  1. Plan a scene around an ordinary object that acquires two evidence-supported meanings. Map surface, recurrence, changed context, interpretation, and limit.
  2. Write a polished 500–600 word scene using subtext, purposeful ambiguity, one juxtaposition, and controlled syntax.
  3. Write a 150–190 word craft rationale quoting your scene and explaining how four choices create layers without sacrificing clarity.
