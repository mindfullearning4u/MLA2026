The Witness Loaf

The bakery window displayed one loaf after closing. Mr. Han called it the witness loaf because his mother had begun the custom during a flour shortage. “If there is one left,” she used to say, “we did not sell tomorrow.”

On the bakery’s final night, his daughter Mina arrived with a buyer’s contract. The new owner would keep the ovens but change the name. Mina placed the papers beside the cooling rack and said, “At least the work continues.”

Mr. Han wrapped the witness loaf. He folded the paper once, twice, then stopped before tying the string. Outside, a woman photographed the old sign.

“You should leave it in the window,” Mina said.

“Tomorrow has already been sold.”

She turned the contract face down. For several minutes, neither spoke. Then Mina cut the loaf in half. She placed one half in the window and wrapped the other for her father.

At midnight, the bakery was dark except for the display lamp. From the sidewalk, the half loaf looked whole until a person stood close enough to see the cut.

Analysis

  1. Write an 80–100 word objective summary.
  2. Identify the literal conflict and turning point.
  3. Explain the original literal purpose of the witness loaf.
  4. Trace how the loaf changes meaning across the passage.
  5. Analyze the subtext of “At least the work continues.”
  6. Explain what the repeated folding gesture suggests and state the limit of your inference.
  7. Interpret “Tomorrow has already been sold” at two levels.
  8. Analyze the contract being turned face down as action and implication.

Synthesis and Application

  1. Explain why the half loaf is purposeful ambiguity rather than a puzzle with one answer.
  2. Analyze how the final sentence controls distance and reader judgment.
  3. Rewrite the final paragraph in 100–130 words using different syntax and explain the changed effect.
  4. Write a 240–300 word original scene in which a repeated object gains a second meaning. Annotate literal layer, pattern, subtext, ambiguity, and stylistic choice.
