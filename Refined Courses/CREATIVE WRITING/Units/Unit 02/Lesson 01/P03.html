Direct Teaching: Building Narrative Movement

Pause and Try: Identify the Transition Job

Sentence: “When the station clock chimed, Noor remembered the voicemail she had refused to answer.” Decide whether the transition is mainly temporal, spatial, causal, or associative. Explain what triggers the movement.

Check your thinking

Associative is the strongest answer. The sound in the present—the clock—triggers a memory. “When” gives time information, but the transition’s deeper job is to connect a present sensation to an earlier experience.

Pause and Choose: Which Revision Builds Cause?

Original: “Mira closed the notebook. Then she left the interview.” Which revision creates a clear causal hinge rather than merely changing the time marker? Choose before opening either explanation.

A. “Mira closed the notebook. Later, she left the interview.”
Not the best choice. “Later” changes chronology but still hides why Mira leaves. The actions remain adjacent events instead of a cause-and-effect sequence.
B. “The final answer contradicted every date in her notes. Mira closed the notebook and left before the next question.”
✓ Correct—the cause is visible. The contradiction supplies the causal hinge. Readers can trace how new evidence changes Mira’s action without the narrator explaining every emotion.

Retry: If your first choice is weaker, close it and select the other choice before continuing. Compare both explanations so you can name why the stronger answer works.

The Six-Stage Scene Method

  1. Orient: establish who, where, and the immediate situation with only necessary context.
  2. Give the character a local goal: define what the character wants in this scene.
  3. Introduce resistance: create an obstacle, uncertainty, or competing desire.
  4. Dramatize choices: use action, dialogue, detail, and thought to reveal decisions.
  5. Create a turn: something changes—knowledge, power, direction, relationship, or expectation.
  6. Land on consequence: end with a result or new question that propels the next unit.

Choose Scene or Summary Deliberately

Use scene for moments of conflict, decision, discovery, or irreversible change. Use summary for repetition, travel, routine, or background whose details are less important than duration. Strong narratives often move from summary into scene: “For three weeks, Dani ignored the messages. On the twenty-second morning, her brother appeared at the bus stop.”

Use Exposition Under Pressure

Background becomes more engaging when it answers a present question. Instead of opening with a family history, reveal only the piece that changes the current action. Readers should receive context when they need it—not merely because the writer knows it.

Transitions Have Jobs

  • Temporal: clarifies sequence or duration—By dusk, three days earlier, while.
  • Spatial: reorients location—across the courtyard, inside the stairwell.
  • Causal: reveals motive or consequence—because of that, therefore, instead.
  • Associative: links present perception to memory or idea through an image, sound, object, or phrase.

The best transition is often embedded in action rather than added as a mechanical connector.

Control Pace with Narrative Distance

To slow a moment, increase sensory precision, action beats, dialogue turns, interior decision, and sentence-level attention. To accelerate, summarize repeated actions, omit predictable steps, and use syntax that groups movement. Slow writing is not automatically important; the amount of space must match the moment’s narrative weight.

Pause and Choose: Match Pacing to Narrative Weight

A character has repeated the same unsuccessful search for three weeks. The fourth search reveals a hidden letter. Which plan uses scene and summary purposefully?

A. Dramatize every search in equal detail so the reader sees all the work.
Not the best choice. Equal space gives routine attempts the same narrative weight as the discovery. Repetition can slow the story without adding a meaningful change.
B. Summarize the repeated searches, then slow into a scene when the letter changes what the character knows.
✓ Correct—the pacing reflects importance. Summary compresses routine time, while scene-level detail emphasizes the discovery, choice, and consequence that move the narrative forward.

Check Coherence

For every paragraph, ask: What changed? Why does this follow? What does the character now know or choose? If the only answer is “time passed,” the transition may be chronological but not meaningful.
