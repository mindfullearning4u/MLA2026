Notebook Title

Lesson 1: Narrative Techniques and Transitions

Vocabulary

  • Narrative: Writing that tells a story or shows a sequence of related events.
  • Narrative technique: A craft move a writer uses to develop a story, such as action, dialogue, description, reflection, pacing, or sensory detail.
  • Action: What a character does in a scene.
  • Dialogue: The exact words characters speak.
  • Description: Details that help the reader picture a person, place, object, or moment.
  • Reflection: A character's thought, realization, memory, or interpretation of what is happening.
  • Transition: A word, phrase, sentence, or paragraph move that shows how one moment connects to the next.

Step 1: Understand the Difference Between Event and Scene

An event tells what happened. A scene lets the reader experience what happened. Creative writers usually need both, but a story becomes more vivid when important moments are shown as scenes.

Event list: Maya found the key. She opened the shed. She saw the bicycle.

Scene version: Maya brushed dirt from the small brass key. At the shed door, she paused, listening for her brother's footsteps. The lock clicked open, and there, leaning against the wall, was the bicycle she thought had been sold.

The scene version uses action, description, and a moment of hesitation. The reader does not just receive information. The reader experiences discovery with the character.

Step 2: Learn the Main Narrative Techniques

Action shows what a character does. Action moves the scene forward. Example: He shoved the envelope under the door.

Dialogue shows what a character says. Dialogue can reveal conflict, personality, relationship, or hidden information. Example: "You promised you would not open it," Ana said.

Description gives the reader concrete details. Description should not be random. It should help the reader understand setting, mood, character, or conflict. Example: The envelope was damp at one corner, as if someone had held it too tightly.

Reflection shows what a character thinks or understands. Reflection helps the reader know why the moment matters. Example: For the first time, Ana wondered whether the promise had protected her or trapped her.

Step 3: Use Transitions to Control Movement

A transition is not only a word like then. A transition can show time, place, cause, contrast, or a shift in focus.

Time transition: Later that afternoon, the hallway was empty.

Place transition: Across the street, the bakery lights flickered on.

Cause transition: Because the note was unsigned, Jamal read it twice.

Contrast transition: Outside, everyone cheered. Inside, Lena stared at the cracked trophy.

Focus transition: What Lena noticed first was not the trophy, but the dust on her father's shoes.
