The Crossing List

For four summers, Ana ferried supplies across Lake Maribel with her uncle. He kept the crossing list folded beneath the compass: fuel, rope, first-aid kit, radio, weather. Ana’s job was to read each item aloud before he untied the boat.

On the first morning she crossed alone, the list had grown a sixth line in her uncle’s handwriting: Do not trust the western marker after rain.

The sky was clear. Ana checked the forecast twice and pushed away from the dock at seven. Halfway across, the eastern shore disappeared behind a white wall of fog.

She reduced the engine and followed the compass. Then something red flashed to her left—the western marker, close enough to touch. According to the chart, it should have been two hundred yards away.

Ana reached for the radio. Static answered. She remembered her uncle laughing during a storm two years earlier: “A list is only useful until the lake adds something.” At the time, she had thought he meant waves.

The marker’s chain scraped the hull. Ana cut the engine. Beneath the fog, water moved steadily against the reeds; she could hear the current pulling south. She turned the compass in her palm, comparing its needle with the sound.

For ten minutes, she let the boat drift. The red marker vanished behind her. When the church bell sounded from the northern shore, Ana restarted the engine and steered toward the third ring.

She reached the supply dock at nine with two wet crates and no fuel to spare. The manager pointed at the folded paper. “Your uncle’s list work?”

Ana crossed out the sixth line. Beneath it she wrote: After rain, verify every instrument with two other kinds of evidence.

Part A — Narrative Architecture

  1. Write a 110–140 word accurate summary.
  2. Identify Ana’s local goal, resistance, turn, and consequence.
  3. Where does summary end and scene begin? Explain the effect of the shift.
  4. Identify two pieces of exposition delivered under present pressure.
  5. Analyze how the crossing list changes function from beginning to end.

Part B — Technique and Transition

  1. Trace three transitions in time, space, or cause. Classify each and explain its job.
  2. Explain how the uncle’s remembered statement creates an associative transition.
  3. Analyze the interaction of sound, visual detail, and action during the drift.
  4. How does sentence length control pacing when the marker appears?
  5. Why is the ten-minute drift compressed rather than fully dramatized?
  6. Explain how the church bell functions as evidence and structural turn.

Part C — Revision Decisions

  1. Identify one transition that could become mechanical if overexplained.
  2. Rewrite one paragraph to slow the moment without changing events. Explain three changes.
  3. Rewrite another moment as concise summary to accelerate pace. Defend the choice.
  4. Propose a different ending transition and explain its tradeoff.

Part D — Original Narrative

  1. Plan a scene using the six-stage method. Include goal, resistance, turn, consequence, one scene-to-summary shift, and three types of transition.
  2. Write a polished 400–500 word narrative scene in which a character’s trusted routine fails and must be revised. Do not copy the lake, list, compass, or family situation.
  3. Write a 120–160 word craft rationale identifying four techniques, explaining two transition choices, and defending what you expanded and compressed.
