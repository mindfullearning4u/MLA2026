P04 Worked Example

Each example shows how to move from element, to evidence, to meaning, to writing transfer.

Worked Example 1: Character Detail

Step-by-step explanation: Follow each modeled step carefully and notice how the thinking moves from the task to evidence, reasoning, and a clear response.

Passage: Niko answered every question with a shrug, but he kept smoothing the folded schedule in his pocket.

Step 1: Identify the element. The element is a character detail.

Step 2: Identify the evidence. Niko shrugs, but he keeps smoothing the schedule.

Step 3: Explain meaning. The contrast suggests that Niko acts casual but is actually worried about what comes next.

Step 4: Transfer the move. In your own scene, give a character a small repeated action that reveals an emotion the character refuses to say directly.

Worked Example 2: Setting Detail

Step-by-step explanation: Follow each modeled step carefully and notice how the thinking moves from the task to evidence, reasoning, and a clear response.

Passage: The classroom was empty except for one desk turned backward and a line of muddy footprints drying near the door.

Step 1: Identify the element. The element is setting.

Step 2: Identify the evidence. The backward desk and muddy footprints are specific setting details.

Step 3: Explain meaning. The details suggest that something happened before the scene began. The setting creates mystery and tension.

Step 4: Transfer the move. In your own writing, use setting evidence to imply an event without explaining it directly.

Worked Example 3: Object Detail

Step-by-step explanation: Follow each modeled step carefully and notice how the thinking moves from the task to evidence, reasoning, and a clear response.

Passage: Before June spoke, she placed the house key in the center of the table.

Step 1: Identify the element. The element is an object detail.

Step 2: Identify the evidence. The house key is placed in the center of the table.

Step 3: Explain meaning. A house key can suggest belonging, leaving, trust, or a decision about home. Placing it on the table makes the object feel important.

Step 4: Transfer the move. Choose one object in your scene and place it where the reader cannot ignore it.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: Adding many details because more detail always means better style.

This is incorrect because extra details can distract the reader if they do not support meaning, mood, conflict, or character.

Correct: Choose a few details that do important work. A strong detail should help the reader understand the character, conflict, setting, or meaning more clearly.

Teachable Explanation: The correct example works because it applies the lesson skill deliberately and explains the reasoning instead of only naming an answer.
