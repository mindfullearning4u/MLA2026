Independent Work: “The Blank Stopwatch”

Read the complete story closely. Answer all 18 questions in complete sentences, then write the required scene and craft rationale. Submit this work with Page 2 vocabulary as Notebook Evidence.

The Blank Stopwatch

On the morning the neighborhood pool was scheduled to close, Amara found the box of stopwatches beneath the lifeguard desk. Their cords were braided with old sunscreen and dust. Most had stopped at different times. One still ticked, although its display was blank.

The city notice called the pool “underused infrastructure.” Amara knew the phrase from budget meetings where every sentence seemed designed to have no owner. The pool was never crowded now. The apartment towers had raised their parking fees, the bus route ended six blocks away, and the recreation department had shortened the season until even warm September afternoons arrived after the gates were locked.

Her grandfather had taught lessons there for thirty-one summers. “Water tells the truth about panic,” he used to say. “It shows you what the body believes.” After his stroke, he remembered the pool in fragments: a whistle, a red towel, the deep-end ladder cold beneath his palm. He no longer remembered that Amara had been his final student.

At noon, Deputy Director Sayeed arrived with a photographer. Amara had prepared attendance charts and letters from residents. Sayeed listened, then pointed toward the cracked filtration housing—the protective casing around the pool’s filtering equipment. “Stories do not repair pumps,” she said. It was not cruel. That made the sentence harder to resist.

A boy named Luis appeared outside the fence holding a paper bag. He had failed Amara’s swim test twice that summer, freezing whenever his feet left the bottom. From the bag he removed the blue stopwatch her grandfather had given him. “Coach said I could keep it when I crossed by myself,” Luis said. “I did it yesterday at my cousin’s building.” The watch had stopped at forty-seven seconds.

The photographer lifted his camera, but Amara raised a hand. She asked Luis whether he wanted his picture taken. He shook his head. She placed the stopwatch beside her charts instead. One object could not prove that the pool was affordable, safe, or fairly funded. Still, it changed the question. The charts counted visits; the stopped watch recorded a promise completed elsewhere.

Amara revised her proposal. She stopped arguing that nothing should change. She paired the repair costs with a transportation survey, a reduced-water training plan, and letters from two apartment managers willing to host beginner classes during construction. The city approved one more season—not a rescue, Sayeed warned, but a test.

That evening, Amara wound the working stopwatch though its face remained blank. The ticking did not tell her how much time they had. It only refused to let silence pretend that time had stopped.

Part A — Element Map

  1. Summarize the central conflict in one precise sentence.
  2. State Amara’s initial desire and the two strongest forces resisting it.
  3. Explain how transportation, scheduling, and the damaged pump make setting an active force.
  4. Trace the causal chain from Luis’s arrival to the revised proposal.
  5. Identify the turning point and explain why it changes Amara’s strategy.

Part B — Meaning and Pattern

  1. Trace the stopwatch motif through at least three appearances.
  2. Explain how the meaning of “stopped time” changes by the ending.
  3. Analyze the contrast between the attendance charts and Luis’s watch.
  4. What does Sayeed’s statement reveal about the story’s central tension?
  5. Write an earned theme statement that includes a qualification or condition.

Part C — Style and Revision

  1. Use context to determine and write a definition for infrastructure, then analyze the tone of the phrase “underused infrastructure.”
  2. Explain how the sentence “It was not cruel” complicates Sayeed.
  3. Identify one syntactic choice that controls pacing and explain its effect.
  4. Rewrite the photographer scene in a sentimental style, then explain why the original restraint is stronger or weaker.
  5. Revise one paragraph to increase urgency without adding new plot information.

Part D — Original Application

  1. Plan a scene in which a setting restriction forces a costly choice. Name the desire, resistance, setting force, turning point, and motif.
  2. Write the scene in 450–550 words. Repeat the motif at least twice with changed meaning, and use syntax to expand the turning point.
  3. Write a 140–180 word craft rationale explaining three element relationships and two stylistic choices using quoted evidence from your scene.
