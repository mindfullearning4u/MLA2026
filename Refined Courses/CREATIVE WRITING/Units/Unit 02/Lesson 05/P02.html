Notebook Title

Lesson 5: Narrator Perspective, Irony, and Satire

Vocabulary

  • Narrator perspective: The narrator's position, knowledge, attitude, and way of interpreting events.
  • Reliable narrator: A narrator the reader has reason to trust.
  • Unreliable narrator: A narrator whose version of events may be incomplete, biased, mistaken, or dishonest.
  • Irony: A contrast between what is expected and what actually happens, or between what is said and what is meant.
  • Satire: Writing that uses irony, exaggeration, or humor to criticize a behavior, habit, rule, or idea.
  • Blind spot: Something the narrator does not understand about the situation, other people, or self.

Step 1: Separate Point of View from Perspective

Point of view tells who is narrating and what pronouns or access the story uses. Perspective goes further. It asks how the narrator understands the world.

Point of view question: Who tells the story?

Perspective question: What does the narrator notice, value, misunderstand, ignore, or judge?

Example: Two first person narrators can describe the same cafeteria. One may notice who sits alone. Another may notice who has the newest shoes. Their point of view is both first person, but their perspectives are different.

Step 2: Check Reliability

A narrator is reliable when the reader has reason to trust the narrator's understanding. A narrator may be unreliable if the narrator is biased, missing information, exaggerating, lying, or misunderstanding a situation.

Passage: I was clearly the calmest person in the room. True, I had knocked over two chairs and shouted at the clock, but that only proved everyone else was making me nervous.

What the narrator says: The narrator claims to be calm.

What the evidence shows: The narrator knocks over chairs and shouts at a clock.

Reader effect: The reader doubts the narrator's self-description. That gap creates irony.

Step 3: Understand Irony and Satire

Irony happens when there is a meaningful gap. A character may say one thing but mean another. A narrator may believe one thing while the evidence suggests something else. A result may be opposite of what was expected.

Satire uses irony, exaggeration, or humor to criticize something. In creative writing, satire might criticize a school rule, social habit, popularity contest, online behavior, or a character's exaggerated obsession.

Satire example: The school announced a new "stress-free week" by assigning three reflection forms, two surveys, and a mandatory relaxation worksheet due by midnight.

The example is satirical because it exaggerates a contradiction: a stress-free week becomes stressful.

Notebook Task Part 1

Part A: Copy the vocabulary words and definitions.

Part C: Write three sentences explaining what the narrator claims, what the evidence shows, and how the gap creates irony.
