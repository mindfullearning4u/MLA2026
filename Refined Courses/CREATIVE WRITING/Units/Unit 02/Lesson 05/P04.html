Worked Example: Three Irony and Satire Models

Example 1 — Dramatic Irony

Text: Readers see Priya slide the scholarship letter beneath a cookbook. Later her father says, “At least we have no more decisions to make,” and she agrees.

  1. Reader knowledge: the letter exists.
  2. Father’s knowledge: he believes no choice remains.
  3. Priya’s knowledge: she knows both the letter and his belief.
  4. Gap: readers understand the agreement is concealment.
  5. Effect: ordinary dialogue creates tension and complicates sympathy.
  6. Control: delay the letter’s contents so irony does not become complete explanation.

Example 2 — Unreliable Perspective

Narrator: “The meeting ended successfully. Only three people left before my presentation finished, and the chairperson was so impressed she kept checking the clock.”

  1. Claim: success.
  2. Evidence: departures and repeated clock-checking.
  3. Reader inference: the audience may be disengaged or rushed.
  4. Reliability signal: the narrator interprets negative evidence as praise.
  5. Effect: humor mixes with vulnerability because self-protection may motivate the misreading.
  6. Revision: add one detail the narrator cannot easily reinterpret, but do not call the narrator delusional.

Example 3 — Satire With a Clear Norm

Premise: A town creates a Department of Authentic Spontaneity. Residents must schedule unscripted moments two weeks in advance.

  1. Target: institutional attempts to manufacture authenticity.
  2. Implied norm: genuine human connection requires freedom and attention.
  3. Distortion: spontaneity permits, surprise inspections, and standardized laughter.
  4. Incongruity: bureaucracy manages what cannot be bureaucratically produced.
  5. Consequence: residents become anxious about reacting incorrectly.
  6. Deadpan tone: official language presents absurd rules as reasonable.
  7. Ethical check: critique the system and incentives, not residents trapped inside it.
