P04 Worked Example

Study how narrator effects are built from evidence, not labels alone.

Example 1: Reliable Perspective

Passage: I did not know why Mara left the room, but I saw her wipe her eyes before she closed the door.

Step 1: Identify what the narrator knows. The narrator knows what was observed.

Step 2: Identify what the narrator does not know. The narrator does not claim to know Mara's private reason.

Step 3: Explain the effect. The narrator feels reliable because the narration separates observation from uncertainty.

Example 2: Irony

Passage: I handled the emergency beautifully, if you ignore the fact that I screamed into a backpack and hid behind the vending machine.

Step 1: Identify the narrator's claim. The narrator says the emergency was handled beautifully.

Step 2: Identify the evidence. The narrator screamed and hid.

Step 3: Explain the gap. The claim and evidence conflict, which creates irony.

Example 3: Satire

Passage: To prove our club valued student voices, the officers created a suggestion box, locked it, lost the key, and congratulated themselves for listening.

Step 1: Identify the target. The target is fake listening or performative leadership.

Step 2: Identify the satirical method. The scene uses irony and exaggeration.

Step 3: Explain the effect. The reader sees that the club claims to value voices while preventing suggestions from being heard.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: Making satire mean insults toward a person.

This is incorrect because satire criticizes a behavior, habit, system, rule, or idea. It should have a clear target and craft purpose.

Correct: Use exaggeration or irony to reveal a contradiction, such as a "simple" process that requires twelve forms.
