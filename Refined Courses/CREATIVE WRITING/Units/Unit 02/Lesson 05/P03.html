Direct Teaching: Perspective, Irony, and Satire

The Evidence-Gap Method

  1. Record the claim: what does the narrator or character say is true?
  2. Collect visible evidence: what details can readers evaluate independently?
  3. Map knowledge: who knows what, and when?
  4. Name the gap: claim versus evidence, expectation versus result, or character knowledge versus reader knowledge.
  5. Classify carefully: verbal, situational, or dramatic irony.
  6. Explain the effect: tension, humor, criticism, sympathy, or revised judgment.
  7. For satire, identify target and norm: what is criticized, and what better value is implied?

Pause and Choose: Classify the Knowledge Gap

Readers know Priya received a scholarship letter, but her father believes no decision remains. Priya calmly agrees with him while hiding the letter. Which explanation best identifies the irony and its effect?

A. “It is verbal irony because Priya says something she knows is false.”
Not the best choice. Her statement may involve concealment, but the scene’s central effect comes from readers knowing a fact the father does not. Focusing only on the spoken line misses the larger knowledge structure.
B. “It is dramatic irony because readers know about the letter while the father does not; the gap increases tension and complicates judgment of Priya.”
✓ Correct—the classification follows the evidence. The answer maps who knows what and explains how unequal knowledge changes the reader’s response, which is the defining work of dramatic irony.

Retry: If your first choice is weaker, close it and select the other choice before continuing. Compare both explanations so you can name why the stronger answer works.

Satire Design Frame

Target → Implied norm → Distortion → Consequence.

If a school rewards “efficiency” by grading essays without reading them, the target is performative assessment, the implied norm is attentive evaluation, the distortion is automated praise, and the consequence reveals who is harmed.

Ethical and Craft Limits

  • Target conduct, systems, or ideas—not vulnerable identity.
  • Make the implied norm understandable.
  • Exaggerate a real tendency rather than invent random absurdity.
  • Give consequences to the people affected, not only clever jokes.
  • Do not label every surprise “ironic.”

Pause and Try — Map the Knowledge Gap

Try first: In the scholarship-letter example, write one sentence each for what the reader knows, what the father knows, and what Priya knows. Then name the effect of the gap.

Reveal the teacher’s reasoning

The reader and Priya know the letter exists; the father believes there is no decision left. Priya’s agreement therefore becomes concealment. The unequal knowledge creates dramatic irony, tension, and a more complicated judgment of her choice.

Pause and Choose: Which Satirical Analysis Is Stronger?

A school awards “listening points” only when a camera detects nodding. Which analysis identifies the actual target and the healthier norm implied by the satire?

A. “The satire targets students because they nod too much.”
Not the best choice. Students are adapting to the incentive. This answer mistakes the people affected by the system for the conduct the satire criticizes.
B. “The satire targets measurement that replaces real listening; its implied norm is attentive human response.”
✓ Correct—the target and norm are precise. The camera rewards visible compliance instead of understanding. Naming that mismatch explains the critique rather than merely retelling the absurd situation.
