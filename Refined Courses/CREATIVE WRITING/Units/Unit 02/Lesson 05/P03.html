P03 Notebook Task - Part 2

Continue by learning how to write narrator effects without confusing the reader.

Step 4: Write the Evidence Before the Judgment

If you want readers to question a narrator, do not simply announce that the narrator is wrong. Show evidence that creates a gap.

Weak version: I was a very organized person, but I was actually not organized.

Stronger version: I was the most organized person in the club, which is why my permission slip was only two weeks late and only slightly stained with juice.

The stronger version lets the reader notice the contradiction. The narrator claims organization, but the evidence suggests disorganization.

Step 5: Keep Satire Focused

Satire should have a target. The target is the behavior, habit, rule, or idea being criticized. Without a target, a scene may become random exaggeration.

Target: A school says it values rest but assigns too many tasks.

Satirical detail: A mandatory relaxation worksheet due by midnight.

Reader effect: The reader sees the contradiction and understands the criticism.

Notebook Task Part 2

Part A: Choose a harmless target for mild satire: overcomplicated rules, fake productivity, popularity contests, or phone obsession.

Part B: Write one sentence stating the target.

Part C: Write a 3-4 sentence scene where a narrator's perspective or exaggeration reveals the problem.

Part D: Explain the gap between what the narrator says and what the reader understands.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: "The narrator is unreliable because I do not like the narrator."

This is incorrect because reliability must be based on evidence, not personal dislike.

Correct: "The narrator is unreliable because the narrator claims to be calm, but the evidence shows the narrator knocking over chairs and shouting at a clock."

This is correct because it connects the claim to specific evidence.

Need help? Ask your TOR if your satire has no clear target or if your irony is not supported by evidence.

Teachable Explanation: The correct example works because it applies the lesson skill deliberately and explains the reasoning instead of only naming an answer.
