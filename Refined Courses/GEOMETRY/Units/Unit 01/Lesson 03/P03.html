How to Test a Conjecture

Example: A student notices that several pairs of angles with matching arc marks are congruent.

Step 1: State the conjecture.

Angles with matching arc marks are congruent.

Step 2: Check the evidence.

The diagram markings show which angles match.

m∠A = m∠B

Step 3: Write a supported conclusion.

Angle A is congruent to angle B because the angles have matching arc marks.

Recognizing Flawed Reasoning

Reasoning is flawed when the conclusion does not follow from the evidence.

Problem: A student claims two segments are congruent because they are both drawn horizontally.

Incorrect:

The segments are congruent because they both go left to right.

Misconception: Direction is being used as evidence for length.

Correct:

The segments can be concluded congruent only if matching tick marks, equal measurements, or given information support that claim.

Correction: A valid argument must connect the conclusion to relevant evidence.

Reasoning Reflection Categories

As you complete reasoning tasks, sort your explanations into three categories.

  • Supported: My claim has relevant evidence and a clear explanation.
  • Incomplete: My claim may be true, but I did not explain enough evidence.
  • Flawed: My reasoning uses the wrong evidence or relies on appearance.

These categories help you revise your arguments before formal proof begins.

Why This Matters Mathematically

Later Geometry lessons require students to justify conclusions, not just identify answers. Reasoning habits prepare you to decide whether an argument is valid.

The habits in this lesson support proof, constructions, angle relationships, congruence, similarity, circles, and coordinate Geometry.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: Make a conclusion from how the diagram appears without checking the given information, construction marks, or stated relationships.

Correct: Use the given information, marked relationships, construction steps, or theorem evidence before writing a conclusion.

Teachable Explanation: Geometry reasoning depends on evidence. A diagram can guide your thinking, but the conclusion must be supported by definitions, markings, construction steps, or proven relationships.
