Before the Worked Examples

Do not start by hunting for an answer choice. Set up the physics first. Identify the object or system, read the given stimulus, and choose the model or formula that belongs to that evidence.

Physics Unit 2 is about interactions. Slow down before solving. First identify the object or system. Next identify every force, mass, velocity, distance, or interaction in the stimulus. Then choose the correct model: free-body diagram, Newton's law, gravity model, orbit model, momentum table, or force comparison chart.

Worked Example 1: Calculate acceleration

Problem: A 4 kg cart has a net force of 20 N. What is acceleration?

  1. Step 1: Write the relationship: a = F / m. Why: acceleration is unknown.
  2. Step 2: Substitute values: a = 20 N / 4 kg. Why: use net force and mass.
  3. Step 3: Divide: 20 / 4 = 5. Why: acceleration is force per mass.

Answer: The acceleration is 5 m/s^2.

Interpretation: This example shows how a veteran Physics teacher would slow down, identify the system, use the diagram/table/formula, and explain the result.

Worked Example 2: Use first law

Problem: A puck slides at constant velocity. What is the net force?

  1. Step 1: Constant velocity means no change in velocity. Why: acceleration is zero.
  2. Step 2: If acceleration is zero, net force is zero. Why: F = ma.
  3. Step 3: State balanced forces. Why: no net force is acting.

Answer: The net force is 0 N.

Interpretation: This example shows how a veteran Physics teacher would slow down, identify the system, use the diagram/table/formula, and explain the result.

Worked Example 3: Compare mass effect

Problem: Why does Trial C accelerate less than Trial B?

  1. Step 1: Both have 20 N. Why: force is controlled.
  2. Step 2: Trial C has more mass. Why: mass resists acceleration.
  3. Step 3: Greater mass with same force gives smaller acceleration. Why: a = F / m.

Answer: More mass reduces acceleration when force stays the same.

Interpretation: This example shows how a veteran Physics teacher would slow down, identify the system, use the diagram/table/formula, and explain the result.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: More mass always means more acceleration.

Correct: With the same net force, more mass means less acceleration.

Teachable explanation: The correct version uses the mapped Physics evidence. The incorrect version skips the system, model, unit, direction, or relationship.
