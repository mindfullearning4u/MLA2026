P04 Worked Example

Before the Worked Examples

Do not start by hunting for an answer choice. Start by setting up the physics. The setup is part of the answer.

Physics often feels difficult because one sentence can contain several ideas at once. Slow the sentence down. First identify the object or system. Next identify the quantity being measured. Then identify the unit, direction, graph axis, table column, or model feature. Only after that should you calculate or choose an answer.

Student QuestionWhat To Do
What is the claim?Find the sentence that answers the investigation question.
What is the evidence?Find the measured value, observation, table row, or model feature.
What is the reasoning?Explain the physics idea that connects the evidence to the claim.

Teacher move: A veteran teacher would not let students say "because it happened." The student must point to the exact data or observation and explain how it supports the claim.

Worked Example 1: Identify evidence

Problem: A student claims that a higher starting position made a cart move faster. Which data support the claim?

  1. Step 1: Find the claim: starting height affected speed. Why: the claim tells what relationship must be supported.
  2. Step 2: Find measured values for both variables. Why: evidence must include actual observations or measurements.
  3. Step 3: Compare Trial A and Trial B. Why: the higher start also has the greater measured speed.

Answer: Trial B started higher and had a greater speed, so the data support the claim.

Interpretation: This example shows how a veteran physics teacher would slow down, identify the evidence, and connect the evidence to the concept before choosing an answer.

Worked Example 2: Separate observation from inference

Problem: A motion sensor records that a cart moved 2.4 meters in 3 seconds. Is this observation or inference?

  1. Step 1: Check whether it was directly measured. Why: observations come from data or tools.
  2. Step 2: The motion sensor recorded distance and time. Why: those are measured quantities.
  3. Step 3: Classify it as observation. Why: it does not yet explain why the cart moved.

Answer: Measured distance and time are observations.

Interpretation: This example shows how a veteran physics teacher would slow down, identify the evidence, and connect the evidence to the concept before choosing an answer.

Worked Example 3: Write CER reasoning

Problem: Use the cart data to explain why Trial B supports the claim.

  1. Step 1: State the claim clearly. Why: the reader must know the conclusion.
  2. Step 2: Use evidence: 30 cm and 1.8 m/s compared with 10 cm and 1.1 m/s. Why: numbers make the support checkable.
  3. Step 3: Add reasoning about energy and motion. Why: reasoning connects the data to the physics idea.

Answer: A complete CER response includes claim, evidence, and physics reasoning.

Interpretation: This example shows how a veteran physics teacher would slow down, identify the evidence, and connect the evidence to the concept before choosing an answer.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: The cart went faster because it looked faster.

Correct: The cart in Trial B went faster because the measured speed was 1.8 m/s, greater than Trial A's 1.1 m/s.

Teachable explanation: The correct version names the evidence and uses the mapped physics concept. The incorrect version is vague or ignores the evidence.
