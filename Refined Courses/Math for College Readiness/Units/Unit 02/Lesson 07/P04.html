P04 Worked Example

Step-by-step explanation: Follow each example in order. Notice how the work identifies the context, selects a strategy, calculates carefully, and interprets the result.

Study all three worked examples carefully. Each example shows the thinking, not just the final answer.

Worked Example 1

Problem: A club needs at least 40 chairs. Each table seats 6 people. Decide whether 7 tables are enough.

  1. Step 1: Identify the constraint: seats must be at least 40.
  2. Step 2: Write the model: 6t = 40.
  3. Step 3: Substitute t = 7: 6(7) = 42.
  4. Step 4: Since 42 = 40, 7 tables are enough.
  5. Step 5: The solution is viable because tables can be counted as whole numbers.

Final check: The solution is interpreted in the real-world situation.

Worked Example 2

Problem: A delivery van can carry no more than 900 pounds. Each box weighs 35 pounds. Is 30 boxes viable?

  1. Step 1: Identify the constraint: total weight must be = 900.
  2. Step 2: Write 35b = 900.
  3. Step 3: Substitute b = 30: 35(30) = 1050.
  4. Step 4: 1050 is greater than 900.
  5. Step 5: 30 boxes is not viable.

Final check: The solution is interpreted in the real-world situation.

Worked Example 3

Problem: A budget allows up to $120 for tickets and fees. Tickets cost $18 each plus a $12 fee. Find the greatest viable number of tickets.

  1. Step 1: Write the constraint: 18t + 12 = 120.
  2. Step 2: Subtract 12: 18t = 108.
  3. Step 3: Divide by 18: t = 6.
  4. Step 4: The greatest viable whole number is 6.
  5. Step 5: The answer makes sense because ticket counts must be whole numbers.

Final check: The solution is interpreted in the real-world situation.

Common Mistake

Incorrect:

t = 6, so 6.5 tickets is okay

Why it is wrong: The inequality allows numbers less than or equal to 6, but ticket counts must be whole numbers in context.

Correct:

t = 6, so the greatest viable whole number is 6 tickets

Correction: The final answer must be mathematically correct and meaningful in the situation.
