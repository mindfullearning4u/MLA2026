The Four-Step Reasoning Routine

Use this routine when a problem has a real-world situation. The routine slows your thinking down so you do not guess or use an operation just because numbers appear in the problem.

  1. Read: Identify the question being asked. Do not solve until you know what the final answer should mean.
  2. Represent: List the quantities, units, and unknown. This helps you organize the important information.
  3. Solve: Choose a strategy that matches the situation. Show enough work so another person can follow your reasoning.
  4. Interpret: Write the answer with units and explain whether it is reasonable.

Example: Use the Routine

Problem: A phone plan costs $25 each month for 4 months. What is the total cost?

Read: The question asks for total cost. The answer should be a dollar amount.

Represent: The quantities are $25 per month and 4 months. The unknown is the total number of dollars.

Solve: Since $25 is charged for each month and there are 4 months, multiply.

25 x 4 = 100

Interpret: The total cost is $100.

Reasonableness Check: Four months at about $25 each should be about $100, so the answer is reasonable.

How to Explain Reasonableness

A reasonableness check is not just saying, "It looks right." A good reasonableness check uses an estimate, units, or context.

  • Estimate: Round numbers to easier values and compare.
  • Units: Check that the answer has the correct label.
  • Context: Ask whether the answer could happen in the real situation.

Common Mistake to Avoid

Do not ignore units. Units tell you what a number means and whether your final answer is complete.

Problem: A car travels 180 miles in 3 hours. What is the speed?

Incorrect:

180 divided by 3 = 60

Why this is incomplete: The number 60 is correct, but the answer does not say what 60 means. A speed needs a unit that compares distance to time.

Correct:

180 miles divided by 3 hours = 60 miles per hour

Correction: Include both the calculation and the meaning. The car travels 60 miles for each hour.
