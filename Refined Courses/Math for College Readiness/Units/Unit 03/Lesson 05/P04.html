P04 Guided Practice / Worked Example

Step-by-step explanation: Follow each example in order. Notice how the work identifies the context, selects a strategy, calculates carefully, and interprets the result.

Worked Example 1: Identify correlation

Problem: A scatterplot shows that students who sleep more hours tend to have higher test scores.

Visual: positive association

  1. Step 1: Identify the variables: sleep hours and test score.
  2. Step 2: The pattern shows that higher sleep hours are associated with higher scores.
  3. Step 3: This is correlation because the variables move together.

Answer: The data show a positive correlation between sleep hours and test scores.

Worked Example 2: Avoid false causation

Problem: A city finds that sunscreen sales and pool attendance both increase in summer.

  1. Step 1: Both variables increase together.
  2. Step 2: A third factor, hot sunny weather, may explain both increases.
  3. Step 3: Do not claim sunscreen sales cause pool attendance.

Answer: The relationship is correlation, not proof of causation.

Worked Example 3: Evaluate a claim

Problem: An ad says a study proves a snack improves memory because students who ate it scored higher.

  1. Step 1: Ask whether students were randomly assigned.
  2. Step 2: Ask whether other factors, such as study time, were controlled.
  3. Step 3: If those details are missing, the claim may be stronger than the evidence.

Answer: The data may show an association, but the claim needs stronger evidence to prove causation.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: Repeating a claim exactly as written without judging the evidence.

Correct: Identify the evidence, check for limits, and write a conclusion that is supported by the data.
