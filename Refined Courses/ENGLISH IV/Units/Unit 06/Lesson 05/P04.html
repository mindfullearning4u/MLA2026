Embedded Presentation Review

Embedded Digital Presentation Review: A public health internship team created a digital presentation about expanding telehealth appointment access for rural patients. The presentation includes a title slide, a chart comparing appointment wait times before and after a pilot program, three source citations, and a final recommendation. However, several slides contain dense paragraphs, one chart lacks axis labels, the color contrast makes two slides difficult to read, and the speaker uses technical terms such as asynchronous intake and care continuity without defining them. The team wants to evaluate whether the presentation is effective for a mixed audience of patients, clinic staff, and county leaders.

Example 1: Evaluate Clarity

Step-by-step explanation: Step 1: identify the specific lesson skill being modeled. Step 2: examine the words, evidence, sentence, or passage detail that matters most. Step 3: explain why the final response or revision demonstrates the skill accurately.

Question: Which feature most clearly weakens presentation clarity?

Modeled thinking: Dense paragraphs and undefined technical terms make it harder for a mixed audience to process the message quickly.

Answer: The presentation should reduce dense text and define technical terms in plain language.

Example 2: Evaluate Credibility

Step-by-step explanation: Step 1: identify what changed from the first example. Step 2: connect the example to the lesson focus. Step 3: explain how the response uses clearer reasoning, stronger evidence, or more precise language.

Question: Which feature supports credibility?

Modeled thinking: Credibility depends on trustworthy support. The source citations and wait-time comparison chart help, but the chart still needs labels.

Answer: The citations and chart support credibility when the chart is labeled clearly enough for the audience to interpret.

Example 3: Evaluate Accessibility

Step-by-step explanation: Step 1: combine the lesson skill with evidence or revision choices. Step 2: check that the explanation stays focused on the assigned standard. Step 3: confirm that the final response is clear, accurate, and written in standard English.

Question: Which revision best improves accessibility?

Modeled thinking: Accessibility means more viewers can read and understand the message. Color contrast and defined terms matter.

Answer: Increase color contrast and define technical terms such as asynchronous intake and care continuity.

Common Mistake

Incorrect example: The chart is enough because data always makes a presentation effective.

Correct example: The chart can strengthen the presentation, but only if labels and explanation help the audience understand what the data proves.

Teachable explanation: Data does not automatically create effectiveness. The audience must be able to interpret the data and connect it to the claim.
