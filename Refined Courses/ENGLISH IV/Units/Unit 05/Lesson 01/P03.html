ENGLISH IV | Unit 05 | Lesson 01

P03 Notebook Task Part 2

Deeper Learning

In advanced writing, conventions do more than correct mistakes. They show logic. A semicolon can show that two complete ideas are closely related. A colon can introduce an explanation, list, or result. A dependent clause can show cause, condition, or contrast.

When revising, ask: What relationship am I trying to show? Cause? Contrast? Result? Condition? Once you know the relationship, choose the structure that makes that relationship clear.

Revision Set

Original sentence: The proposal included strong evidence, it did not explain implementation clearly.

Meaning to preserve: The proposal has evidence, but the explanation of implementation is weak.

Step-by-Step Application

Step 1: Identify the error. The original sentence is a comma splice because two complete sentences are joined with only a comma.

Step 2: Identify the relationship. The ideas contrast: strong evidence versus weak implementation explanation.

Step 3: Choose a correct structure. Use a comma and "but" to show contrast.

Step 4: Revised sentence: The proposal included strong evidence, but it did not explain implementation clearly.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: The proposal included strong evidence, it did not explain implementation clearly.

Correct: The proposal included strong evidence, but it did not explain implementation clearly.

Why this matters: The incorrect version creates a comma splice. The correct version uses a coordinating conjunction to show contrast between the two ideas.

Notebook Directions

Write one sentence with a convention error from your own draft or from the lesson. Then revise it and explain what relationship the corrected punctuation shows.

Deeper Learning Support

Mapped standard focus: MLA.ENG4.L.01

Use this second-pass routine to check whether your reasoning is specific, defensible, and mature enough for Grade 12 mastery.

  1. Read the draft for purpose first. Decide what the writing is supposed to prove, explain, evaluate, or communicate.
  2. Revise one layer at a time: reasoning, organization, evidence, transitions, sentence control, style, and conventions.
  3. Keep the original purpose while strengthening clarity and authority. Revision should make the thinking easier to follow, not replace it with a different message.
  4. Check whether the final version sounds polished, precise, audience-aware, and ready for academic or workplace communication.
