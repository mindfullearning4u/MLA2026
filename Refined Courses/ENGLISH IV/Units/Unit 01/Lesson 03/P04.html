Passage: Transit Access and Student Participation

A community college review found that students with reliable evening transportation were more likely to attend tutoring sessions, library workshops, and study groups after work. The report did not claim that transportation alone caused higher grades. Instead, it concluded that dependable transit expanded students' access to academic support services. The strongest evidence came from attendance records showing that late-afternoon workshop participation increased after the college added a shuttle route to nearby bus stops. The review recommended continuing the shuttle program while also studying other barriers, including childcare, course scheduling, and internet access.

Example 1: Relevant Evidence

Step-by-step explanation: Step 1: identify the specific lesson skill being modeled. Step 2: examine the words, evidence, sentence, or passage detail that matters most. Step 3: explain why the final response or revision demonstrates the skill accurately.

Claim: Reliable transportation can expand student access to academic support.

Modeled thinking: I need evidence about access to support, not a general detail about transportation.

Evidence paraphrase: Workshop attendance rose after the college added a shuttle route to bus stops.

Reasoning: This evidence is relevant because it links transportation access to participation in support services.

Example 2: Avoiding Overstatement

Step-by-step explanation: Step 1: identify what changed from the first example. Step 2: connect the example to the lesson focus. Step 3: explain how the response uses clearer reasoning, stronger evidence, or more precise language.

Modeled thinking: The passage says the shuttle expanded access. It does not say the shuttle caused higher grades.

Accurate paraphrase: The report connected dependable transit with greater access to tutoring, workshops, and study groups.

Reasoning: This preserves the conclusion without adding a causal claim about grades.

Example 3: Oral Explanation

Step-by-step explanation: Step 1: combine the lesson skill with evidence or revision choices. Step 2: check that the explanation stays focused on the assigned standard. Step 3: confirm that the final response is clear, accurate, and written in standard English.

Modeled thinking: A brief oral explanation should have a claim, evidence, and explanation.

Oral response: Reliable transportation appears to improve access to academic support because the report found workshop attendance increased after a shuttle route was added. That evidence is relevant because it shows students used support services more when transportation was available.

Reasoning: The response is organized, uses evidence, and explains relevance.

Common Mistake

Incorrect example: The review proves transportation is the only barrier students face.

Correct example: The review recommends studying other barriers, including childcare, scheduling, and internet access.

Teachable explanation: The incorrect example ignores the final sentence. The correct example preserves the source's broader recommendation and avoids exaggeration.
