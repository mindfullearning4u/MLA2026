Passage: Precision in Civic Conversation

When people discuss a public issue, precision matters. A speaker who describes a policy as beneficial is making a broad positive judgment, but a speaker who calls it equitable is making a more specific claim about fairness. Likewise, describing evidence as relevant means it connects to the issue, while describing it as credible means it can be trusted. Academic vocabulary allows writers and speakers to make exact distinctions. Without precise words, an argument may sound confident while leaving the audience unsure about what is being claimed, how evidence supports it, or why the conclusion should be accepted.

Example 1: Beneficial vs. Equitable

Modeled thinking: Beneficial asks whether something helps. Equitable asks whether something is fair. These words can overlap, but they are not interchangeable.

Precise sentence: A policy may be beneficial because it improves outcomes, but it may not be equitable if the improvements are not shared fairly.

Reasoning: The sentence uses each word for its exact function.

Example 2: Relevant vs. Credible

Modeled thinking: Relevant evidence connects to the issue. Credible evidence can be trusted. Evidence should ideally be both, but each word evaluates a different quality.

Precise sentence: The statistic is relevant because it addresses the policy's cost, but it is not credible unless the source is trustworthy.

Reasoning: The sentence evaluates connection and trustworthiness separately.

Example 3: Paraphrase with Vocabulary Precision

Original idea: Academic vocabulary allows writers and speakers to make exact distinctions.

Modeled thinking: The key idea is not that academic words make writing sound fancy. The key idea is that they allow exact distinctions.

Accurate paraphrase: Academic language helps writers and speakers express precise differences between related ideas.

Reasoning: This keeps the function of academic vocabulary and uses original wording.

Common Mistake

Incorrect example: The source is relevant because it is trustworthy.

Correct example: The source is credible because it is trustworthy.

Teachable explanation: The incorrect sentence uses relevant to describe trustworthiness. Relevant means connected to the issue. Credible means trustworthy. At Grade 12, analysis must make that distinction because imprecise vocabulary weakens reasoning.
