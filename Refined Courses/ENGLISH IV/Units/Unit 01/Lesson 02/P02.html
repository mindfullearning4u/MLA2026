P02 Notebook Task Part 1

Notebook Title

Precise Academic Vocabulary for Analysis

Vocabulary

  • Precise: exact and specific.
  • Beneficial: producing a positive effect or advantage.
  • Equitable: fair and just across people or groups.
  • Relevant: directly connected to the issue or claim.
  • Credible: trustworthy and believable.
  • Distinction: a meaningful difference between two ideas.
  • Claim: a position or point that a speaker or writer presents as true.

Embedded Passage

Passage: Precision in Civic Conversation

When people discuss a public issue, precision matters. A speaker who describes a policy as beneficial is making a broad positive judgment, but a speaker who calls it equitable is making a more specific claim about fairness. Likewise, describing evidence as relevant means it connects to the issue, while describing it as credible means it can be trusted. Academic vocabulary allows writers and speakers to make exact distinctions. Without precise words, an argument may sound confident while leaving the audience unsure about what is being claimed, how evidence supports it, or why the conclusion should be accepted.

Detailed Teaching: How Vocabulary Builds Analysis

Step 1: Identify the topic. The passage discusses how people use language when discussing public issues.

Step 2: Identify word pairs that are related but not identical. Beneficial and equitable both describe positive qualities, but they do not mean the same thing. Relevant and credible both describe evidence, but they evaluate different qualities.

Step 3: Define the distinction. Beneficial asks whether something helps. Equitable asks whether it is fair. Relevant asks whether evidence connects to the issue. Credible asks whether evidence can be trusted.

Step 4: Use the exact word your meaning requires. If you mean "fair," use equitable. If you mean "connected to the claim," use relevant. This precision makes your analysis stronger.

Step 5: Check conventions. Precise vocabulary still needs standard grammar and punctuation. A sophisticated word used in a confusing sentence does not create strong academic writing.
