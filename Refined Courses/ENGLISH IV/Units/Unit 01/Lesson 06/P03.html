P03 Notebook Task - Part 2

Embedded Passage

Passage: Community Feedback and Decision Making

Before changing the schedule for a public study center, the planning team gathered feedback from students, tutors, and local employers. The comments did not produce complete consensus, but they revealed a shared concern: evening access mattered for students who worked during the day. The team recommended extending hours twice a week and reviewing attendance data after three months. This recommendation was credible because it responded to stakeholder concerns, used a limited trial period, and included a plan for evaluating results.

Continued Notebook Application

Academic vocabulary should make your meaning more precise, not simply more formal. A word is effective when it matches the idea and helps the audience follow your reasoning.

Step 1: Choose a vocabulary word that fits the passage.

Step 2: Use it in a written sentence.

Step 3: Read the sentence aloud and check whether it sounds clear.

Step 4: Adjust wording for an oral audience while keeping the academic meaning accurate.

Deeper Learning

Speaking and writing require different delivery choices, but both require precise meaning. In writing, you can use longer sentences if they stay clear. In speaking, shorter sentences usually help the listener. The academic vocabulary should remain accurate in both forms.

Common Mistake

Incorrect example: The team had full consensus because everyone shared the same concern.

Correct example: The team did not have full consensus, but the feedback revealed a shared concern about evening access.

Teachable explanation: The incorrect sentence misuses consensus because the passage says complete consensus did not occur. The correct sentence preserves the limitation and uses the academic term accurately.

Use this second-pass routine to check whether your reasoning is specific, defensible, and mature enough for Grade 12 mastery.

  1. Read the word or phrase in context before deciding meaning. Advanced texts often use familiar words in specialized or layered ways.
  2. Use etymology, derivations, foreign phrases, and reference materials when context alone is not enough.
  3. Separate denotation from connotation and tone. A precise word may shape the reader's attitude as much as it communicates a definition.
  4. Choose the meaning or wording that best fits purpose, audience, genre, and rhetorical effect.
