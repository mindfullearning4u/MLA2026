P02 Notebook Task Part 1

Notebook Title

Accurate Paraphrase Begins with Close Reading

Vocabulary

  • Paraphrase: a restatement of a source's meaning in your own words.
  • Preserve: to keep something accurate and unchanged in meaning.
  • Qualification: a limit or condition that narrows a claim, often signaled by words such as although, however, usually, or specifically.
  • Emphasis: the part of an idea the writer makes most important.
  • Distort: to change meaning so it no longer matches the original.

Embedded Passage

Passage: On Careful Reading

Careful reading is not the same as slow reading. A careful reader notices how each sentence changes or refines the idea before it. When a writer qualifies a claim with words such as however, although, or specifically, the reader must preserve that qualification in a paraphrase. A strong paraphrase does more than replace words with synonyms; it restates the full meaning, including relationships among ideas, limits on claims, and the author's emphasis. In college and workplace settings, inaccurate paraphrase can distort a source, weaken an argument, or create confusion about what a document actually says.

Detailed Teaching: How to Read Before You Paraphrase

Step 1: Find the main idea. Ask, "What is the whole passage mainly explaining?" This passage explains that accurate paraphrase depends on careful reading, not just word replacement.

Step 2: Notice how each sentence contributes. Sentence 1 separates careful reading from slow reading. Sentence 2 explains what careful readers notice. Sentence 3 introduces qualifications. Sentence 4 defines a strong paraphrase. Sentence 5 explains why accuracy matters in college and workplace contexts.

Step 3: Identify relationships among ideas. The passage uses contrast: careful reading is not merely slow reading. It also uses cause and effect: inaccurate paraphrase can distort a source and weaken an argument.

Step 4: Preserve qualifications and emphasis. The passage emphasizes that paraphrase must keep the full meaning, including limits, relationships, and emphasis. If you leave out those parts, your paraphrase may be incomplete or misleading.

Step 5: Write in your own sentence structure. A paraphrase should not copy the original sentence pattern with a few substituted words. It should restate the meaning using your own organization while staying accurate.

Notebook Directions

  1. Write the notebook title.
  2. Copy the vocabulary words and write a student-friendly meaning for each one.
  3. Make a two-column chart. Label the left column "Original Meaning Unit" and the right column "My Accurate Restatement."
  4. Break the passage into at least four meaning units and restate each unit in your own words.

Model Example

Original meaning unit: "Careful reading is not the same as slow reading."

Accurate restatement: Reading carefully means paying attention to meaning, not simply reading at a slower speed.

Why this works: The restatement keeps the contrast between careful reading and slow reading, but it uses a different sentence structure and wording.
