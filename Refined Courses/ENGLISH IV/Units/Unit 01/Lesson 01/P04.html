Embedded Passage

Passage: On Careful Reading

Careful reading is not the same as slow reading. A careful reader notices how each sentence changes or refines the idea before it. When a writer qualifies a claim with words such as however, although, or specifically, the reader must preserve that qualification in a paraphrase. A strong paraphrase does more than replace words with synonyms; it restates the full meaning, including relationships among ideas, limits on claims, and the author's emphasis. In college and workplace settings, inaccurate paraphrase can distort a source, weaken an argument, or create confusion about what a document actually says.

Example 1: Paraphrasing a Contrast

Original idea: Careful reading is not the same as slow reading.

Modeled thinking: The phrase "not the same as" creates a contrast. I must keep that contrast. I should not turn it into "careful reading means slow reading."

Accurate paraphrase: Reading carefully means attending to meaning, not simply reducing reading speed.

Reasoning: This keeps the contrast and uses a different structure.

Example 2: Paraphrasing a Qualification

Original idea: When a writer qualifies a claim, the reader must preserve that qualification in a paraphrase.

Modeled thinking: The important word is "qualifies." A qualified claim has a limit or condition. A paraphrase must keep the limit.

Accurate paraphrase: If an author narrows or limits a point, the paraphrase must keep that limit instead of turning the point into a broader claim.

Reasoning: This shows what the qualification does and explains why it must remain.

Example 3: Paraphrasing Consequences

Original idea: Inaccurate paraphrase can distort a source, weaken an argument, or create confusion.

Modeled thinking: The passage lists three consequences. A strong paraphrase should keep all three unless the task asks for a summary.

Accurate paraphrase: When a paraphrase changes a source's meaning, it can misrepresent the source, damage the strength of an argument, and make the document harder to understand.

Reasoning: This keeps the cause-and-effect relationship and restates each consequence with precise academic language.

Common Mistake

Incorrect example: A paraphrase should use different synonyms so the source looks original.

Correct example: A paraphrase should restate the source's full meaning in original wording and structure while preserving relationships, limits, and emphasis.

Teachable explanation: The incorrect example focuses on appearance and synonym replacement. That does not prove understanding. The correct example focuses on meaning, structure, and accuracy, which is what MLA.ENG4.R.10 requires.
