ENGLISH IV | Unit 03 | Lesson 06

P02 Notebook Task - Part 1

Notebook Title

Rhetorical Choices Across Texts

In your notebook, you will track how writers make purposeful choices to influence different audiences.

Vocabulary

  • Rhetorical choice: A deliberate writing decision used to influence audience response.
  • Diction: The writer's word choice.
  • Tone: The writer's attitude toward the subject or audience.
  • Framing: The way a writer presents an issue so readers view it from a particular angle.
  • Appeal: A persuasive strategy that uses logic, emotion, credibility, or values.

Step-by-Step Teaching

Step 1: Identify the shared issue. Before comparing rhetoric, make sure the texts are addressing the same topic or problem.

Step 2: Identify each author's purpose. Ask what each author wants the audience to think, feel, or do.

Step 3: Name the rhetorical choices. Look for loaded words, statistics, direct address, repetition, contrast, organization, examples, and appeals.

Step 4: Explain the effect. A rhetorical choice matters only if you explain how it affects the reader. For example, urgent diction may create pressure, while measured diction may build trust.

Step 5: Evaluate effectiveness. Decide whether the choice fits the purpose and audience. Effective rhetoric is not simply emotional or impressive. It must help the text achieve its purpose responsibly.

Embedded Texts

Text A: Advisory Memo on Food Waste

Last month, cafeteria staff measured the amount of edible food discarded after lunch. The average was 118 pounds per day. A composting pilot would reduce landfill waste, and a share table could redirect unopened items to students who need them. Because the data show a repeated problem, the school should test a two-month food recovery plan before expanding it.

Text B: Student Editorial on Food Waste

Every afternoon, perfectly good food lands in the trash while students leave school hungry. We should be embarrassed by a system that wastes resources and ignores need. A share table is not a luxury. It is a practical act of responsibility. If our school claims to value community, it should stop treating uneaten food like garbage.

Model Example

Both texts support reducing food waste, but they use different rhetorical choices. Text A uses data and cautious language such as "pilot" and "test" to build credibility with decision-makers. Text B uses moral diction such as "embarrassed" and "responsibility" to create urgency. Text A may be more effective for administrators who want evidence before approving a policy, while Text B may be more effective for students who need to feel personally responsible for the issue.

Notebook Directions

In your notebook, create a two-column chart labeled Text A and Text B. For each text, record the author's purpose, one rhetorical choice, the effect of that choice, and the audience for whom the choice is most effective.

Step-by-Step Teaching Support

Lesson focus: Rhetorical choices across multiple texts

Mapped standard focus: MLA.ENG4.R.12

Use this sequence before you complete the notebook task. It makes the Grade 12 thinking process explicit so you can evaluate, explain, and apply the skill independently.

  1. Identify the claim, audience, purpose, and context before evaluating effectiveness. A choice is effective only in relation to what the writer is trying to accomplish.
  2. Separate evidence from reasoning. Evidence shows what the author uses; reasoning explains how the author connects evidence to the claim.
  3. Evaluate validity by asking whether the reasoning is logical, the evidence is relevant, and the conclusion follows from the support.
  4. Compare effects across texts when needed. Grade 12 analysis should explain how different authors use similar information or rhetorical tools for different ends.
