P02 Notebook Task Part 1

Notebook Title

Evaluating Informational Text Structure and Features

Vocabulary

  • Text structure: The way information is organized, such as cause-effect, problem-solution, comparison, sequence, or description.
  • Text feature: A tool that supports understanding, such as a heading, chart, caption, statistic, example, or transition.
  • Effectiveness: How well a choice achieves its purpose for the reader.
  • Coherence: The clear connection among ideas in a text.
  • Revision recommendation: A specific suggestion for making a text stronger.

Detailed Teaching: From Identification to Evaluation

Identifying structure is the first step, but it is not the final goal. Evaluation asks whether the structure helps the reader understand the author's message. You should ask what the structure does and how well it works.

Step 1: Identify the main structure. Ask: Is the author explaining a problem, comparing options, showing causes, or describing a process?

Step 2: Identify the features. Ask: Do headings, examples, data, or transitions guide the reader?

Step 3: Connect structure to purpose. Ask: Why would the author choose this organization?

Step 4: Judge effectiveness. Ask: Does the structure make the point clearer, or does it create confusion?

Step 5: Recommend improvement. A strong recommendation is specific, such as adding a comparison chart, moving a definition earlier, or grouping examples by cause.

Embedded Passage

Passage: "Cooling the City"

Many cities are becoming hotter because pavement and buildings absorb heat during the day and release it slowly at night. One response is to plant more trees along streets and around public buildings. Trees provide shade, release moisture into the air, and reduce the amount of heat held by concrete surfaces. Another response is to install reflective roofing materials that send more sunlight away from buildings. Both strategies can help, but they work differently. Trees improve public spaces and air quality, while reflective roofs can be added quickly to large buildings. City planners often need both approaches because heat affects neighborhoods in different ways.

Notebook Directions

  1. Copy the notebook title.
  2. Copy and define the five vocabulary terms.
  3. Identify the passage's main structure.
  4. List two text features or organizational choices that help the reader.
  5. Write one sentence evaluating whether the structure is effective.

Model Example

The passage uses a problem-solution structure with comparison. It begins by explaining the urban heat problem, then presents trees and reflective roofing as possible solutions. This structure is effective because it helps the reader understand why both strategies matter and how they differ.

Step-by-Step Teaching Support

Use this sequence before you complete the notebook task. It makes the Grade 12 thinking process explicit so you can evaluate, explain, and apply the skill independently.

  1. Read the draft for purpose first. Decide what the writing is supposed to prove, explain, evaluate, or communicate.
  2. Revise one layer at a time: reasoning, organization, evidence, transitions, sentence control, style, and conventions.
  3. Keep the original purpose while strengthening clarity and authority. Revision should make the thinking easier to follow, not replace it with a different message.
  4. Check whether the final version sounds polished, precise, audience-aware, and ready for academic or workplace communication.
