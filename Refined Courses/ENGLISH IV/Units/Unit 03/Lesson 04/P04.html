ENGLISH IV | Unit 03 | Lesson 04

P04 Worked Example

Example 1: Identify Claim and Evidence

Claim: Schools should extend lunch periods by ten minutes.

Evidence: Students rush through meals, some throw away food, and 61 percent of survey respondents wanted a longer lunch.

Step 1: The claim asks for a policy change.

Step 2: The evidence describes student experience and preference.

Modeled thinking: The evidence is relevant, but it may not fully prove all benefits of the policy.

Example 2: Evaluate Validity

Question: Is the reasoning valid?

Step 1: The argument links rushed meals to the need for longer lunch.

Step 2: That connection is logical.

Step 3: The argument would be stronger with evidence about nutrition or focus.

Modeled answer: The reasoning is partly valid because rushed meals support the need for more time, but the argument needs stronger evidence to prove broader academic or health benefits.

Example 3: Consider a Related Argument

Related argument: Schools should keep lunch periods the same length to protect instructional time.

Step 1: This related argument addresses the same policy issue.

Step 2: It uses a different priority: instructional time.

Step 3: A strong evaluation should compare the reasoning behind both positions.

Modeled answer: The related argument creates a valid concern because adding lunch time could affect the schedule, so the original argument should address how the school would protect instructional time.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: If an argument has evidence, it is automatically valid.

Correct: Evidence supports validity only when it is relevant, sufficient, and logically connected to the claim through clear reasoning.

Teachable explanation: Validity depends on reasoning, not just the presence of evidence.
