P03 Notebook Task - Part 2

Deeper Learning

Strong argument reasoning answers the question "So what?" after evidence appears. If a paragraph only gives evidence, the reader may understand the topic but not the argument. Your reasoning must explain the evidence's function.

Reasoning can do several jobs. It can show cause and effect, explain a condition, answer a likely objection, define the importance of a detail, or connect evidence to a larger consequence.

Embedded Argument Situation

Issue: Should a city require public event organizers to provide reusable water stations?

Source A: A sanitation report found that single-use plastic bottles were one of the most common waste items after large outdoor events.

Source B: An event organizer explained that reusable water stations require rental fees, setup space, and volunteers to monitor lines.

Source C: A city pilot program reported lower plastic waste when water stations were placed near entrances and food areas.

Step-by-Step Application

Claim: Cities should require reusable water stations at large public events when event permits include practical placement and staffing requirements.

Evidence: Source A shows that plastic bottles are a major waste problem, and Source C shows that station placement affected the success of a pilot program.

Reasoning: This evidence supports the claim because the policy targets a documented waste source while using placement rules to make the solution workable rather than symbolic.

Validity check: The claim is not too broad. It includes conditions that respond to Source B's concern about cost, space, and staffing.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: Water stations should be required because plastic bottles are bad.

Correct: Water stations should be required at large public events when placement and staffing rules make the stations practical, because the evidence shows both a waste problem and a workable pilot solution.

Teachable Explanation: The incorrect claim is vague and relies on a general judgment. The correct claim uses academic precision, addresses implementation, and connects reasoning to specific evidence.

Notebook Directions

Write one claim, two evidence sentences, and two reasoning sentences for the water station issue. Label each sentence as Claim, Evidence, or Reasoning.

Deeper Learning Support

Lesson focus: In-depth argument claims, valid reasoning, and evidence

Mapped standard focus: MLA.ENG4.W.02

Use this second-pass routine to check whether your reasoning is specific, defensible, and mature enough for Grade 12 mastery.

  1. Identify the claim, audience, purpose, and context before evaluating effectiveness. A choice is effective only in relation to what the writer is trying to accomplish.
  2. Separate evidence from reasoning. Evidence shows what the author uses; reasoning explains how the author connects evidence to the claim.
  3. Evaluate validity by asking whether the reasoning is logical, the evidence is relevant, and the conclusion follows from the support.
  4. Compare effects across texts when needed. Grade 12 analysis should explain how different authors use similar information or rhetorical tools for different ends.
