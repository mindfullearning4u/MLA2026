P03 Notebook Task - Part 2

Deeper Learning

After you synthesize sources, the next step is deciding what the synthesis allows you to argue or analyze. A synthesis statement explains the relationship among sources. A claim uses that relationship to take a position or make an interpretation.

For example, if sources show that flexible schedules help workers focus but can weaken communication, a strong claim should not ignore either side. A stronger claim explains the condition that makes the policy effective.

Embedded Source Set

Research question: Should cities expand shaded public seating in high-traffic areas?

Source A: A city health brief reports that shaded seating can reduce heat stress for older adults, transit riders, and outdoor workers.

Source B: A budget memo notes that seating projects require maintenance plans because damaged seating can become unsafe or unused.

Source C: A downtown business survey found that shoppers stayed longer in areas with shaded seating and clear walkway access.

Step-by-Step Application

Step 1: Find what the sources share. Sources A and C both show benefits of shaded seating. Source A focuses on health and access; Source C focuses on community and business activity.

Step 2: Find the complication. Source B does not reject shaded seating, but it adds a condition: the city must plan maintenance.

Step 3: Write a synthesis statement. The sources suggest that shaded seating can improve public comfort and community use, but the success of the project depends on maintenance and placement.

Step 4: Turn the synthesis into a claim. Cities should expand shaded public seating in high-traffic areas when the plan includes maintenance funding and protects walkway access.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: Source A says shaded seating helps people. Source B says it costs money. Source C says shoppers like it.

Correct: Together, the sources suggest that shaded public seating can improve health, comfort, and local activity, but only if cities plan for maintenance and safe placement.

Teachable Explanation: The incorrect version lists sources separately. The correct version explains the relationship among the sources and shows how the evidence could support a thoughtful argument.

Notebook Directions

Write one synthesis statement and one claim using the shaded public seating source set. Underline the words in your claim that show the condition or limit of your argument.

Deeper Learning Support

Lesson focus: Synthesizing research for argument and analysis

Mapped standard focus: MLA.ENG4.RS.01

Use this second-pass routine to check whether your reasoning is specific, defensible, and mature enough for Grade 12 mastery.

  1. Begin by defining the topical issue, research question, or analytical claim. Grade 12 research must stay focused enough to support in-depth reasoning.
  2. Separate facts, perspectives, claims, and source judgments. Do not treat every source as equally useful or equally credible.
  3. Synthesize by explaining relationships among sources: agreement, disagreement, emphasis, limitation, or implication.
  4. Use evidence to build a defensible conclusion, then explain why the conclusion matters for the topic, argument, or analysis.
