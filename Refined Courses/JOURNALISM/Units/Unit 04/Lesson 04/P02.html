P02 Notebook Task Part 1

Notebook Title

Comparing Opposing Arguments in Editorial Media

Set up your notebook to compare claims, evidence, reasoning, and validity.

Vocabulary

  • Claim: the position an argument asks readers to accept.
  • Opposing argument: an argument that takes a different position on the same topic.
  • Evidence: facts, examples, data, or expert statements used to support a claim.
  • Validity: whether a claim is logical and well supported.
  • Effectiveness: how well an argument persuades its intended audience.

Embedded Source

Embedded Media Text: Two Editorial Drafts on the Same Topic

Topic: Should the school create a phone-free lunch area?

Argument A: The school should create a phone-free lunch area because many students need a place to talk without screens taking over the table. A guidance counselor says some students report feeling ignored when everyone around them is scrolling. A pilot table in the media center attracted twenty students during one week, and several said it helped them meet classmates. Supporters say the area would be optional, so students who want to use phones at lunch could still sit elsewhere.

Argument B: The school should not create a phone-free lunch area because lunch is one of the few flexible times students have during the day. Many students use phones to check family messages, manage club reminders, or finish quick tasks. A student council survey found that sixty-eight percent of respondents opposed lunch phone restrictions. Opponents argue that a separate area may divide students instead of teaching respectful conversation habits for all tables.

Step-by-Step Teaching

Step 1: Name the shared topic. Both arguments discuss whether the school should create a phone-free lunch area.

Step 2: Identify Argument A claim. Argument A says the school should create the area.

Step 3: Identify Argument B claim. Argument B says the school should not create the area.

Step 4: List evidence for each side. Argument A uses counselor insight and pilot-table participation. Argument B uses student needs and survey data.

Step 5: Evaluate relevance. Relevant evidence directly supports the claim. A survey about student opposition is relevant to whether the policy may be accepted.

Step 6: Compare strength. A strong comparison explains both sides and judges evidence, not personal preference.

Notebook Directions

Create a comparison chart with four rows: Claim, Evidence, Reasoning, and Possible Weakness. Fill in one column for Argument A and one column for Argument B.

Model Example

Argument A claim: The school should create a phone-free lunch area.

Evidence: A pilot table attracted twenty students during one week.

Reasoning: This evidence supports the claim because it shows some students may use and value the optional area.
