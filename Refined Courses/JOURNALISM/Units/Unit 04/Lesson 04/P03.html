P03 Notebook Task Part 2

Continued Notebook Application

Now move from listing evidence to evaluating evidence. Evaluation means judging how well evidence supports a claim.

Deeper Learning

Step 1: Check whether the evidence is specific. A number, named source, or direct example is usually stronger than a general statement.

Step 2: Check whether the evidence is relevant. Evidence about student phone use is relevant. Evidence about unrelated lunch menu choices would not be relevant.

Step 3: Check whether the reasoning explains the connection. A claim needs a bridge between evidence and conclusion.

Step 4: Notice limits. Argument A shows some interest in a pilot table, but twenty students may not represent the whole school. Argument B uses a larger survey, but it does not prove that an optional area would harm students.

Step 5: Write a balanced judgment. A strong evaluation can say one argument has stronger evidence while still recognizing a weakness.

Embedded Source Reminder

Embedded Media Text: Two Editorial Drafts on the Same Topic

Topic: Should the school create a phone-free lunch area?

Argument A: The school should create a phone-free lunch area because many students need a place to talk without screens taking over the table. A guidance counselor says some students report feeling ignored when everyone around them is scrolling. A pilot table in the media center attracted twenty students during one week, and several said it helped them meet classmates. Supporters say the area would be optional, so students who want to use phones at lunch could still sit elsewhere.

Argument B: The school should not create a phone-free lunch area because lunch is one of the few flexible times students have during the day. Many students use phones to check family messages, manage club reminders, or finish quick tasks. A student council survey found that sixty-eight percent of respondents opposed lunch phone restrictions. Opponents argue that a separate area may divide students instead of teaching respectful conversation habits for all tables.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: Argument B is better because I like having my phone at lunch.

Correct: Argument B may be more effective because it uses survey data from students, but it should still explain how an optional area would divide students.

Teachable explanation: The incorrect answer is personal preference. The correct answer evaluates evidence and reasoning while recognizing a limitation.

Notebook Directions

Write one sentence evaluating Argument A and one sentence evaluating Argument B. Each sentence must mention evidence and explain whether that evidence is strong, limited, or incomplete.
