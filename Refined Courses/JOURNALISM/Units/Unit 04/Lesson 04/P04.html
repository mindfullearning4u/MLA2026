P04 Worked Example

Example 1

Question: What are the opposing claims?

Modeled thinking: I look for what each argument wants the school to do. Argument A supports the phone-free lunch area. Argument B opposes it.

Answer: Argument A claims the school should create a phone-free lunch area, while Argument B claims the school should not create one.

Reasoning: This answer accurately compares the positions on the same topic.

Example 2

Question: Which evidence strengthens Argument A?

Modeled thinking: I need evidence that shows the area might work. The pilot table attracted twenty students and several said it helped them meet classmates.

Answer: The pilot-table evidence strengthens Argument A because it shows real student participation and a possible social benefit.

Reasoning: The evidence is relevant because it connects directly to the proposed phone-free area.

Example 3

Question: Which evidence strengthens Argument B?

Modeled thinking: I need evidence showing why students may oppose the idea. The survey says sixty-eight percent opposed lunch phone restrictions.

Answer: The survey evidence strengthens Argument B because it shows broad student opposition to restrictions during lunch.

Reasoning: The evidence is persuasive because it uses a percentage from the audience affected by the proposal.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: Argument A is automatically valid because a counselor agrees with it.

Correct: The counselor quote supports Argument A, but validity also depends on whether the evidence and reasoning show the proposal would help students.

Teachable explanation: A source can strengthen an argument, but it does not automatically prove the whole claim. Validity requires relevant evidence and logical reasoning.
