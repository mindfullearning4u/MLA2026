P03 Notebook Task Part 2

Deeper Journalism Application

Active listening means you do not simply move from question one to question two. You listen for a useful detail, unclear statement, or missing fact. Then you ask a follow-up that helps the audience understand the story more accurately.

Good listening also includes confirming what you heard. A journalist can say, I want to make sure I understood that correctly, and then restate the key fact before writing it into notes.

Step-by-Step: Active Listening During an Interview

Step 1: Ask a prepared question connected to the reporting purpose.

Step 2: Listen for a specific detail, number, name, date, reason, or quote.

Step 3: Ask a follow-up that clarifies or deepens the answer.

Step 4: Confirm important facts before ending the interview.

Step 5: Record notes accurately and mark anything that still needs verification.

Embedded Interview Scenario

Source response: The media club started the morning news show because students were missing announcements when they arrived late. We wanted a recorded version they could watch later.

Think: The source gives a reason and a solution. A strong follow-up should ask for a detail that helps readers understand the effect.

Notebook Directions

Answer in three labeled parts.

Part 1: Write one follow-up question based on the source response.

Part 2: Explain what detail your follow-up would help gather.

Part 3: Write one sentence confirming a fact you would verify before publishing.

Common Mistake

Incorrect example:

The reporter should ask, Do you think the news show is the best thing the school has ever done?

Why this is incorrect: This is a loaded question. It pushes the source toward praise instead of gathering clear information.

Correct example:

The reporter should ask, How many students usually watch the recorded announcements after school?

Why this is correct: This follow-up asks for specific information that can help the audience understand the effect of the news show.

Mastery Support: How to Tell Whether a Follow-Up Is Strong

A strong follow-up does three things. First, it connects to something the source just said. Second, it asks for information the audience needs. Third, it helps the reporter avoid guessing.

Weak follow-up: Is there anything else? This is too broad.

Stronger follow-up: You said students missed announcements when they arrived late. How can students access the recorded version? This is stronger because it listens to the source and asks for a useful detail.
