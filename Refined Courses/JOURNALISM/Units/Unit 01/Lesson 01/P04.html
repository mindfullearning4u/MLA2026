P04 Worked Example

Example 1: Identifying a News Brief

Step-by-step explanation: Follow each modeled step carefully and notice how the thinking moves from the task to evidence, reasoning, and a clear response.

Scenario: The cafeteria will begin breakfast service at 7:15 a.m. next Monday. The principal confirmed the change in an email to families.

Step 1: Identify the audience need. Students and families need quick, accurate information about a schedule change.

Step 2: Choose the format. A news brief fits because the information is timely, factual, and direct.

Step 3: Explain quality expectations. The brief should include the start date, time, source confirmation, and who is affected.

Modeled thinking: I would not use a feature story unless the purpose were to explore student experiences with breakfast access. Here, the audience mostly needs essential facts.

Example 2: Identifying a Feature Story

Step-by-step explanation: Follow each modeled step carefully and notice how the thinking moves from the task to evidence, reasoning, and a clear response.

Scenario: A student photographer documents the football team’s season from the sidelines and describes how sports photography changed her confidence.

Step 1: Identify the audience need. Readers need a human-interest story with detail and context.

Step 2: Choose the format. A feature story fits because it centers on a person, experience, and development over time.

Step 3: Explain quality expectations. The feature should include accurate facts, descriptive detail, relevant quotations, and a clear focus.

Modeled thinking: The story still must be factual. A feature can be engaging, but it cannot invent scenes or quotations.

Example 3: Identifying an Editorial

Step-by-step explanation: Follow each modeled step carefully and notice how the thinking moves from the task to evidence, reasoning, and a clear response.

Scenario: A student journalist argues that the school newspaper should publish a monthly fact-checking column and supports the idea with examples of online misinformation.

Step 1: Identify the audience need. Readers need a supported point of view about a school media issue.

Step 2: Choose the format. An editorial fits because the writer takes a position and supports it with evidence.

Step 3: Explain quality expectations. The editorial should make a clear claim, use credible evidence, acknowledge audience concerns, and maintain an appropriate tone.

Modeled thinking: An editorial is not just a complaint. It is opinion journalism that must still be accurate, fair, and evidence-based.

Common Mistake

Incorrect example:

A digital post is always easier, so journalists should use it for every story.

Why this is incorrect: The best format depends on purpose, evidence, audience, and publication needs. A digital post may be useful for quick updates, but it may not provide enough depth for a feature or enough structure for an editorial.

Correct example:

A journalist should choose the format that best serves the audience’s information need. A brief works for fast factual updates, a feature works for human-interest context, and an editorial works for a supported position.

Teachable Explanation: The answer evaluates format based on journalism purpose instead of convenience.
