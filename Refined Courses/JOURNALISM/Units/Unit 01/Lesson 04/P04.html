P04 Worked Example

Evaluate design choices in journalism.

Example 1: Headline Size

Situation: A routine club meeting is given a giant headline that makes it look like a major breaking story.

Step 1: The design choice is oversized headline emphasis.

Step 2: The audience may think the story is more urgent than it is.

Step 3: A better choice is a normal headline size that matches the story's importance.

Example 2: Chart Scale

Situation: A chart makes a small survey difference look huge by cutting off most of the scale.

Step 1: The design choice is a misleading scale.

Step 2: The audience may misunderstand the size of the difference.

Step 3: A better chart shows a clear scale and labels the numbers accurately.

Example 3: Caption Placement

Situation: A photo appears without a caption, so readers do not know who is pictured or when the event occurred.

Step 1: The design choice is missing caption information.

Step 2: The audience lacks context.

Step 3: A better design places a concise, accurate caption directly with the photo.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: A visual design is good if it looks interesting, even when it changes what readers think happened.

This is incorrect because journalism design must support accurate understanding.

Correct: A visual design is strong when it attracts attention and helps readers understand accurate information.

This is correct because journalism design must serve both audience engagement and truthfulness.

P04 Worked Example

Evaluate whether visual design supports journalism quality.

Example 1: Large Photo

Step 1: A large photo can draw attention to the story.

Step 2: The photo should show the actual event, person, or object being reported.

Step 3: The caption should identify what the audience sees.

Conclusion: A large photo supports journalism when it is relevant and accurately captioned.

Example 2: Chart

Step 1: A chart can help readers compare numbers quickly.

Step 2: The chart must label data clearly.

Step 3: The scale should not exaggerate the difference between numbers.

Conclusion: A chart supports journalism when it clarifies data honestly.

Example 3: Mobile Layout

Step 1: Many readers view journalism on phones.

Step 2: A mobile layout needs readable text, clear spacing, and visuals that fit the screen.

Step 3: The design should not hide captions or source information.

Conclusion: Mobile-friendly design supports audience access when it keeps important information visible.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: A visual is good journalism if it looks exciting.

This is incorrect because excitement alone does not prove accuracy, relevance, or format quality.

Correct: A visual is good journalism when it attracts attention and accurately supports the story purpose.

This is correct because strong design must serve the audience and the facts.
