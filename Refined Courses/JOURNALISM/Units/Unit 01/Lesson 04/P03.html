P03 Notebook Task Part 2

Deeper Journalism Application

Visual culture can push journalists toward fast, bold, image-heavy design. That can help audiences scan information, but it can also create problems if images are unclear, captions are missing, or layout choices exaggerate the story.

A journalism designer must ask: Does this visual choice help the audience understand verified information?

Step-by-Step: Analyze Design for Journalism

Step 1: Identify the story purpose.

Step 2: Identify the visual choice, such as image, caption, layout, color, or headline size.

Step 3: Explain how the choice helps or hurts audience understanding.

Step 4: Judge whether the choice is accurate and format-appropriate.

Embedded Scenario

Scenario: Photo Selection Problem

The newsroom has two photos for an article about the robotics team. Photo A shows the team working with the robot during practice. Photo B shows a dramatic close-up of broken wires from an old project that is not part of the current story. The article explains that the team is preparing for competition and has not had equipment problems.

Think: Photo B may look dramatic, but it could mislead readers about the current story.

Notebook Directions

Answer in three labeled parts.

Part 1: Identify which photo better supports the article.

Part 2: Explain why the other photo could mislead the audience.

Part 3: Write an accurate caption for the better photo.

Common Mistake

Incorrect example:

Photo B is better because it is more dramatic and will get more attention.

Why this is incorrect: The answer values attention over accuracy. A dramatic image can mislead if it does not represent the story truthfully.

Correct example:

Photo A is better because it accurately shows the robotics team preparing for competition, which matches the verified focus of the article.

Why this is correct: The answer judges design by factual support, audience understanding, and format-specific quality.
