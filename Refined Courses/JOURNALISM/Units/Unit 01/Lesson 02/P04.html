P04 Worked Example

Example 1: Role Assignment

Step-by-step explanation: Follow each modeled step carefully and notice how the thinking moves from the task to evidence, reasoning, and a clear response.

Scenario: A team has to publish a 300-word article about a campus blood drive by 3:00 p.m.

Step 1: Identify required tasks: gather facts, confirm source information, draft the article, edit for clarity, check conventions, and submit.

Step 2: Assign roles: reporter contacts the sponsor, writer drafts, editor checks accuracy and clarity, publication manager tracks the deadline.

Step 3: Add an ethical checkpoint: no claim about donation totals is published unless confirmed by the sponsor.

Modeled thinking: A clear workflow prevents confusion and makes accuracy someone’s responsibility.

Example 2: Active Listening During Disagreement

Step-by-step explanation: Follow each modeled step carefully and notice how the thinking moves from the task to evidence, reasoning, and a clear response.

Scenario: One teammate wants a dramatic headline. Another worries the headline exaggerates the facts.

Step 1: Restate the concern: You are concerned that the headline promises more than the article proves.

Step 2: Compare the headline to verified facts.

Step 3: Revise the headline so it attracts readers without misleading them.

Modeled thinking: Active listening slows down the argument and brings the team back to evidence.

Example 3: Ethical Compromise

Step-by-step explanation: Follow each modeled step carefully and notice how the thinking moves from the task to evidence, reasoning, and a clear response.

Scenario: The article is ready, but one quote has not been confirmed because the student source has not responded.

Step 1: Identify the risk. Publishing an unconfirmed quote may misrepresent the source.

Step 2: Choose a compromise. Remove the quote, paraphrase only verified information, or delay that detail until confirmation.

Step 3: Protect the audience. Publish only what the team can verify.

Modeled thinking: Ethical compromise changes the product without changing the truth.

Common Mistake

Incorrect example:

Collaboration means everyone should vote, and the majority choice should be published.

Why this is incorrect: A majority vote cannot make false or unverified information acceptable. Journalism teams can vote on design or order, but accuracy and fairness are not optional.

Correct example:

Collaboration means the team listens, divides work, solves problems respectfully, and publishes only information that meets ethical and factual standards.

Teachable Explanation: The answer connects teamwork to journalism quality, not just agreement.
