==================================================
CORE CONCEPT 1 — MARKET STRUCTURE
==================================================

Teach:

Market structure describes characteristics of a market that influence:

- competition;
- firm behavior;
- pricing;
- entry;
- product choice.

Primary classification variables:

1. Number of firms
2. Product similarity/differentiation
3. Barriers to entry
4. Firm control over price
5. Nature of competition

==================================================
CORE CONCEPT 2 — PURE COMPETITION
==================================================

Teach idealized characteristics:

- many buyers and sellers;
- standardized or very similar product;
- low barriers to entry/exit;
- individual firms have little market power;
- firms are price takers in the standard model.

Do not claim many real markets perfectly satisfy all assumptions.

Use as a benchmark model.

==================================================
PURE COMPETITION EXAMPLE CONTROL
==================================================

Do not state:

“Agriculture is perfectly competitive.”

Instead:

“Some agricultural commodity markets may approximate several characteristics of the pure-competition model.”

Maintain model/real-world distinction.

==================================================
CORE CONCEPT 3 — MONOPOLISTIC COMPETITION
==================================================

Teach:

- many firms;
- differentiated products;
- relatively low barriers to entry;
- some control over price;
- significant nonprice competition.

Examples of differentiation may involve:

- quality;
- brand;
- location;
- customer experience;
- features.

Do not imply the term means:

“a mixture of monopoly and conspiracy.”

==================================================
CORE CONCEPT 4 — OLIGOPOLY
==================================================

Teach:

- small number of dominant firms;
- substantial barriers to entry;
- products may be standardized or differentiated;
- firms are strategically interdependent.

INTERDEPENDENCE is essential.

Explain:

A firm's decisions may depend on anticipated reactions of rival firms.

Do NOT teach illegal collusion procedures.

If collusion is mentioned:

describe conceptually only as coordination among firms that may be restricted by law.

==================================================
CORE CONCEPT 5 — MONOPOLY
==================================================

Teach:

A monopoly exists when a single firm is the sole or overwhelmingly dominant seller in a defined market with substantial barriers to entry.

Important:

Market definition matters.

Do not label any large company automatically a monopoly.

Potential barriers:

- legal;
- technological;
- control of key resources;
- very high startup/infrastructure cost;
- network effects where appropriate.

==================================================
NATURAL MONOPOLY — INTRODUCTION ONLY
==================================================

Introduce:

A natural monopoly may arise where large fixed infrastructure costs and economies of scale make one provider potentially lower-cost than multiple duplicative providers.

Possible example:

local utility infrastructure.

Do not complete regulatory-policy analysis here.

Unit 4 will return to regulation.

CORE CONCEPT 6 — PRICE COMPETITION
==================================================

Teach:

Price competition occurs when firms compete substantially through the prices they charge.

Examples:

- discounts;
- temporary price reductions;
- lower posted prices.

Do not teach predatory-pricing doctrine here.

==================================================
CORE CONCEPT 7 — NONPRICE COMPETITION
==================================================

Teach:

Firms may compete through:

- product quality;
- features;
- branding;
- advertising;
- customer service;
- convenience;
- location;
- warranties;
- innovation.

Students must understand:

Nonprice competition does NOT mean price is irrelevant.

It means competition occurs through dimensions other than direct price reductions.

Primary competition:

Scenario B:

- menu;
- service;
- location;
- atmosphere.

Primary distinction:
nonprice competition.

==================================================
CORE CONCEPT 8 — MARGINAL REVENUE
==================================================

Teach:

Marginal Revenue

=
additional revenue generated by selling one additional unit or output increment.

Do not require advanced derivation.

==================================================
CORE CONCEPT 9 — FIRM OUTPUT DECISION
==================================================

Reconnect Unit 1 Lesson 3 and Unit 2 Lesson 4.

Simplified profit-maximizing logic:

Continue increasing output while:

MR > MC.

Optimal stopping point occurs near:

MR = MC

under the standard model.

If:

MC > MR

for the next unit,

producing that additional unit would reduce profit.

==================================================
IMPORTANT PRECISION
==================================================

Do NOT state:

“A firm always maximizes profit exactly where MR = MC”

without qualification.

Teach:

Under the standard marginal model, a profit-maximizing firm chooses the output level at which marginal revenue and marginal cost are equal or as close as possible, provided producing remains economically preferable to relevant alternatives and model assumptions apply.

At this course level:

Do not add shutdown-rule analysis unless explicitly required later.

MODEL LIMITATION
==================================================

Explain:

The simple MR/MC graph is an economic model.

Real firms also face:

- uncertainty;
- changing prices;
- capacity limits;
- imperfect information;
- strategic behavior.

==================================================
MARKET STRUCTURE AND PRICE CONTROL
==================================================

Teach general pattern:

Pure Competition:
very limited individual price control.

Monopolistic Competition:
some price discretion due to differentiation.

Oligopoly:
meaningful pricing discretion but rival reactions matter.

Monopoly:
substantial market power relative to competitive firms, subject to demand and institutional constraints.

Do NOT say:

“Monopolies can charge any price they want.”

Demand and other constraints still matter.

==================================================
COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS — LESSON 5
==================================================

MISCONCEPTION 1:

Pure competition means firms do not care about profit.

CORRECTION:

Firms still pursue economic objectives; they simply have little individual control over market price under the model.

---

MISCONCEPTION 2:

Monopolistic competition means monopoly.

CORRECTION:

It contains many competing firms with differentiated products.

---

MISCONCEPTION 3:

Oligopoly means exactly two firms.

CORRECTION:

It means a market dominated by a small number of significant firms.

---

MISCONCEPTION 4:

A monopoly can charge any price.

CORRECTION:

A monopoly still faces consumer demand and other constraints.

---

MISCONCEPTION 5:

Advertising is price competition.

CORRECTION:

Advertising is normally nonprice competition.

---

MISCONCEPTION 6:

The firm should produce as much as technologically possible.

CORRECTION:

Additional output should be compared using marginal revenue and marginal cost.

---

MISCONCEPTION 7:

If total revenue rises, every additional unit is profitable.

CORRECTION:

The relevant comparison is the added revenue versus added cost.

---

MISCONCEPTION 8:

MR = MC means the firm earns zero total profit.

CORRECTION:

MR = MC concerns the last output increment, not total profit.

==================================================

LABOR MARKET

→ supply labor

Wage
= price of labor services in simplified model.

==================================================
CORE CONCEPT 2 — LABOR DEMAND
==================================================

Teach:

Labor demand represents employers' willingness and ability to hire workers at different wage rates, other factors held constant.

Generally:

higher wage
→ lower quantity of labor demanded

under the standard model.

==================================================
DERIVED DEMAND
==================================================

Teach:

Demand for labor is often DERIVED from demand for the goods/services workers help produce.

Example:

Demand for home construction increases.

This may increase demand for:

- carpenters;
- electricians;
- other construction labor.

Do NOT claim every increase in product demand increases all categories of labor.

Technology and production methods matter.

==================================================
CORE CONCEPT 3 — LABOR SUPPLY
==================================================

Teach:

Labor supply represents workers’ willingness and ability to offer labor at different wage rates, other factors constant.

At introductory level:

higher wages may attract more labor to an occupation or market.

But labor supply can be affected by:

- skills;
- credentials;
- location;
- working conditions;
- training time;
- alternative occupations.

CORE CONCEPT 4 — INCREASE IN LABOR DEMAND
==================================================

If labor demand increases and labor supply is unchanged:

Possible causes:

- greater demand for product/service;
- increased worker productivity;
- expansion of industry.

CORE CONCEPT 5 — INCREASE IN LABOR SUPPLY
==================================================

employment quantity ↑.

Do not present this as a judgment about immigration or any political issue.

==================================================

4. Education

6. Experience
7. Specialized skills
8. Credentials/licensing

==================================================
CORE CONCEPT 8 — PRODUCTIVITY
==================================================

Workers who produce more valuable output per unit of time may command higher compensation under certain market conditions.

But:

productivity is not the only wage determinant.

Do not teach:

"Wage = productivity"

as an exact universal equality.

==================================================
CORE CONCEPT 9 — EDUCATION AND TRAINING
==================================================

Teach as INVESTMENT IN HUMAN CAPITAL.

Potential benefits:

- new skills;
- greater productivity;
- eligibility for occupations;
- improved job mobility.

Potential costs:

- tuition/training cost;
- time;
- forgone earnings;
- uncertainty.

This is an economic tradeoff.

Do NOT turn into postsecondary-finance planning.

That belongs in Personal Financial Literacy.

==================================================
CORE CONCEPT 10 — COMPENSATING WAGE DIFFERENTIAL
==================================================

Introduce conceptually:

Jobs with less desirable or riskier working conditions may, under some conditions, need to offer higher compensation to attract workers.

Important qualifications:

- not guaranteed;
- bargaining power matters;
- labor-market conditions matter;
- workers may have limited alternatives.

Do not frame dangerous work as desirable.

==================================================
CORE CONCEPT 11 — TOTAL COMPENSATION
==================================================

Teach distinction:

WAGE / SALARY

vs

TOTAL COMPENSATION.

Total compensation may include:

- wages/salary;
- employer-paid benefits;
- paid leave;
- retirement contributions;
- other employer-provided benefits.

Do not teach personal benefit selection.

==================================================
CORE CONCEPT 12 — OCCUPATIONAL EARNINGS DATA
==================================================

When using wage data:

distinguish:

- median;
- mean;
- entry-level;
- experienced;
- national;
- state/local.

Do not present one statistic as what every worker earns.

If live BLS data are used:

record source and date.

Prefer fictional datasets for permanent instructional tasks.

==================================================
CORRELATION / CAUSATION CONTROL
==================================================

Example:

Workers with more education have higher average earnings in a dataset.

Valid:

There is an association in the dataset.

Not automatically valid:

Education alone caused the entire earnings difference.

Other factors may contribute.

This reinforces economic-data literacy.

==================================================
CORE CONCEPT 13 — TECHNOLOGY AND LABOR DEMAND
==================================================

Technology may:

- substitute for some tasks;
- complement other workers;
- create new occupations;
- change skill demand.

Do NOT teach:

“Technology always destroys jobs.”

or

“Technology always creates more jobs than it removes.”

Analyze context.

==================================================
CORE CONCEPT 14 — SPECIALIZATION
==================================================

Specialized workers may have:

- higher productivity;
- scarcer skills;
- greater training requirements.

This may influence wages.

Prepare students for comparative advantage later without teaching international trade yet.

==================================================
COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS — LESSON 6
==================================================

MISCONCEPTION 1:

Workers demand labor and companies supply jobs.

CORRECTION:

In the labor-market model, workers supply labor and firms demand labor.

---

MISCONCEPTION 2:

Every worker in one occupation earns the same wage.

CORRECTION:

Earnings vary by many factors.

---

MISCONCEPTION 3:

More education guarantees higher earnings.

CORRECTION:

Education may increase human capital but outcomes depend on many market conditions.

---

MISCONCEPTION 4:

Higher productivity always causes an automatic proportional wage increase.

CORRECTION:

Productivity is important, but compensation depends on multiple economic/institutional factors.

---

MISCONCEPTION 5:

A risky job always pays more.

CORRECTION:

Compensating differentials may arise under certain market conditions but are not guaranteed.

---

MISCONCEPTION 6:

Technology always lowers labor demand.

CORRECTION:

Technology can substitute for some tasks and complement/create demand for others.

---

MISCONCEPTION 7:

Salary is total compensation.

CORRECTION:

Benefits and other employer-provided compensation may also be part of total compensation.
