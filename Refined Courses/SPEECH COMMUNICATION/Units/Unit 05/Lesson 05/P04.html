Worked Example 1

Task: Identify denotation.

Word: revise.

  1. Denotation: to improve or change a draft.
  2. Speech use: revise describes improving speech notes.
  3. Reasoning: the direct meaning fits feedback and improvement.

Worked Example 2

Step-by-step explanation: Follow each modeled step carefully and notice how the thinking moves from the task to evidence, reasoning, and a clear response.

Task: Compare connotation.

Words: stubborn and determined.

  1. Both suggest someone does not give up easily.
  2. Stubborn often sounds negative.
  3. Determined often sounds positive.

Worked Example 3

Step-by-step explanation: Follow each modeled step carefully and notice how the thinking moves from the task to evidence, reasoning, and a clear response.

Task: Revise for audience impact.

Draft: Our group was lazy.

  1. Problem: lazy sounds blaming.
  2. Revision: Our group needed a clearer work plan.
  3. Reasoning: the revision identifies the problem without attacking people.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: I should use the strongest-sounding word even if it makes the audience defensive.

Correct: I should use a precise word that supports my purpose and keeps the audience willing to listen.

Strong wording should support communication, not damage it.

Lesson focus: Connotation, denotation, and audience impact

Mapped standard focus: MLA.SPEECH.COM.07

As you study the worked examples, look for how each model makes a communication decision and explains why that decision works.

  1. Identify the audience before choosing words, tone, or examples. A strong speaker adapts to what listeners need and expect.
  2. State the purpose in one clear sentence so every communication choice supports that goal.
  3. Choose voice and tone deliberately. Ask whether the language sounds respectful, appropriate, and useful for the situation.
  4. Check that the final message fits the audience, purpose, and setting without becoming too casual, too vague, or too formal.
