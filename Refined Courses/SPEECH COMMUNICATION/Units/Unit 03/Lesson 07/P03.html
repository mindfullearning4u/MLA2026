P03 Notebook Task - Part 2

Continued Notebook Application

Now connect the parts of the persuasive plan. A strong plan does not list ideas randomly. Each part should lead to the next part. The claim creates the direction. Evidence gives support. Reasoning explains the support. The counterclaim shows fairness. The rebuttal protects the claim while staying respectful.

Deeper Communication Thinking

Before speaking, ask: What does my audience already believe? What might they question? What evidence would help them trust my reasoning? What tone will make them more willing to listen? Persuasion is not only about being right. It is about helping the audience follow your thinking.

Embedded Example

Counterclaim: Some students may think peer rehearsal takes too much time.

Rebuttal: Peer rehearsal may take a few minutes, but it can prevent larger problems during the final speech because the speaker can fix unclear points early.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: Peer rehearsal is best because people who disagree are wrong.

Correct: Peer rehearsal is useful because feedback can reveal unclear transitions, and the speaker can revise before presenting.

The incorrect version attacks people instead of proving the claim. The correct version uses evidence and reasoning that an audience can follow.

Deeper Learning Support

Lesson focus: Integrated persuasive speech planning

Mapped standard focus: MLA.SPEECH.COM.01, MLA.SPEECH.LST.04

Use this second-pass routine to check whether your thinking is specific, audience-aware, and connected to the lesson skill.

  1. Identify the claim first. A persuasive speech needs a clear position before evidence or rhetoric can be evaluated.
  2. Connect reasons and evidence directly to the claim. Evidence should prove, clarify, or justify the position.
  3. Address opposing views fairly. A strong rebuttal explains why the speaker's position remains stronger.
  4. Evaluate audience effect. Rhetorical choices should strengthen credibility, emotion, logic, or urgency without weakening fairness.
