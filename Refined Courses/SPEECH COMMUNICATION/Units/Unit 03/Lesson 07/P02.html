P02 Notebook Task - Part 1

Notebook Title

Integrated Persuasive Speech Planning

Vocabulary

  • Claim: the position the speaker wants the audience to understand or accept.
  • Evidence: facts, examples, details, or source information that support the claim.
  • Reasoning: the explanation that shows why evidence proves the claim.
  • Counterclaim: an opposing position that a fair speaker recognizes.
  • Rebuttal: a respectful answer to the counterclaim.

Step-by-Step Teaching

A persuasive speech plan begins with one clear claim. The claim tells the audience exactly what the speaker believes or recommends. Next, the speaker chooses evidence that directly supports the claim. Evidence does not work by itself; the speaker must explain the evidence with reasoning so the audience understands the connection. A strong plan also includes an opposing view. This shows the speaker understands more than one side. The rebuttal then explains why the speaker still supports the original claim.

  1. State the claim in one clear sentence.
  2. Choose evidence that directly fits the claim.
  3. Explain how the evidence proves the claim.
  4. Name one fair opposing view.
  5. Respond to that opposing view respectfully.

Model Example

Speech purpose: Convince classmates to rehearse presentations with a peer before presenting.

Claim: Students should practice with a peer because feedback helps them improve before the final presentation.

Evidence: A peer may notice unclear transitions or missing details during rehearsal.

Reasoning: This matters because the speaker can revise before the audience hears the final speech.

Step-by-Step Teaching Support

Lesson focus: Integrated persuasive speech planning

Mapped standard focus: MLA.SPEECH.COM.01, MLA.SPEECH.LST.04

Use this sequence before completing the notebook task. It shows how to approach the communication skill without guessing or skipping steps.

  1. Identify the claim first. A persuasive speech needs a clear position before evidence or rhetoric can be evaluated.
  2. Connect reasons and evidence directly to the claim. Evidence should prove, clarify, or justify the position.
  3. Address opposing views fairly. A strong rebuttal explains why the speaker's position remains stronger.
  4. Evaluate audience effect. Rhetorical choices should strengthen credibility, emotion, logic, or urgency without weakening fairness.
