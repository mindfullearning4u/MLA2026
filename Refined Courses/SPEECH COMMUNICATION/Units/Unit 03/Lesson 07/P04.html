P04 Worked Example

Worked Example 1

Step-by-step explanation: Follow each modeled step carefully and notice how the thinking moves from the task to evidence, reasoning, and a clear response.

Task: Plan a claim and evidence for a persuasive speech about school announcements.

  1. Claim: Announcements should be shorter and more focused.
  2. Evidence: Students often miss key information when announcements include too many unrelated details.
  3. Reasoning: Shorter announcements help listeners remember the most important information.

Worked Example 2

Step-by-step explanation: Follow each modeled step carefully and notice how the thinking moves from the task to evidence, reasoning, and a clear response.

Task: Add a counterclaim and rebuttal.

  1. Counterclaim: Some people may say longer announcements include more helpful information.
  2. Rebuttal: More information can be helpful, but it should be organized by priority so the audience can follow and remember it.
  3. Reasoning: The rebuttal respects the opposing view while explaining why focus still matters.

Worked Example 3

Step-by-step explanation: Follow each modeled step carefully and notice how the thinking moves from the task to evidence, reasoning, and a clear response.

Task: Connect rhetorical awareness to the plan.

  1. Audience: classmates and school staff.
  2. Tone: respectful and practical.
  3. Speaker choice: use examples from school routines instead of blaming any person.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: Long announcements are annoying, so my idea is better.

Correct: Focused announcements help listeners remember key information because the most important details are easier to identify.

The incorrect example is mostly opinion. The correct example gives a reason the audience can evaluate.

Worked Example Reasoning Support

Lesson focus: Integrated persuasive speech planning

Mapped standard focus: MLA.SPEECH.COM.01, MLA.SPEECH.LST.04

As you study the worked examples, look for how each model makes a communication decision and explains why that decision works.

  1. Identify the claim first. A persuasive speech needs a clear position before evidence or rhetoric can be evaluated.
  2. Connect reasons and evidence directly to the claim. Evidence should prove, clarify, or justify the position.
  3. Address opposing views fairly. A strong rebuttal explains why the speaker's position remains stronger.
  4. Evaluate audience effect. Rhetorical choices should strengthen credibility, emotion, logic, or urgency without weakening fairness.

Teachable Explanation: The correct example works because it applies the lesson skill deliberately and explains the reasoning instead of only naming an answer.
