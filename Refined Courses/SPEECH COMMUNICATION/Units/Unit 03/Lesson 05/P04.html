P04 Worked Example

Study each example carefully before independent work.

Example 1

Argument A: Full scripts are best because speakers can read every word.

Step 1: Claim: full scripts are best.

Step 2: Evidence: speakers can read every word.

Step 3: Weakness: it does not address natural delivery.

Example 2

Argument B: Note cards are better because they cue main points while helping speakers speak naturally.

Step 1: Claim: note cards are better.

Step 2: Evidence: note cards cue main points.

Step 3: Reasoning: cues support memory without forcing word-for-word reading.

Example 3

Comparison: Argument B is more effective because it addresses both memory and delivery. Argument A only addresses remembering words.

Result: The comparison focuses on evidence and reasoning instead of personal preference.

Common Mistake - Incorrect

Incorrect: Argument A is bad because the speaker does not know anything.

The mistake skips the reasoning needed for strong speech communication.

Common Mistake - Correct

Correct: Argument B is stronger because it gives a specific benefit and explains how feedback helps speakers revise.

The corrected version names the communication choice and explains why it works.

Worked Example Reasoning Support

Lesson focus: Comparing opposing arguments

Mapped standard focus: MLA.SPEECH.LST.04

As you study the worked examples, look for how each model makes a communication decision and explains why that decision works.

  1. Identify the claim first. A persuasive speech needs a clear position before evidence or rhetoric can be evaluated.
  2. Connect reasons and evidence directly to the claim. Evidence should prove, clarify, or justify the position.
  3. Address opposing views fairly. A strong rebuttal explains why the speaker's position remains stronger.
  4. Evaluate audience effect. Rhetorical choices should strengthen credibility, emotion, logic, or urgency without weakening fairness.
