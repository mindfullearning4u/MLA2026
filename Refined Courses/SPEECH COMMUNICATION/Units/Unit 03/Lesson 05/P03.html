Continued Notebook Application

When comparing arguments, use neutral language. You can say one argument is stronger without saying the other speaker is foolish or wrong as a person.

A strong comparison frame is: Argument ___ is stronger because its evidence ___ and its reasoning ___.

Common Mistake - Incorrect

Incorrect: Argument A is bad because the speaker does not know anything.

The incorrect version attacks the speaker instead of comparing argument quality.

Common Mistake - Correct

Correct: Argument B is stronger because it gives a specific benefit and explains how feedback helps speakers revise.

This correction uses the lesson skill clearly and respectfully.

Deeper Learning Support

Lesson focus: Comparing opposing arguments

Mapped standard focus: MLA.SPEECH.LST.04

Use this second-pass routine to check whether your thinking is specific, audience-aware, and connected to the lesson skill.

  1. Identify the claim first. A persuasive speech needs a clear position before evidence or rhetoric can be evaluated.
  2. Connect reasons and evidence directly to the claim. Evidence should prove, clarify, or justify the position.
  3. Address opposing views fairly. A strong rebuttal explains why the speaker's position remains stronger.
  4. Evaluate audience effect. Rhetorical choices should strengthen credibility, emotion, logic, or urgency without weakening fairness.
