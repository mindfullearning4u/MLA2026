P02 Notebook Task - Part 1

Notebook Title

From Source Evidence to Clear Explanation

Vocabulary

  • Evidence: information from a source that can support a point.
  • Inference: a reasonable conclusion based on evidence.
  • Source explanation: the speaker's explanation of why source information matters.
  • Claim: the main point the speaker is trying to prove or support.

Source Note

Scenario: A student team is preparing a briefing about improving attendance at school club meetings.

Source note: A survey of 60 club members showed that 42 members were more likely to attend when they received a reminder that included the agenda, meeting time, and location.

Step-by-Step Teaching

  1. First, identify the evidence exactly as the source gives it.
  2. Next, ask what the evidence suggests without exaggerating it.
  3. Then write the inference as a careful conclusion.
  4. Finally, explain how the evidence supports the communication purpose.

For this source, the evidence is not simply "reminders help." The evidence is more specific: reminders that include the agenda, time, and location were connected to stronger attendance interest.

Model Example

Claim: Clubs should send complete meeting reminders.

Evidence: 42 of 60 surveyed members were more likely to attend when reminders included an agenda, time, and location.

Inference: Members may attend more consistently when they know what will happen and where to go.

Explanation: This source supports the claim because it connects clear meeting information with increased willingness to attend.

Step-by-Step Teaching Support

Lesson focus: Evidence, inference, and source explanation

Mapped standard focus: MLA.SPEECH.RES.02

Use this sequence before completing the notebook task. It shows how to approach the communication skill without guessing or skipping steps.

  1. Identify the claim first. A persuasive speech needs a clear position before evidence or rhetoric can be evaluated.
  2. Connect reasons and evidence directly to the claim. Evidence should prove, clarify, or justify the position.
  3. Address opposing views fairly. A strong rebuttal explains why the speaker's position remains stronger.
  4. Evaluate audience effect. Rhetorical choices should strengthen credibility, emotion, logic, or urgency without weakening fairness.
