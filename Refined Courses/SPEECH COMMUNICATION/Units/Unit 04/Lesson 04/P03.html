P03 Notebook Task - Part 2

Continued Notebook Application

To use an inference in a speech, explain both the conclusion and the evidence. Do not say, "This source means preparation is good" without showing which details support that idea. The audience needs to hear your reasoning.

Deeper Communication Thinking

Inferences help speakers go beyond repeating a source. They allow the speaker to explain why a detail matters. Still, the inference must stay close to the source. If the source says room previewing reduces confusion, it is reasonable to infer that the speaker may feel more prepared. It is not reasonable to infer that every nervous speaker will become perfect.

Embedded Source

Source: "Seeing the room, checking where to stand, and noticing where the audience will sit can reduce last-minute confusion."

Supported inference: Physical preparation can make delivery smoother because the speaker has fewer surprises.

Common Mistake

Incorrect: The source proves no speaker will ever be nervous if they see the room.

Correct: The source suggests that seeing the room may reduce confusion and help the speaker feel more prepared.

The incorrect inference goes too far. The correct inference is careful and supported by the source wording.

  1. Start with the speech question or information need. Research should answer a purpose, not collect random facts.

  3. Paraphrase source information accurately so the speech sounds natural and avoids copying.
  4. Explain how the source evidence supports the speech point, central idea, or inference.
